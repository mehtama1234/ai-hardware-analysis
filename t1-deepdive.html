Serving language models fast

attention and the model's running notes
106 papers · 40 read in full · 66 from the abstract

the problemThe problem, in depth

Start with what a language model actually does when it writes for you. It produces text one word-piece at a time. To pick the next word-piece, it looks back at everything written so far — your question plus everything it has already said — and asks, for each earlier word, "how much should this one influence what I say next?" That looking-back-and-weighing step is the heart of the machine. It is the single operation that lets the model keep track of who did what to whom across a long passage. When people in this field say "attention," that weighing step is what they mean: every new word pays a certain amount of attention to every earlier word.

Here is the catch that shapes this entire theme. Every time the model reads an earlier word, it does not re-read the raw word. Instead, when that word first went by, the model computed and wrote down a little summary of it — two bundles of numbers, one that says "here is what I offer for others to match against" and one that says "here is the content I carry." The model stores these summaries so it never has to recompute them. This growing pile of stored summaries is the model's running notes: a notebook that gets one new page for every word-piece the model has ever seen in this conversation. In the jargon it is called the KV cache, but running notes is the honest name. The notebook is the reason a chatbot can hold a long thread in its head at all — and it is also the reason serving these models fast is so hard.

To feel why it is hard, you have to know the one root fact that governs all modern computers, and it is not the fact most people expect. We imagine computers are limited by how fast they can do arithmetic. They are not, anymore. A modern AI chip — a flexible number-crunching chip that can do many multiplications at once, what the field calls a GPU — can perform arithmetic breathtakingly faster than it can fetch the numbers to do the arithmetic on. The multiplying is nearly free; the moving of data from memory into the part that multiplies is the expensive, slow, bottleneck step. Picture a genius accountant who can add a thousand numbers in a blink, but the ledgers live in a warehouse across town and someone has to drive each page over one at a time. The accountant spends almost all day waiting at the loading dock. That waiting — that data movement — is the true cost, and nearly every idea in this theme is, underneath, an idea about moving less data or moving it a shorter distance.

Now put the running notes and the data-movement bottleneck together, because their collision is the whole problem. Writing the very first response to a long prompt — the field calls this the prefill — is heavy on arithmetic: the model reads thousands of words at once and can keep the multiplying units busy. But generating each subsequent word — the decode step — is the opposite. To produce just one new word-piece, the model must haul the entire notebook, every page of running notes for the whole conversation, out of memory and past the multiplying units, only to do a comparatively tiny amount of math with it. One word out, the whole notebook dragged across town. As the conversation grows, the notebook grows, and each new word gets slower to produce because there are more pages to haul. Several papers here measure exactly this: past a few thousand words of context, simply reading the notebook eats more than half of all the time spent generating (hpca-2025-092, mlsys-2025-025).

The notebook is not just slow to move; it is bulky to store, and it must live in the AI chip's own small, fast, expensive memory to be reachable at all. A large model serving a long conversation can spend hundreds of thousands of bytes of notes per word-piece (isca-2025-130). Fit too few conversations' notebooks into the chip's memory and the expensive chip sits idle serving only a handful of users; try to fit too many and you run out of room and must throw someone's notebook away mid-conversation, forcing an expensive recomputation. Deciding how to pack, grow, share, shrink, and evict these notebooks — while the multiplying units stay fed and no user waits too long — is a memory-management problem of the first order (asplos-2025-049, asplos-2025-109).

And it matters because this is now the dominant cost of running AI. When millions of people chat with a model, the electricity bill and the hardware bill are overwhelmingly spent on this one loop: haul the notebook, weigh the past, emit one word, repeat. A serving system that halves the data it moves per word roughly halves the cost of the whole enterprise. That is why an entire research field has converged on the seemingly narrow question of how to do attention and manage the running notes efficiently. It is not narrow. It is where the money, the energy, and the waiting all live.

the approachesHow the field actually tackles it

1.Keep the notebook in fast memory and never re-do work: caching and reuse

The most fundamental move is simply to keep the running notes rather than recompute them, and then to reuse them across conversations wherever two conversations overlap. If a thousand users all start with the same long instruction paragraph, the model would, naively, compute and store a fresh notebook for that identical paragraph a thousand times. That is a thousand copies of the same work and the same storage. The obvious fix is to compute the notes for the shared opening once and let everyone borrow them — this is prefix caching, sharing the pages that all conversations have in common.

Doing this cleanly is harder than it sounds because conversations branch: two chats share an opening but then diverge. The natural data structure is a tree, where the shared trunk is stored once and each conversation is a branch. Systems keep the notebook organized this way so overlapping chats reuse the trunk and only pay for their own branches (mlsys-2025-034, mlsys-2025-004). A related trick appears in bulk workloads: when a model must answer the same question template over thousands of rows of a spreadsheet, cleverly reordering the rows so consecutive rows share as long a common prefix as possible turns most of the work into cheap reuse (mlsys-2025-029). Multi-turn chat is the everyday case — rather than rebuild the whole notebook each time you send a new message, the system holds the prior turns' notes in memory between messages (asplos-2025-064). The trade-off is always memory: cached notes you might reuse are notes you cannot evict for someone else, so the interesting work is in deciding what is worth keeping (mlsys-2025-004).

2.Pack the notebook memory efficiently instead of wasting it

Even once you decide to keep the notebook, you must physically lay it out in the chip's limited memory, and how you do that determines how many users you can serve at once. The early practice reserved a fixed, large slab of memory per conversation sized for the worst case — the longest the conversation might ever get. Most conversations stay short, so most of that reserved space sat empty and unusable. The influential fix was to break the notebook into small fixed-size pages and hand them out on demand, the same way an operating system manages a computer's memory, so a short conversation uses few pages and a long one grows page by page with almost no waste.

That paging idea unlocked serving far more users per chip, but it introduced its own friction: the pages of one conversation's notebook are now scattered around memory rather than sitting in one contiguous run, which complicates the fast attention code and adds overhead. So a second wave asks how to get the flexibility of on-demand pages while keeping each notebook looking like one continuous block to the math routines — one approach leans on the chip's own address-translation hardware to present a tidy contiguous view over physically scattered memory (asplos-2025-109). A parallel line of work is smarter admission and eviction: given a batch of running conversations whose notebooks are all growing, predict how much notebook memory the batch will actually need a few steps into the future — using the observed distribution of how long past answers ran — so you can safely admit more users without the panic of running out and throwing someone's notes away (asplos-2025-049). The shared thread is that memory capacity, not arithmetic, is the scarce resource, and packing it tightly directly buys throughput.

3.Write a faster attention routine that never spills to slow memory

Independent of what is stored, there is the question of how the weighing step itself is computed on the chip, and this is where the data-movement principle bites hardest. A naive attention computation builds a giant table of how-much-each-word-attends-to-each-other-word, writes that whole table out to the chip's main memory, then reads it back to finish. For a long passage that table is enormous, and writing and re-reading it is pure wasted driving to the warehouse and back. The breakthrough style of routine, which the whole field now builds on, never materializes the full table: it processes the passage in small tiles that fit in the chip's tiny on-chip scratchpad, carrying just enough running totals to stitch the tiles together, so the big table is never moved at all.

Because that tiled routine is so central, much of the recent work is about making it handle the messy reality of real serving. One line builds a single flexible engine that handles the many notebook layouts — scattered pages, shared trees, ragged batches — through one unified representation, and compiles a specialized routine for each variant of attention on first use (mlsys-2025-000). Another gives researchers a simple way to describe a new attention variant in a few lines and have an efficient tiled routine generated automatically, instead of hand-writing chip code for every idea (mlsys-2025-015). A subtler problem is that during single-word generation the tiled routine can leave most of the chip's parallel units idle, because there is only one new word to work on; a clever re-slicing of the work — exploiting the fact that the running totals can be combined in any order — refills those idle units for long conversations (mlsys-2025-037). And on small devices like phones, where two different compute units would otherwise take turns and leave one idle, the routine is restructured so both run at once (mlsys-2025-038).

4.Move less notebook by making the numbers smaller or skipping unimportant pages

If the cost is dominated by hauling the notebook, two levers attack the notebook itself: make each page smaller, or don't haul every page. Making pages smaller means storing the notes in coarser numbers — recording each value with fewer digits of precision, the field's term is smaller numbers or quantization. A note stored in four digits instead of sixteen is a quarter the size to store and a quarter the data to move, and for many models this barely dents the quality of the output. Some methods go further and fold this shrinking directly into the fast attention routine so the notes never have to be expanded back to full size before use, which is what preserves the speed gain (mlsys-2025-025).

The other lever is skipping. In a long passage, most earlier words turn out to matter very little for the next word; the attention weights are heavily concentrated on a handful of positions. So rather than haul the whole notebook, haul only the pages likely to matter and ignore the rest — this is sparse attention. The hard part is deciding which pages matter without reading them all first. Approaches sample a small slice of the weighing to estimate which regions are important and then fetch only those (mlsys-2025-060); designate some attention paths to look only at nearby recent words plus a few anchor words, and others to look broadly, unifying both in one routine (mlsys-2025-047); or notice that a page's importance often stays roughly constant across several consecutive words and skip re-fetching stable pages (hpca-2025-092). A distinct architectural version squeezes the notebook at the source: redesign the model so the notes are stored in a compressed low-dimensional form, cutting bytes-per-word dramatically before any serving trick is applied (isca-2025-130). All of these trade a little accuracy, or a little risk of missing something, for a large cut in data moved — and a recurring cautionary finding is that these savings can evaporate or even reverse once measured inside a real serving system rather than a toy benchmark (mlsys-2025-007).

5.Split the two jobs — first-read and word-by-word — onto the right hardware

Recall that the heavy first-read of a prompt (prefill) is arithmetic-bound and keeps the multiplying units busy, while producing each subsequent word (decode) is data-movement-bound and mostly waits on the notebook. These are opposite appetites, and running them on the same chip in the same moment means each interferes with the other: a long first-read stalls everyone's word-by-word generation. The disaggregation approach physically separates them — dedicate one pool of chips to first-reads and another to word-by-word generation, sized and tuned for their different needs, and hand the notebook off between them.

The subtlety is that handing the notebook from the first-read pool to the generation pool is itself a data move that can become the new bottleneck, so systems stream the notes over incrementally as they are produced and schedule carefully to keep both pools busy without stalls (isca-2025-034, mlsys-2025-040). In a cloud with a grab-bag of chip types, this splitting becomes an assignment problem: send first-reads to the chips with the most arithmetic muscle and word-by-word work to the chips with the fastest memory, then route each request across a network of mismatched chips and links to keep everything flowing (mlsys-2025-023, asplos-2025-097). Because the two phases also draw different amounts of power and heat, some systems use the split as a knob to manage energy and temperature across a datacenter, dialing chip speed and layout to the phase being run (hpca-2025-082, asplos-2025-127). The unifying insight is that a language model is really two different workloads wearing one name, and treating them as one wastes hardware.

6.When the notebook won't fit, compute where the data already lives

For very long conversations the notebook grows past what even a large chip's fast memory can hold, and the usual fallback — park the overflow in slower, roomier memory and drag it back when needed — reintroduces the exact warehouse-driving problem in its worst form, now over a slow link between chips. The more radical response inverts the arrangement: instead of hauling the notebook to the processor, put a small processor next to the memory and do the weighing there, so only the tiny answer travels back rather than the whole bulky notebook.

This shows up at several layers of the memory hierarchy. Some designs place compute inside the roomy main memory modules and keep the rarely-needed parts of the model there, moving only results (hpca-2025-063). Others push the notebook and its weighing all the way down into the storage drive — the flash chips that normally just hold files — exploiting the fact that a drive's internal bandwidth is far higher than the thin link connecting it to the processor, so attention over a giant notebook runs inside the drive (hpca-2025-068, isca-2025-061). A related split pairs a big general chip for the arithmetic-heavy first-read with a lean, memory-fast chip purpose-built for the word-by-word phase, streaming the notebook between them (isca-2025-080). And custom silicon can bake the notebook-skipping and number-shrinking tricks directly into hardware for large efficiency wins (hpca-2025-092, micro-2025-096). The common theme is the deepest expression of the root principle: if moving the data is the cost, then move the computation to the data instead.

where it standsWhat's solved, what's open

The field has largely solved the crude waste. Keeping and packing the running notes on demand rather than recomputing or over-reserving them, reusing shared openings across conversations, and computing attention with tiled routines that never spill the big table to slow memory are now standard practice and jointly deliver order-of-magnitude gains — these ideas ship in the serving systems running today. Splitting the arithmetic-heavy first-read from the memory-bound word-by-word generation is well understood and increasingly deployed. What remains open is mostly about the long tail and the honest accounting. Shrinking notes to smaller numbers and skipping unimportant pages give large speedups but trade against accuracy in ways that are model-, task-, and even sample-specific, and several papers here show those wins can shrink or vanish once measured inside a full serving stack rather than a clean benchmark (mlsys-2025-007). Truly enormous contexts — hundreds of thousands to a million words — still strain memory capacity and push people toward exotic answers like computing inside memory or storage chips, which work but are not yet commodity hardware. And the newest architectures that change how the notebook is stored at the source (compressed or recurrent-state designs) break the tidy caching and reuse tricks that the rest of the field depends on, so the tooling has to be reinvented for them (mlsys-2025-004). The root problem — that moving data, not doing arithmetic, is the true cost — is not going away; the field is steadily discovering how far it can be pushed.

Past-Future Scheduler for LLM Serving under SLA Guarantees

What it does

When many users send requests to a large language model (AI text generator) at once, the server must juggle them in GPU memory. Existing schedulers either guess too pessimistically (holding memory slots idle) or too optimistically (cramming in too many requests until memory runs out and some must be aborted and restarted). This paper introduces the Past-Future Scheduler, which predicts how much memory each running request will actually need in the future and admits new requests only when that future peak stays safely below capacity.

The method, in detail

The idea. Every time a language model generates text, it builds up a growing memory structure called the key-value cache — a running record of everything it has processed so far — one slot per generated word. The cache for a request keeps growing until the model finishes. If many requests run together (continuous batching — swapping finished requests out and new ones in at each generation step), you need to know how much memory the whole batch will need in the future, not just right now.

How it works. The scheduler has two components.

1. Output-length prediction. Instead of guessing each request will run to the maximum possible length, the scheduler samples a predicted length from the distribution of the last 1,000 recently completed requests. For a request already partly done, it samples only from the conditional distribution of lengths longer than what has already been generated. This updates every generation step, so predictions improve as requests progress.

2. Future memory estimation. Given those predicted lengths, the scheduler ranks currently running requests by how many steps each has left. It then computes memory usage at each moment a request will finish — because when the first request finishes it releases memory, making room for others. The peak future memory needed is the maximum across all those future completion moments: max(M at step t+1, M at step t+2, ...). A new queued request is admitted only if adding it keeps that peak below GPU capacity.

The key design choice. Looking forward through the completion timeline — rather than just summing all current requests or ignoring output lengths — lets the scheduler time admissions precisely: a request may be held for a few extra generation steps while a running request finishes and frees memory, then admitted cleanly with no evictions.

The result. Compared to aggressive schedulers (vLLM) on heavy workloads, harmful request evictions drop from over 90% to near zero, and measured throughput under strict response-time guarantees (goodput) is 2–3 times higher, with less than 1% scheduling overhead.

Coach: Exploiting Temporal Patterns for All-Resource Oversubscription in Cloud Platforms

What it does

Cloud data centers waste enormous amounts of CPU, memory, and storage because each rented virtual machine (VM) is given its full requested amount even when it only uses a fraction. Coach is a system built at Microsoft Azure that studies the time-of-day usage rhythms of over one million VMs and uses those patterns to safely oversell all resources at once, fitting up to 26% more VMs onto the same hardware with minimal slowdown for tenants.

The method, in detail

The idea. Most VMs use only 20-50% of what they reserved, and many have predictable daily rhythms — one VM peaks at noon while a neighbor peaks at night. If you pair those VMs on the same physical server, their peaks rarely collide, so the server's physical resources can be shared.

How it works. Coach introduces a new VM type called CoachVM. Each CoachVM gets two slices of every resource: a guaranteed slice (always reserved, fully backed by real hardware) and an oversubscribed slice (drawn from a shared pool when needed). A machine-learning model — a random forest (a collection of decision trees that vote on an answer) — looks at the VM's size, its customer's history, and the time of day, and predicts how much of the oversubscribed slice will actually be needed in each 4-hour window throughout the day. The cluster scheduler then matches VMs with complementary patterns onto the same server.

The key design choice: memory. CPU is easy to share because it can be instantly reassigned between VMs. Memory is much harder — a VM's memory pages are locked to specific physical addresses and cannot be quickly swapped. Coach handles this by splitting memory into a physically-backed guaranteed portion (always fast) and a virtually-addressed oversubscribed portion that is mapped to a slower backing store and deprioritized using the guest operating system's own NUMA (memory topology) settings, so the guest naturally avoids it without any code changes.

Contention safety. A local agent on each server watches memory pressure every 20 seconds. If trouble is brewing, it first trims cold (unused) pages to free physical memory; if that is not enough, it pulls from unallocated server memory; as a last resort it live-migrates a VM away.

The result. Coach lets servers host 26% more VMs compared to no oversubscription, with under 10% slowdown even for sensitive latency-critical workloads, and less than 1% memory contention incidents.

CoServe: Efficient Collaboration-of-Experts (CoE) Model Inference with Limited Memory

What it does

When a factory edge device (like a laptop-grade GPU with 12 GB of memory) needs to run hundreds of specialized AI inspection models that together require far more memory than the device has, the system must constantly swap models in from disk — and that swapping can eat over 90% of all processing time. CoServe solves this by exploiting a feature unique to this kind of multi-model pipeline: the order in which models will be needed can be predicted ahead of time, not just guessed from history. It uses that prediction to reorder requests, evict the right models, and tune memory split, achieving 4.5–12× more throughput than prior systems.

The method, in detail

The idea. A 'Collaboration of Experts' (CoE) system routes each incoming task through a chain of specialized AI models. Because the routing rules are fixed (or can be pre-computed from real data), CoServe knows in advance which model each request will need — unlike competing 'Mixture of Experts' architectures where the route is only decided at runtime.

How it works. CoServe runs in three phases. Before the system goes live, an offline profiler runs small benchmark jobs on the actual device to measure how fast each model runs at different batch sizes, how much memory each model needs, and how often each model is used across real workloads. This builds a performance table and a usage-probability score for every model.

At startup, CoServe uses that usage-probability score to load the most popular models into GPU memory first, filling it up in descending order of likelihood.

During live operation, two mechanisms reduce unnecessary model swaps. First, the request scheduler groups requests that need the same model together in its execution queue — instead of serving them first-come-first-served, it sorts them so a model already loaded stays loaded for a cluster of back-to-back requests. When multiple parallel execution lanes exist (some on the GPU, some on the CPU), incoming requests are assigned to the lane that minimizes total wait time, not just per-request wait time. Second, when a model must be evicted to free memory for a new one, a two-stage rule applies: first evict 'orphaned' subsequent models (those that cannot run yet because their prerequisite model is not loaded), sorted by size to free the most memory with the fewest evictions; only then evict based on ascending usage probability.

The key design choice. Memory is split between keeping models loaded and handling batch inference. CoServe uses a sliding-window search over a probability curve of model usage to find the optimal split automatically per device.

The result. On real circuit-board inspection workloads (300+ models, 60 GB total, on a 12 GB GPU), CoServe delivers 4.5× to 12× higher throughput compared to Samba-CoE, the previous state of the art, while reducing the number of model swaps by up to 94%.

DynamoLLM: Designing LLM Inference Clusters for Performance and Energy Efficiency

What it does

Running large AI language models in the cloud requires racks of expensive, power-hungry GPUs that today are always kept running at maximum speed — even at 2 AM when traffic is a fraction of the midday peak. DynamoLLM is an energy-management system that continuously and automatically reconfigures a GPU cluster (how many servers are on, how many GPUs share each model copy, and how fast each GPU runs its clock) to match the actual load, cutting energy use by 53% and carbon emissions by 38% while still meeting response-time guarantees.

The method, in detail

The insight. Not all user requests are alike: a short coding snippet and a long conversational essay need very different amounts of GPU muscle. Short requests finish fine on fewer GPUs running at a lower clock speed; long, token-heavy requests need more GPUs at full speed. Today's systems ignore this and provision for the worst case all the time.

How it works. DynamoLLM sorts incoming requests into nine buckets based on predicted input and output length (short/medium/long for each dimension, producing nine combinations: SS, SM, ..., LL). A lightweight BERT-based classifier predicts whether the response will be short, medium, or long before inference starts. Each bucket gets its own pool of AI server instances, configured with the GPU count and clock speed optimal for that request type. A three-tier hierarchy of controllers manages the cluster: the Cluster Manager (running every 30 minutes) decides how many servers each pool needs based on predicted load. The Pool Manager (every 5 minutes) decides how many GPUs each server in that pool should share the model across — called tensor parallelism (TP), where TP2/TP4/TP8 means splitting the model across 2, 4, or 8 GPUs. The Instance Manager (every 5 seconds) fine-tunes the GPU clock frequency.

The key design choice — minimizing reconfiguration cost. Switching configurations normally takes minutes (reloading model weights, reinitializing distributed software). DynamoLLM makes this fast via three tricks: pre-warming server snapshots in the background before they are needed; using a bipartite graph matching algorithm to figure out the minimum weight data that must move between GPUs when changing from, say, TP4 to TP8 (in that case, the four existing GPUs already have half the needed data, and only need to ship half their weights to four new GPUs in parallel via the direct GPU-to-GPU NVLink interconnect, taking about 50ms); and keeping the GPU frequency control program pre-loaded in memory so frequency changes take milliseconds instead of seconds.

The result. On week-long production traces from Azure's Conversation and Coding services, DynamoLLM cuts energy by 47–56% compared to a fixed single-pool baseline, reduces carbon emissions by 38%, and cuts customer costs by 61%, while actually improving tail response latency for most request types.

Dadu-Corki: Algorithm-Architecture Co-Design for Embodied AI-powered Robotic Manipulation

What it does

Running a robot arm with an AI brain (a large language model, or LLM — a model that understands language and plans actions) is too slow: the system has to predict one move, execute it, wait, then predict the next, creating a loop that takes up to 249 ms per frame and prevents smooth, real-time motion. Corki breaks this bottleneck by teaching the LLM to predict a whole short trajectory (a smooth mathematical curve covering several future moves) at once, then using a custom hardware chip on the robot to convert that trajectory into motor commands at high speed, while simultaneously streaming the next camera image back to the server in the background.

The method, in detail

The idea. Most robot-AI systems today work frame by frame: snap a photo, send it to a server, wait for the LLM to predict the next single joint movement, execute it, repeat. This forces all three stages — LLM thinking, robot motion, data transmission — to happen one after another every 33 ms. LLM thinking alone eats 72% of the time and 96% of the energy.

How it works. Corki changes what the LLM outputs. Instead of a single next-step movement tuple, the LLM now outputs four coefficients of a cubic polynomial (a smooth S-shaped mathematical curve) for each of the robot's six degrees of motion. From those eight numbers, the robot's on-board controller can sample the exact desired position, velocity, and acceleration at any moment without asking the server again. This lets the LLM run up to 5.1× less often.

The hardware piece. Even with fewer LLM calls, converting a trajectory curve into real motor torque signals 100 times per second (the rate needed for smooth motion) is too slow on a standard processor. The authors build a small custom chip (implemented on a field-programmable gate array, an FPGA — a chip whose circuits can be reconfigured after manufacture). Inside it, five computation stages — pose, velocity, acceleration, mass matrix, and torque — are chained like an assembly line using small FIFO queues (first-in-first-out buffers). Different robot links are processed in parallel, cutting latency by 86% versus naive sequential computation. A clever approximation detects when a joint has barely moved and skips recomputing its heavy matrices (the robot's inertia bookkeeping), eliminating over 51% of matrix recalculations with no measurable accuracy loss.

The catch. Trajectories that are too long miss environmental changes, so Corki watches the curvature of the planned path and cuts the trajectory short whenever a sharp turn or gripper state change is detected, then immediately requests a new LLM prediction.

The result. End-to-end frame latency drops by up to 5.9× and task success rate improves by up to 13.9% compared to the RoboFlamingo baseline, while the FPGA accelerator uses only 13.6% of its available logic resources.

Insights into DeepSeek-V3: Scaling Challenges and Reflections on Hardware for AI Architectures

What it does

DeepSeek-V3 is a 671-billion-parameter AI model trained on only 2,048 GPUs — far fewer than comparable models — by co-designing every part of the system (model architecture, number format, parallelism strategy, network topology) around the specific strengths and weaknesses of available hardware. This paper from the DeepSeek-AI team is a candid engineering postmortem: here is what was bottlenecked, here is how we worked around it, and here is what hardware vendors should build next. The central lesson is that hardware constraints are not obstacles to route around but design parameters to design with.

The method, in detail

Memory: shrinking the model's running notes. When generating text, a language model must remember every previous word's key and value vectors (its 'running notes' or KV cache — the stored summaries of past context used for attention). For LLaMA-3.1-405B these notes consume 516 KB per token; at long conversations this overwhelms GPU memory. DeepSeek-V3 uses Multi-head Latent Attention (MLA): instead of storing full key-value pairs for every attention head, a single compressed 'latent vector' is cached (70 KB per token, a 7x reduction), and the full vectors are reconstructed on the fly by a learned projection. This makes the model's memory cost manageable without losing quality.

Compute: a model of specialists. Rather than a single dense network where every parameter is used for every input word, DeepSeek-V3 uses a Mixture of Experts (MoE) design — 256 specialist sub-networks (experts), of which only 9 are activated per token. The full model has 671B parameters but each forward pass touches only ~37B, achieving compute costs comparable to a dense 37B model while matching the quality of a much larger dense model.

Number format: using lower precision carefully. Standard AI training uses 16-bit numbers (BF16). DeepSeek-V3 trains largely in FP8 (8-bit floating point), halving memory and doubling arithmetic throughput. The catch is that NVIDIA's Hopper GPU accumulates intermediate products in a reduced-precision format (only 13 mantissa bits — called FP22 in the paper), which is not quite accurate enough for reliable large-scale training. The team works around this with fine-grained scaling: activations are quantized in 1x128 tiles and weights in 128x128 blocks, with per-tile scale factors ensuring precision is preserved where it matters.

Parallelism: routing experts to stay close. Expert parallelism sends each token over the network to whichever GPU holds the chosen expert. The H800 cluster's GPU-to-GPU interconnect within a single node (NVLink, running at 400 GB/s) is 4x faster than the inter-node network (InfiniBand, effectively 40 GB/s per GPU). Node-Limited Routing constrains each token to visit experts on at most 4 of 8 nodes, so most traffic stays on the fast intra-node link, cutting inter-node bandwidth demand substantially.

Network topology: a cheaper fat tree. Large clusters typically use three-tier switching networks, which are expensive. DeepSeek used a Multi-Plane Fat-Tree (MPFT): 8 independent two-tier switching planes, one per NIC per node, supporting up to 16,384 GPUs at the same cost-per-endpoint as a two-tier network for 2,048 GPUs. Traffic is isolated per plane; the NCCL communication library's PXN mechanism handles cross-plane forwarding via NVLink.

The result. The method, in detail

The problem in depth. In a pure attention-only model, the model's record of past tokens grows as individual token-sized blocks. If two users share a 1,000-token system prompt, you cache those blocks once and reuse them for both. You can also cache a prefix of any length by simply keeping the first N blocks. Recurrent states work differently: the state at token 1,000 was computed by overwriting the state at token 999, which overwrote the state at token 998, and so on — there is no way to recover the state at token 500 from the state at token 1,000. To support prefix matching you would have to save checkpoints at every possible split point, but each checkpoint is 10-100 times larger than a single token's attention record (because it compresses the entire history into a dense matrix). Saving a checkpoint every 32 tokens for a 10,000-token sequence on a 7B hybrid model consumes 17 GB of GPU memory — more than many GPUs have — and most of those checkpoints will never be reused.

Admission policy: only cache what will likely be reused. Marconi classifies reuse into two scenarios. First, 'purely input' prefixes: the same system prompt or few-shot examples appear at the start of many requests. Marconi runs a speculative insertion — it traces where the new request's input tokens would land in a prefix tree (a data structure that records past sequences as shared branches) before the request even starts processing. If inserting the new sequence creates a new branch point in the tree, that branch point is a shared prefix worth saving, and Marconi checkpoints the recurrent state at that exact position during processing. Second, 'input-plus-output' prefixes: multi-turn conversations where each new turn appends to previous turns. Here Marconi simply saves the recurrent state at the last decoded token after every turn, since the next turn will resume from exactly that point. All other possible checkpoint positions are rejected at admission time.

Eviction policy: favor states that save the most computation per byte of memory. Standard caches evict the least-recently-used entry. Marconi instead scores each cached entry by: recency score plus alpha times FLOP-efficiency, where FLOP-efficiency is the number of floating-point operations saved by a cache hit divided by the entry's size in bytes. For attention records, a longer sequence both saves more computation and takes more memory — these cancel out, giving a roughly constant efficiency. For recurrent states, a longer sequence saves far more computation but the state is always the same size — so FLOP-efficiency grows steeply with sequence length. This means Marconi deliberately keeps states for long sequences at the expense of states for short ones, a tradeoff that is worthwhile because hybrid models process short sequences so quickly that a cache miss barely matters. The alpha weight is tuned automatically by replaying a bootstrap window of recent requests.

What it does

The method, in detail

The idea. Meanwhile, compressed KV caches (shrinking stored values to 4 or 2 bits) still have to decompress back to 16-bit before doing the attention math, largely erasing the benefit.

FlashQ — quantized attention math. After the computation, key and value tiles are further compressed to 4-bit or 2-bit for storage in the KV cache using channel-wise asymmetric quantization (a finer-grained scheme that sets a separate scale and offset per feature dimension to handle outlier values). A head-wise mixed precision scheme assigns 2-bit compression to the least sensitive attention heads (ranked by a gap×standard-deviation priority score) and 4-bit to the rest, balancing memory savings against accuracy.

Attention scores below a threshold are set to exactly zero (the 'sparse' part), keeping the lookup table small and reducing computation.

The key design choice. By running all matrix multiplies in 8-bit integer and softmax in 16-bit on tensor cores, the entire attention computation stays on the fast path. No step needs to leave the tensor cores for CUDA cores.

The idea. If a different action takes the top spot, the process repeats until the winner stabilizes, which often takes just 1–3 recomputations even when hundreds of actions exist.

Running a very large language model on a server with limited GPU memory requires storing most of the model's weights in regular CPU memory and shuttling them over to the GPU as needed — but that data transfer bus (PCIe) is the bottleneck, eating up 87–98% of total runtime. FlexInfer solves this by recognizing that AI generation has two very different phases and picking the best hardware strategy for each phase separately, cutting total latency by 75–76% compared to prior state-of-the-art offloading systems.

The method, in detail

The idea. Large language model inference has two distinct stages. The prefill phase processes the whole input prompt at once — this is mathematically intensive (lots of parallel multiply-accumulate work) and GPUs dominate. The decode phase generates one output token at a time using a running scratchpad called the KV cache (the model's memory of what it has already processed) — this is memory-bandwidth-bound and light on computation, so the overhead of shuttling weights over the PCIe bus hurts most here. Existing systems like FlexGen use one fixed strategy for both phases and are consequently slow.

How it works. FlexInfer offers three execution modes: CPU-only (run everything on the CPU using its large main memory), GPU-offload (transfer weights layer by layer from CPU to GPU via the PCIe bus), and SplitGen (assign the first N-M layers to the CPU and keep the last M layers permanently in GPU memory). At startup, FlexInfer runs an analytical estimator — a set of equations that take in the model's size, the hardware's compute throughput, memory bandwidth, and PCIe speed — and predicts the time each mode would take for both the prefill and decode phases separately. It then picks the best mode for each phase independently.

The key design choice. The estimator accounts for real efficiency gaps between theoretical and actual hardware performance using offline calibration, so its predictions are accurate without expensive profiling at runtime. Because it treats prefill and decode as separate decisions, it can pick GPU-offload for prefill (where GPU compute wins) and SplitGen for decode (where keeping the last few layers resident in GPU memory eliminates PCIe transfers almost entirely).

FastTree: Optimizing Attention Computation for Tree-Structured LLM Inference

What it does

When many AI queries share a long common prefix — for example, all using the same system prompt — modern serving systems already store that prefix once in a tree structure to save memory. But they still run the attention calculation separately for each query, which is slow and wastes the GPU's fastest math units. FastTree reorganizes those separate calculations into batches that process shared prefixes together, unlocking the GPU's high-throughput hardware and dramatically cutting attention latency.

The method, in detail

The idea. During text generation, each token a model produces must 'attend' to all previous tokens. The model stores past token representations (called keys and values) in a structure called the key-value cache. When many queries share the same history — say, the same long system prompt followed by different user questions — that shared portion of the cache can be stored once in a radix tree (a branching data structure where each branch holds one shared segment). Existing systems save memory this way but still run attention for each query independently, loading the shared keys and values separately from slow global GPU memory for every single query.

How it works. FastTree first decides, at runtime, how to group queries around their shared context. It models this as a binary assignment problem: for each edge in the tree, choose whether to concatenate the parent's context down into the child (so queries at the leaf inherit the full merged prefix) or keep them split. A greedy algorithm traverses the tree in breadth-first order and picks whichever choice — merge or split — costs less, estimated by how much wasted padding and extra memory traffic each choice creates.

Once groups are chosen, a custom attention computation runs for each group together. This is inspired by FlashAttention (a well-known technique that keeps intermediate results in the GPU's fast on-chip memory instead of writing them to the slow large pool). All queries in a group share the same keys and values in fast memory, loaded only once. Because multiple queries are combined, the computation changes from a matrix-times-vector operation (which the GPU handles poorly) into a matrix-times-matrix operation that the GPU's dedicated math units — called tensor cores — execute many times faster.

The key design choice. A long context node can create a 'tail effect' where one thread block takes far longer than others, leaving most of the GPU idle. FastTree detects this and splits long contexts into segments that can run in parallel, recovering full GPU utilization.

The result. FastTree achieves up to 5.1× speedup over FlashAttention for the attention kernel and up to 2.2× higher end-to-end serving throughput over the state-of-the-art SGLang system on real-world tree-structured benchmarks.

What it does

When a language model generates text one token at a time (the 'decode' phase), the attention step — where the model compares the new token against every previous token in the conversation — is starved of GPU parallelism. The method, in detail

The idea. A GPU runs many parallel 'worker groups' (called Streaming Multiprocessors, or SMs). During token generation, attention needs to compare the new token against thousands of cached previous tokens. How it works. LeanAttention uses a technique called Stream-K decomposition, borrowed from fast matrix multiplication. The key design choice. LeanAttention proves this formally and uses it to reduce partial outputs from differently-sized chunks without any approximation, producing bit-exact results.

The catch. There is a small synchronization cost when one SM must wait for another to finish a partial result before it can merge, but the paper shows this overhead is under 3% in almost all practical cases.

MAS-Attention: Memory-Aware Stream Processing for Attention Acceleration on Resource-Constrained Edge Devices

What it does

Smartphones and other edge devices run AI attention computations one step at a time — first the big matrix multiply, then the normalization step called softmax — even though the chip has two separate kinds of compute units that could run those steps at the same time. MAS-Attention schedules these two streams of work in parallel across the two unit types, cutting attention latency by up to 2.75× without changing the model's answers.

The method, in detail

The idea. Attention — the mechanism at the heart of transformer models — requires two main operations: (1) a large matrix multiply (multiplying queries by keys to produce attention scores) and (2) softmax, which normalizes each row of those scores into probabilities. Softmax then feeds a second matrix multiply (scores times values). On a datacenter GPU with hundreds of cores, these can be parallelized aggressively. On a smartphone accelerator (NPU — neural processing unit), there are only a handful of compute units: a MAC unit that handles matrix multiplies, and a VEC unit that handles element-wise operations like softmax. Existing methods run MAC and VEC sequentially, leaving one idle at all times.

How it works. MAS-Attention treats the two matrix multiplies and the softmax as three overlapping streams. It divides the query matrix into row-chunks and pipelines them. In any given round: the VEC unit is computing softmax on the scores from the previous chunk, while the MAC unit is simultaneously computing either the next chunk of scores or the weighted sum with the values matrix. This overlapping is 'semi-synchronous' — the two units run independently within a round but must sync at round boundaries to pass data.

Tiling to fit on-chip memory. Edge accelerators have tiny on-chip memory (around 5 MB in the simulated device). The query and key matrices are divided into fine-grained sub-tiles for matrix multiplies, while score and probability matrices are tiled at the row level (one full row at a time, required by softmax). The best tile sizes are found offline using a combination of Monte Carlo Tree Search (an algorithm that explores the space like a game tree) and Genetic Algorithms, which refine the ordering.

The catch. Sometimes the softmax computation cannot finish because on-chip memory runs out. When that happens, MAS-Attention proactively overwrites the less-critical key or value tiles in memory (they can be reloaded from DRAM later), freeing space so the softmax result — which cannot be reloaded — can complete.

The result. On a simulated edge accelerator, MAS-Attention achieves up to 2.75× speedup and 54% energy reduction versus the leading edge-focused method (FLAT). On a real Huawei smartphone NPU, the speedup reaches 1.76× for attention.

What it does

The method, in detail

The idea. Generating text with a large language model has two distinct phases. In the prefill phase the system reads your entire prompt at once — hundreds or thousands of tokens — which keeps the GPUs busy with math; the bottleneck is compute and inter-GPU communication. In the decode phase the model generates one word at a time; each step moves very little data but has to reload the entire model's weights from GPU memory repeatedly, so weight-loading dominates. These two phases benefit from opposite parallelism strategies: pipeline parallelism (different GPUs handle different layers sequentially, passing only a thin activation vector between them) has low communication overhead and works great for prefill; tensor parallelism (every GPU holds a slice of every layer and they collaborate on each computation, but must exchange partial results at every layer via a broadcast called all-reduce) shards the weights so each GPU loads less, making it better for decode. No single static strategy wins both phases.

How it works. Seesaw keeps all GPUs but switches how model weights are split between the two phases. After completing prefill for a batch, it re-loads each GPU's required weight slices from CPU RAM to rearrange the sharding — an operation called re-sharding. The intermediate attention state (the running notes the model keeps, called the key-value cache) is moved through CPU shared memory during the switch, automatically reformatted to match the new layout.

The key design choice — tiered buffering and transition-minimizing scheduling. Re-sharding is expensive, so Seesaw avoids doing it too often. It first accumulates many prefill results in CPU memory (tiered key-value cache buffering), transitioning to decode only when CPU memory is full. During decode it processes the entire accumulated batch before switching back. This minimizes how many times the re-sharding overhead is paid while keeping the decode batch as large as possible for high throughput. KV cache transfers between CPU and GPU run in background threads, overlapping with computation.

The result. On PCIe-connected GPU clusters (where all-reduce communication is slow), Seesaw achieves a geometric mean speedup of 1.36x and up to 1.78x over vLLM, with larger gains on GPUs with slower interconnects where the all-reduce penalty in tensor parallelism is most severe.

What it does

The method, in detail

The idea. In a multi-agent simulation, an agent's action in step 5 can only affect another agent if that other agent could physically reach it by step 5. Two agents on opposite ends of the map simply cannot interact, so there is no reason for one to wait on the other. Traditional frameworks (borrowed from reinforcement learning) ignore this and impose a global barrier at every step — everyone stops until the slowest agent finishes its AI call.

How it works. AI Metropolis builds a spatiotemporal dependency graph: each node is an agent at a specific time-step, and edges mean 'A must wait for B.' The rule is precise — agent A at step 5 is only blocked by agent B at step 4 if their map distance is small enough that B could have written to a region A is about to read. Agents that must act together (because they're close and could interact) form a 'cluster' and are dispatched as a unit to a worker process. Workers run clusters independently in parallel, each making its own AI calls and then committing results to a shared in-memory database. A central controller watches completions, recomputes dependencies, and immediately releases any cluster that is no longer blocked — without waiting for everyone else.

The key design choice. Priority scheduling: the controller's dispatch queue ranks clusters by their current time-step, so agents that are behind get their AI calls answered first. This prevents a lagging agent from blocking many others and keeps the critical path moving.

The catch. The blocking rules are conservative — an agent may be held back even if the threatening write never actually happens. This leaves a small gap versus the theoretical optimum.

The method, in detail

The problem. When a language model generates each new word, it runs an 'attention' operation — essentially asking: which earlier words matter right now? For very long inputs, this becomes the dominant bottleneck, consuming over 75% of runtime at 128,000-token contexts. LServe attacks this with two complementary forms of skipping, unified into a single framework.

Static sparsity — heads that never look far. The attention computation is split across dozens of parallel 'heads,' each looking at the history in a slightly different way. LServe identifies (once, offline, using a published technique called DuoAttention) that roughly half the heads only ever care about a few recent words and a small set of very early 'anchor' words. These streaming heads are given a fixed, cheap pattern — they always skip the vast middle of the history. Dynamic sparsity — picking which pages matter right now. For the remaining 'dense' heads that do need to look far back, LServe avoids loading all stored past key-value pairs (the model's running memory of prior words). Instead, the history is divided into fixed physical memory pages (large, for hardware efficiency) but scored using finer logical sub-pages (small, for accuracy). Each sub-page's importance is estimated cheaply by comparing the current query against precomputed per-page min/max key summaries — a dot-product trick. Only the top-ranked physical pages are loaded. Because consecutive generated words tend to attend to the same pages (temporal locality of natural language), the page-selection result is reused across several generation steps without recomputation, reducing selector overhead by 4x.

The catch. Larger physical pages are better for memory bandwidth but hurt page-selection accuracy; the hierarchical two-level scheme decouples these concerns, letting the hardware use big pages while scoring at fine granularity.

The method, in detail

The accuracy guide. If CRA is 0.95, you kept 95% of the attention mass. Experiments show CRA correlates tightly with model accuracy, so setting a CRA threshold lets you trade off speed for quality in a principled way.

Stage 1 — chunked sampling. Instead of computing attention for all query words, the query sequence is divided into equal chunks. Only a small block of queries at the tail of each chunk is fully computed. These sample scores are then averaged (reduced) at a block granularity of 128 tokens in two directions: column (global tokens that many queries attend to) and slash (diagonal bands representing nearby context). This gives a cheap but accurate estimate of which blocks of key-value pairs matter.

Stage 2 — block filtering. Using the sampled scores, SampleAttention independently selects the fewest column-strip blocks and slash-strip blocks needed to meet the CRA threshold in each direction. It does this with a cumulative-sum top-k: sort the block scores, accumulate until you reach the threshold, and take those blocks. The selected indices are extended to cover the full sequence, merged into a single sparse mask, and used to run a sparse version of FlashAttention2 (a standard fast attention algorithm).

The key design choice. Decomposing the joint column+slash selection into two independent problems reduces the search from N² combinations to N+N, making the selection fast enough to run at inference time without pre-profiling per attention head.

Smart cache-replacement rules carve out a protected slice of the cache for the primary VM's data, so shifting to the borrowing VM does not evict important data. The hardware also monitors the primary VM's request queue and yanks the borrowed core back the instant the primary VM needs it, before any slowdown is felt.

GPUs All Grown-Up: Fully Device-Driven SpMV Using GPU Work Graphs

What it does

Multiplying a sparse matrix (a grid of numbers where most entries are zero) by a vector is a fundamental operation in scientific simulation and graph computing, but it is notoriously hard to accelerate on GPUs because different rows of the matrix have wildly different numbers of non-zero entries and benefit from different algorithms. Today, the CPU decides which algorithm to use for each row before handing work to the GPU — an expensive round-trip that adds latency and hurts cache reuse. This paper uses 'GPU Work Graphs,' a new programming model borrowed from graphics rendering, to let the GPU schedule its own work on-the-fly as it processes rows: preprocessing and computation are interleaved on the device, each row's algorithm is selected and dispatched in hardware without involving the CPU, and the working data stays hot in the GPU's caches. The result is a fully device-driven pipeline that eliminates CPU-GPU synchronization entirely.

SpecEE: Accelerating Large Language Model Inference with Speculative Early Exiting

What it does

Large language models generate text one word (token) at a time, and a technique called 'early exiting' tries to stop processing partway through the network when the model is already confident about its answer — saving computation. The bottleneck is a small gatekeeper program that must scan a vocabulary of roughly 30,000 possible next tokens at every layer of the network to decide whether to exit early, and this scanning alone eats up to 20% of total inference time. SpecEE fixes this by using a tiny 'draft' model (a much smaller language model) to pre-screen the vocabulary and hand the gatekeeper a short list of plausible candidates instead of all 30,000, so the gatekeeper's job becomes far cheaper. The system also profiles how often each layer of the main network actually triggers an early exit and deploys the gatekeeper unevenly — spending more effort on the layers where it matters most. A special tree-aware mapping lets SpecEE work alongside 'speculative decoding' (a separate trick where multiple candidate continuations are processed simultaneously as a tree), without the combination exploding in complexity.

TransFusion: End-to-End Transformer Acceleration via Graph Fusion and Pipelining

What it does

Transformer models (the architecture behind ChatGPT and similar AI) consist of many repeated layers, each combining large matrix multiplications (multiplying two giant grids of numbers together), a normalization step, and an attention mechanism (which lets each word look at every other word). Previous chip designs tried to speed up individual pieces of these layers in isolation, but as models grew deeper and processed longer text, the cost of moving data between layers — shuffling numbers in and out of memory between each step — became the dominant bottleneck. TransFusion treats the entire stack of layers as one unified computation graph and pipelines it: while one layer's results are being computed, the next layer's inputs are already being loaded, and the memory layout of intermediate results is arranged to maximize reuse without spilling to slower memory. An algorithm called TileSeek (using a tree-search strategy similar to how game-playing AI explores moves) finds the best way to break computations into chunks that fit on-chip memory.
