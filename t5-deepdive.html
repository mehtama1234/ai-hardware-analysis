Skipping the work that doesn't matter

most of the numbers barely count
121 papers · 15 read in full · 106 from the abstract

the problemThe problem, in depth

Start with a picture of what a computer chip actually does when it runs one of today's big AI systems. Deep down, almost all of the work is one boring thing repeated a mind-boggling number of times: multiply two numbers, add the result to a running total, repeat. A model answering a question, drawing a picture, or recognizing a face is really doing billions of these tiny multiply-and-add steps. So you might guess that the way to make things faster is to build a chip that can do more multiplications per second. And engineers have done exactly that: the arithmetic parts of a modern chip are astonishingly fast and cheap. A single multiply now costs a vanishingly small amount of energy and time.

Here is the twist that this entire theme is built around. The multiplications are not the expensive part anymore. The expensive part is getting the numbers to the place where the multiplication happens. Every number a chip works with lives somewhere in memory, and memory is physically far away from the tiny circuits that do the math. 'Far away' here means micrometers or millimeters, but at the speeds a chip runs, even that distance is a long haul. Moving a number from main memory to the arithmetic unit can cost hundreds of times more energy and time than the multiplication that number takes part in. Imagine a chef who can chop an onion in a fraction of a second, but the onions are stored in a warehouse across town. The chopping is not the bottleneck. The driving back and forth is. That warehouse-across-town problem is the one root problem underneath this whole field: computers are limited by moving data around, not by doing arithmetic.

Once you see the world this way, a very tempting observation jumps out. A huge fraction of the numbers these AI models work with are zero, or so close to zero that they might as well be. Multiplying anything by zero gives zero, and adding zero to a running total changes nothing. So every zero that a chip dutifully fetches from memory, hauls across the chip, multiplies, and adds is pure wasted effort. It cost real energy and real time to move a number that had no effect on the answer. The word for 'mostly zeros' is sparsity. The entire theme is about one idea: if most of the work does not matter because it involves zeros or near-zeros or exact repeats, can we arrange to skip that work entirely, and skip the data movement it would have required?

The reason this is hard, and the reason it takes a whole research field rather than a single trick, comes down to a mismatch between how zeros are scattered and how chips like to work. Chips are fastest when they do the same operation on a big, dense, regularly-shaped block of numbers, all marching in lockstep. That regularity is what lets a chip fetch data in efficient bursts and keep all its arithmetic units busy. But zeros in a real AI model are scattered irregularly, like holes in Swiss cheese, and you do not know where they will be until you actually run the model on real input. To skip a zero you first have to find it, keep track of where the surviving non-zeros are, and then feed those survivors to the arithmetic units without leaving them idle waiting for the next scattered piece. The bookkeeping to track 'which numbers are non-zero and where do they live' can itself cost more time and memory than you saved by skipping the zeros. This is the central tension the theme wrestles with over and over: skipping useless work sounds free, but the machinery to figure out what is useless is not free at all.

There is a second, subtler layer to the problem that shows up again and again in these papers. Sparsity does not only mean 'this particular number is zero.' It can also mean 'we do not need this whole chunk of the model right now,' or 'we already computed something identical a moment ago,' or 'the human looking at this will never notice if we skip it.' In a large language model built as a Mixture-of-Experts, for example, the model is deliberately split into many specialist sub-networks called experts, and each word only wakes up a handful of them; the rest sit dormant. In a diffusion model that draws an image by refining it over hundreds of passes, most of what changes from one pass to the next is tiny, so most of the previous pass could simply be reused. In a spiking network inspired by biological neurons, activity comes in sparse on-or-off pulses, and many neurons fire in the exact same pattern, so their results are identical and one computation could serve for all of them. Each of these is a different flavor of 'work that does not matter,' and each demands its own way of detecting and skipping the waste.

All of this matters because the models keep getting bigger far faster than memory gets faster or cheaper. A frontier model may have hundreds of billions of numbers in it, far more than can fit in the fast memory sitting next to the arithmetic units, so the numbers have to be shuttled in from slower, more distant storage. On a phone or a wearable, the constraints are even harsher: limited memory, limited battery, and storage that is painfully slow at fetching scattered little pieces. If you cannot skip the work that does not matter, you simply cannot run these models at all in those places, or you burn through a battery in minutes, or a data center spends a fortune in electricity moving zeros around. Sparsity is not a luxury optimization. For a large and growing class of AI workloads, exploiting it is the difference between feasible and impossible.

the approachesHow the field actually tackles it

1.Skip the zeros: don't compute what multiplies to nothing

The most direct approach is exactly what the theme's name promises. If a number is zero, do not fetch it, do not multiply it, do not spend a cycle on it. The catch, as noted above, is that zeros are scattered irregularly, so the real engineering is in the machinery that finds the surviving non-zero numbers and hands only those to the arithmetic units, keeping the units busy rather than idling while they wait for the next scattered survivor.

Different papers attack this at different levels of granularity. BADA (hpca-2025-023) goes down to the level of individual bits inside each number: it breaks numbers into thin slices and skips any slice that is all zeros, and it does this jointly for both the model's fixed knowledge and the incoming data, so a chunk is skipped if either side is zero. Prosperity (hpca-2025-094) and Phi (isca-2025-109) work on spiking networks, where activity is naturally a stream of on-or-off pulses; instead of just skipping the off pulses, they notice that many rows of the computation have the exact same on-pulse pattern and therefore produce the exact same result, so they compute it once and reuse it. The Transitive Array (isca-2025-006) pushes reuse even further, spotting when a partial result computed a moment ago can stand in for one needed now, and builds a chip that does almost no multiplying at all.

What all of these trade off is generality for the cost of detection. The finer-grained the zeros you chase, the more bookkeeping you need to track what survived, and that bookkeeping burns area, energy, and time. The art is making the skip-detection cheaper than the work it saves.

2.Make the zeros tidy: structured sparsity the hardware can love

If scattered, irregular zeros are the problem because chips hate irregularity, one clean solution is to force the zeros into a tidy, predictable pattern in the first place. Instead of letting a model zero out numbers wherever it wants, you insist on a rule like 'in every group of four numbers, exactly two must be zero.' This is called structured sparsity (the common form is written N:M, meaning N survivors out of every M). Because the pattern is regular and known in advance, the chip can be built to march through it at full speed with almost no bookkeeping.

The trouble is that forcing zeros into a rigid pattern throws away accuracy, because the numbers you are forced to delete are not always the least important ones. Several papers try to recover that lost accuracy while keeping the hardware-friendly regularity. TB-STC (hpca-2025-062) exploits a symmetry between the forward and backward passes of training to find a two-dimensional block pattern that stays regular for the hardware yet hugs the accuracy of the messy, unconstrained case much more closely. On the small-chip end, an ISA extension for tiny microcontrollers (mlsys-2025-031) adds a single custom instruction, xDecimate, that performs the structured skip-and-multiply in one shot, so even a low-power chip can exploit the pattern.

The deeper trade-off in this branch is portability. A model tuned for one hardware's exact pattern will not run efficiently on another's. TASD (mlsys-2025-022) tackles this head-on by mathematically decomposing any messy, unstructured sparse model into a stack of tidy structured pieces, so it can run on rigid structured hardware without any special retraining.

3.Reconcile sparse with dense: get the best of both worlds

There is a stubborn, unintuitive fact hiding in this whole field: sometimes it is faster to do the wasteful dense computation, zeros and all, than to pay the bookkeeping cost of being clever about the zeros. A chip's dense math units are so heavily optimized that the overhead of tracking scattered survivors can more than eat up the savings. This branch takes that fact seriously and tries to blend the two worlds rather than pick a side.

CROSS (hpca-2025-060) is a compiler, a tool that translates a model into instructions before it runs, and its whole job is to look at each region of a model and decide: is this region sparse enough that skipping zeros pays off, or is it better to just run the fast dense math here? It then routes each region to whichever kind of computation wins, and even rewrites the sparse parts into a shape the dense math units can chew on. SparseWeaver (hpca-2025-039) does a similar thing for graph workloads directly in hardware, converting irregular sparse operations into balanced dense ones on the fly. RTSpMSpM (isca-2025-129) is the most surprising member: it noticed that finding non-zero results in sparse matrix math is mathematically the same shape of problem as figuring out where a ray of light hits a 3D object, so it repurposes the ray-tracing hardware already sitting unused inside graphics chips to do sparse math.

The trade-off here is decision cost and complexity. You need a smart-enough analysis to know when to be sparse and when to be dense, and getting that call wrong can leave you slower than either pure approach.

4.Wake only the part you need: conditional and expert sparsity

The previous branches skip individual zero numbers. This one skips whole chunks of the model. Modern large language models are increasingly built as a Mixture-of-Experts: rather than one giant network that processes every word, the model is split into many specialist sub-networks, and a lightweight router decides which few experts each word should visit. The rest stay asleep. This means the model can hold an enormous amount of knowledge in total while only ever running a small slice of it for any given word, a very different and coarser kind of 'most of the work does not matter.'

This coarse sparsity creates its own distinctive problems, and a large cluster of papers is devoted to them. The total pile of experts is far too big to fit in a chip's fast memory, so the sleeping experts have to live in slower, more distant memory and be fetched only when a word wakes them. MoE-Lightning (asplos-2025-024) and Klotski (asplos-2025-045) both fight the resulting shuttling problem, streaming expert data in a pipeline so the fetch of the next expert overlaps with the compute of the current one; Klotski cleverly gangs together words from many simultaneous requests that happen to need the same expert, so one fetch serves many. On phones, Neuralink (asplos-2025-141) and Dynamic Input Pruning (mlsys-2025-017) push the idea to the level of individual neurons, loading only the ones a given input actually activates. Even fault-tolerance benefits: MoC-System (asplos-2025-022) notices that only the awakened experts change during a training step, so it only needs to save those, slashing the cost of the safety backups that huge training runs depend on.

The recurring trade-off is that waking experts on demand turns a compute problem into a data-movement and prediction problem. You have to guess which experts you will need soon enough to start fetching them, arrange them in memory so they can be fetched in efficient bursts rather than scattered dribbles, and keep the arithmetic units fed while the fetching happens. Several of these papers (asplos-2025-105, mlsys-2025-055) are really about overlapping the communication between chips with the computation, because in a big enough system, just shuffling experts between chips can eat nearly half the total time.

5.Skip the repeats: reuse what barely changed and cut what won't be seen

Some work does not matter not because it is zero, but because it is nearly identical to something already computed, or because no one will notice if it is dropped. This branch exploits redundancy across time and redundancy against human perception.

Diffusion models, the systems that generate images by refining a rough guess over hundreds of small steps, are the classic case of redundancy across time. From one step to the next, most of the picture barely moves. EXION (hpca-2025-035) exploits this directly: it computes one step fully, then for the many following steps it simply reuses the earlier result wherever the change would have been tiny, avoiding the bulk of the arithmetic. The perceptual angle shows up in the graphics-rendering papers. MetaSapiens (asplos-2025-081) leans on a fact about human eyes: we see sharply only in the small central spot we are looking at and quite blurrily everywhere else, so it renders the center in full detail and the periphery cheaply, and separately prunes away the fine scene-detail that contributes little to the final look. FlexNeRFer (isca-2025-085) similarly strips redundant computation out of 3D scene reconstruction so it can run on a phone.

The trade-off in this branch is quality risk. Reusing an old result or dropping a 'barely noticeable' detail is a bet that the error stays invisible. These papers spend much of their effort proving that the bet is safe, bounding how much the reused-or-dropped work could have changed the final answer so that the shortcut never produces a visibly wrong result. A related, gentler cousin is model pruning (mlsys-2025-042), which permanently removes the most redundant layers of a model and then carefully repairs the small quality loss.

6.Feed the machine efficiently: formats, tiling, caching, and scheduling for irregular data

This branch steps back from 'which work to skip' and focuses on the plumbing: once you have decided to skip zeros, how do you store, fetch, and route the scattered survivors so the arithmetic units never sit idle? This is where the field's dirty secret lives. The bookkeeping around sparse data can quietly dominate everything, and a large body of work is devoted to making that bookkeeping cheap. It is unglamorous but it is where much of the real speedup is won or lost.

Several papers attack how sparse data is packed and moved. AccelES (hpca-2025-113) invents compact storage formats so that scanning a sparse matrix touches memory efficiently. SeaCache (micro-2025-068) tackles the fact that variable-length pieces of sparse data do not fit neatly into the fixed-size slots of a chip's fast local memory, wasting capacity, and it dynamically re-partitions that memory to hold both the data and the bookkeeping about it. A cluster of tiling papers (isca-2025-067, micro-2025-091) decides how to chop a giant sparse computation into chip-sized bites when you cannot know the sparsity pattern in advance; HYTE blends an offline first guess with on-the-fly adjustment, while another takes a probabilistic view, modeling where the non-zeros are likely to fall. Bootes (micro-2025-105) reorders the rows of a sparse matrix so that similar rows sit together and their memory accesses overlap, cutting distant-memory traffic.

Because no single arrangement wins for every input, a further sub-group makes the hardware reconfigurable and picks the right configuration at runtime. Quartz (micro-2025-038) and SPASM (hpca-2025-097) build flexible chips that can reshape their dataflow to match the pattern in front of them; Misam (micro-2025-106) uses a lightweight learned model to predict, from a matrix's structure, which hardware configuration will be fastest and reconfigures on the fly. The unifying trade-off across this branch is flexibility versus overhead: a chip general enough to handle any sparsity pattern risks being mediocre at all of them, so every paper here is really negotiating how much adaptability is worth its cost.

7.Sparsity beyond neural networks: graphs, scientific solvers, and specialized kernels

Sparsity is not just an AI phenomenon. It is the natural state of many older, foundational computations, and this branch carries the theme's core idea into those domains, where the mostly-zero structure comes from the real world rather than from a deliberately trained model.

Graphs are the clearest example: a social network or a web of citations is a giant grid of 'who connects to whom,' and it is overwhelmingly empty because most pairs of things are not directly connected. Multiplying such sparse grids together, the core operation behind graph analytics and graph neural networks, is exactly the sparse matrix problem the theme keeps circling. Papers like HSMU-SpGEMM (hpca-2025-099) and the SpMV framework in SPASM (hpca-2025-097) squeeze more speed out of this on flexible chips and custom hardware. Scientific computing brings its own version: Telos (isca-2025-044) accelerates a sparse solver at the heart of simulating physical systems like heat flow or fluid motion, where the sparsity comes from the fact that each point in a simulated space only directly influences its immediate neighbors. Hopps (asplos-2025-039) applies the same skip-the-idle logic to pattern-matching engines that scan streams of text, where most of the many little pattern-checkers are inactive at any instant. SparseTransX (mlsys-2025-009) reformulates knowledge-graph training so that its scattered, inefficient memory accesses collapse into a single clean sparse-matrix multiplication.

The reason to treat this as its own branch is that the sparsity here is structural and permanent, baked into the shape of the data, rather than something a model learned and might change. That often makes the pattern more predictable, which these papers exploit, but the patterns are also wildly diverse from one graph or simulation to the next, which is why flexibility and clever data layout, the concerns of the previous branch, dominate here too.

where it standsWhat's solved, what's open

What is genuinely solved: the field knows how to skip zeros, and for the well-behaved cases the wins are large and real, from a few times faster to, in the most specialized accelerators, hundreds of times more energy-efficient than a general-purpose chip. Structured sparsity is shipping in commercial hardware, Mixture-of-Experts offloading has matured to the point of running trillion-number models on a handful of modest chips, and reuse tricks for diffusion and graphics reliably cut work with no visible quality loss. The recurring, hard-won lesson across all of it is that the arithmetic was never the bottleneck; almost every paper's real contribution is a cheaper way to find, store, move, and schedule the scattered survivors so the data movement shrinks.

What is still open is the tension that will not go away: irregularity versus efficiency. The messier and more input-dependent the sparsity, the more bookkeeping it costs to exploit, and there is still no general answer to 'how sparse must data be, and in what pattern, before skipping actually beats just doing the dense work.' Most accelerators are narrow, tuned to one workload's flavor of sparsity, and pay dearly when handed a different one, which is why so much recent effort goes into reconfigurable chips and learned runtime decisions rather than fixed designs. Honest gaps remain in end-to-end numbers (many of these papers report only partial or workload-specific speedups), in portability across hardware, and in the fact that aggressive sparsity, especially reuse and pruning, is ultimately a bet against quality that each new model architecture forces the field to re-examine. The direction of travel is clear: away from fixed 'sparse chips' and toward flexible machinery that decides, per input and per region, exactly which work does not matter and can safely be skipped.

FSMoE: A Flexible and Scalable Training System for Sparse Mixture-of-Experts Models

What it does

Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) models are a style of large AI model where each input token is routed to just a handful of specialized sub-networks called experts, rather than running through every part of the model. Training these models across many GPUs is slow because tokens must be physically shipped between machines (a collective messaging operation called AlltoAll), and this communication time is not well-hidden behind computation. FSMoE is a training system that finds and exploits every possible overlap between different kinds of communication and computation to minimize wasted time, and it does so in a way that works for many different MoE routing strategies, not just one.

The method, in detail

The idea. When training MoE models on a cluster, four types of operations compete for time: (1) AlltoAll Dispatch/Combine — sending tokens between machines so each expert receives the right inputs; (2) AllGather/ReduceScatter — within-node messages that reassemble or split expert outputs across GPUs inside a single server; (3) expert computation; and (4) Gradient-AllReduce — synchronizing weight updates across data-parallel replicas. Existing systems overlap only some pairs; FSMoE tries to overlap all of them simultaneously.

How it works. First, FSMoE breaks every MoE layer into six plug-in sub-modules (Gate, Order, I-Order, Dispatch, Combine, Expert) with hook points for custom logic. This modular design lets researchers swap in any routing algorithm without changing the scheduler. Second, a profiler fits simple linear performance models to each sub-module (startup cost + bytes-per-second), then classifies the workload into one of four cases depending on which operation dominates. For each case, a math optimization (called SLSQP, a standard constrained solver) finds the optimal number of chunks r to split the input into, so that communication and computation tiles interleave without gaps. Crucially, the forward pass and backward pass each get their own optimal chunk count, because the backward pass involves extra gradient work. Third, the gradient synchronization step is handled separately: a two-step algorithm greedily assigns gradient chunks to the idle gaps already computed by step two, then uses a global evolutionary optimizer to fine-tune the assignment across all layers.

The key design choice. The system operates on three parallel GPU streams simultaneously — one for intra-node messages, one for inter-node messages, and one for computation — and deliberately stages the chunked input so all three streams stay busy.

The result. FSMoE is 1.18-1.22x faster than Tutel/PipeMoE across 1,458 tested configurations and 1.19-3.01x faster on real GPT-2 and Mixtral MoE models on clusters of 32-48 GPUs.

EXION: Exploiting Inter-and Intra-Iteration Output Sparsity for Diffusion Models

What it does

Diffusion models — the AI systems that generate images, videos, and audio from text — are slow and energy-hungry because they repeat the same large neural network hundreds of times. EXION is a custom chip design paired with smart software that skips the vast majority of those repeated calculations by noticing that most outputs barely change from one iteration to the next, achieving up to 379x speedup and over 3000x better energy efficiency versus a standard server GPU.

The method, in detail

The idea. A diffusion model works by running a neural network repeatedly, each pass removing a little more noise from a scrambled image until a clean picture emerges. Most of the computation happens in two places: the 'feed-forward network' layers (FFN — two stacked linear transformations with a nonlinear activation between them) and the attention mechanism (which lets every part of the image look at every other part). EXION attacks both.

How it works — inter-iteration reuse (FFN-Reuse). The key observation is that the output of the nonlinear activation function (called GELU — a smooth curve that squashes large values and zeroes out small ones) changes very little between consecutive iterations. EXION does one full 'dense' pass, records which outputs are above a threshold and which are below, then for the next N passes it reuses the below-threshold outputs unchanged and only recomputes the above-threshold ones. This makes 70–97% of those outputs zero-work in sparse iterations, cutting FFN computation by 52–85%.

How it works — within-iteration skipping (eager prediction). Before doing the expensive exact attention calculation, EXION runs a cheap approximation using logarithms (which turn multiplications into additions, much faster). It identifies which attention scores will be negligibly small and skips computing them. On average this skips 26% of the query calculations and 22% of the key/value projections. A two-step refinement of the approximation (finding two leading bits instead of one) avoids the accuracy loss that a single-step version would cause.

Data compaction (ConMerge). Sparse matrices — where most values are zero — are useless if the hardware still has to check every slot. EXION first 'condenses' by removing entire columns that are all-zero, then 'merges' pairs of sparse blocks into one denser block so the math units stay busy. For Stable Diffusion, merging reduces remaining columns from 77% down to 8%.

The key hardware piece. A 16x16 grid of dot-product units (tiny multiply-accumulate circuits) has per-unit switches that let it flexibly route inputs from different memory banks to handle whatever mix of dense and sparse data ConMerge produces — without the large complex routing networks other designs require.

The result. Compared to an NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada server GPU: 3.2–379x faster, 45–3068x more energy-efficient. Chip area: 152 mm^2 for the 24-core version versus 609 mm^2 for the GPU.

SparseTransX: Efficient Training of Translation-Based Knowledge Graph Embeddings Using Sparse Matrix Operations

What it does

Knowledge graphs store facts as triples — (Paris, capital-of, France) — and training an AI to learn useful number-vector representations of those triples is slow because of irregular memory access patterns called scatter and gather operations. SparseTransX reformulates that training step as a single sparse-matrix multiply (SpMM — multiplying a mostly-empty matrix by a dense one), replacing many small random reads and writes with one highly-optimized bulk operation, achieving up to 5.3x CPU speedup and 4.2x GPU speedup.

The method, in detail

The idea. During each training step, a knowledge graph model looks up the number-vector (embedding) of each entity in a triple, computes a score, and scatters the error signal (gradient) back to each entity's embedding slot. These individual lookups and scatter-writes are slow because they access memory in an irregular pattern that defeats the cache and prevents the CPU or GPU from doing work in bulk.

How it works. Instead of looking up entities one at a time, SparseTransX builds a sparse incidence matrix A. This is a mostly-zero table where each row represents one training triple, and the column for the head entity gets a +1 while the column for the tail entity gets a -1. Multiplying A by the dense embedding table E — a standard sparse-dense matrix multiply (SpMM) — directly produces the vector difference (head - tail) for every triple in the batch simultaneously. For models like TransE that also need the relation vector, the sparse matrix is extended to include a column for the relation (with +1), and the embedding matrix is extended to stack entity and relation embeddings together. The same trick carries through to the backward pass: the gradient computation reduces to another SpMM using the transpose of A, which is also sparse.

The key design choice. Because the incidence matrix has exactly two or three nonzero entries per row regardless of graph size, it stays extremely sparse even for enormous graphs. This means the SpMM kernel can use cache-blocking, SIMD vectorization (processing multiple numbers at once using wide CPU registers), and GPU warp-level primitives — all techniques already perfected for sparse graph neural network libraries — without any modifications.

The result. Across seven knowledge graph datasets on CPU (AMD EPYC 64-core) and GPU (NVIDIA A100), SparseTransX beats existing frameworks TorchKGE, DGL-KE, and PyTorch Geometric by 2-5.3x in training time and uses 2-11x less GPU memory, while producing the same or better prediction accuracy.

Distributed Submodular Subset Selection at Billion Scale

What it does

Training a machine learning model on every available image or data point is wasteful — many examples are redundant. A class of mathematical tools called submodular functions can score candidate subsets so that a greedy selection finds a diverse, high-utility sample that closely matches what training on the full dataset would achieve. The catch is that all existing algorithms require at least one central machine with enough main memory (RAM) to hold either the full dataset or the final chosen subset, which becomes impossible at billion-scale. This paper removes that requirement with a fully distributed algorithm that never needs to centralize the data.

The method, in detail

The idea. Submodular subset selection has a diminishing-returns property: adding a data point to an already-large, diverse subset adds less value than adding the same point to a small subset. The standard approach greedily picks the point with the highest marginal gain at each step, but this is inherently sequential and requires all data in one machine's memory.

How it works — bounding first. The core insight is that for each candidate data point you can compute both a maximum possible utility (how valuable it could be if no similar points are in the subset yet) and a minimum guaranteed utility (how valuable it is even accounting for all possible similar neighbors that might also be included). If a point's guaranteed minimum utility is higher than the maximum possible utility of the k-th best remaining point, it must be in the optimal subset — add it without looking at anything else. Conversely, if a point's maximum utility is too low, discard it. Both these checks can be done independently on each machine using only local neighbor lists and a shared global threshold, with no centralized join needed. Machines iteratively tighten these bounds across rounds (alternating "grow" steps that add certain winners and "shrink" steps that drop certain losers) until no more decisions can be made.

The key design choice. The paper uses pairwise submodular functions — the value of a subset is the sum of individual point utilities minus a penalty proportional to how similar the selected points are to each other. This pair-wise structure means the only information each machine needs about other machines is a list of which of its neighbors have been selected so far, communicated as lightweight ID sets rather than raw data vectors.

Distributed greedy for the remainder. If bounding does not fully determine the subset (common for mid-sized targets), the remaining candidates are randomly partitioned across machines. Each machine runs the greedy algorithm on its local shard and emits its local winners. The union of winners becomes the input for the next round, with re-partitioning. Using more rounds (up to 32) with adaptive partition sizing — fewer partitions when the candidate set has shrunk — consistently reaches within 2–3% of the centralized solution quality.

The result. The algorithm scales to 13 billion data points (a stress-test version of ImageNet), runs on Apache Beam distributed infrastructure, and produces subsets within 0–5% of the quality achievable by a centralized algorithm that requires terabytes of RAM on one machine, at a fraction of the memory cost.

N:M Sparse DNN Kernels for RISC-V MCUs via Custom ISA Extension

What it does

Running AI models on tiny, battery-powered microcontrollers (the small chips inside IoT sensors and wearables) is much harder than on a laptop or GPU, because the chip has almost no memory and very limited power budget. One way to shrink an AI model enough to fit is pruning — zeroing out the least-important weights — but standard chips can't skip the zeroed-out math, so the speedup is theoretical rather than real. This paper makes pruning genuinely fast on RISC-V microcontrollers (a popular open-source chip design for low-power devices) by writing specially crafted compute routines for a pattern called N:M sparsity, and adding one new chip instruction that cuts the overhead of those routines nearly in half.

The method, in detail

The idea. N:M sparsity means: in every block of M weight values, exactly N are kept non-zero and the rest are forced to zero during training. The pattern is regular enough to store compactly (just the non-zero values plus a few bits recording which positions they occupy) and predictable enough that hardware can exploit it, but irregular enough that mainstream dense routines can't handle it without wasted work.

How it works. The authors target the Vega chip (blueprint for the commercial GAP9 microcontroller), which has eight small RISC-V processor cores sharing fast on-chip memory. Their sparse convolution and fully-connected routines use a technique called 'Decimate Im2col': the standard step that rearranges image patches into a flat buffer (im2col — image to column — is a classic trick to turn convolution into a matrix multiply) runs unchanged, and then a new inner-loop step called 'decimate' picks only the activations corresponding to non-zero weights from that buffer. The key bottleneck is unpacking the compressed position indices and loading the right memory bytes for each non-zero weight — this takes 19 out of every 22 instructions in the software-only version.

The key design choice. A single new instruction, xDecimate, fuses index unpacking and memory loading into one operation. It uses a small auto-incrementing counter register so consecutive calls automatically advance to the next non-zero position — no explicit index arithmetic needed. This shrinks the inner loop from 22 instructions to 12, a 1.9x raw instruction speedup, at a chip-area cost of only 5% extra silicon.

The result. The method, in detail

The idea. Standard backpropagation has four biologically and practically inconvenient properties: (1) weight transport — the backward pass needs the exact same weight matrices used in the forward pass, transposed; (2) non-locality — the error at layer 5 must reach all the way back to layer 1; (3) update locking — layer 1 cannot update until the full forward and backward passes finish; (4) frozen activities — the output of each layer must be kept in memory until the backward pass arrives. Bio-FO eliminates all four.

How it works. Each hidden layer l in the network is trained completely independently:

1. Forward pass through layer l computes an activation vector h_l from the previous layer's output. 2. This replaces the need for a true backward path — there is no symmetry requirement because B_l is never learned. 3. A standard cross-entropy loss is computed between a_l and the true label. The gradient flows backward only through B_l to compute a correction for h_l, which then propagates back through layer l alone to update its weights. 4. Layer l's weights are immediately updated. No other layer is involved. No other layer's activations need to be stored.

A sparsity mask S_l can be applied to the weight matrix at each layer, enabling the same algorithm to work for locally connected (LC) networks and convolutional neural networks (CNNs) — where each unit only connects to nearby units — which is a more biologically realistic architecture.

The key design choice. Using a fixed random projection matrix B_l rather than a learned backward pathway avoids weight transport while still providing a useful error signal: it is mathematically an unbiased estimate of the true gradient direction. The 'local only' training rule means layers can update in parallel or on a rolling basis, using only the memory for one layer at a time.

The result. The method, in detail

The idea. Basic algebra says A x B = (A1 + A2) x B = A1 x B + A2 x B. TASD applies this decomposition greedily: at each step, extract the largest elements that fit the pattern into the next term and keep the residual for the next term.

How it works for weights (static). The key design choice. Keeping the output partial-sum matrix C stationary in fast on-chip memory while rotating the decomposed A tiles through avoids expensive off-chip memory traffic for the accumulation, making the overhead of two sequential passes small.

The method, in detail

The idea. When GPU A is sending tokens to GPU B's specialist, GPU A's specialist should simultaneously be doing math on the tokens it already received. Existing systems do this at a coarse level — split work into two big chunks and pipeline them — but the two halves don't fit together neatly in time, leaving the GPU idle at the start and end of each chunk.

How it works — shared tensor dependency resolving. Each MoE layer has a shared buffer (called a shared tensor) that one operation writes into and the next reads from. Communication and computation are bundled into a single fused GPU program (a 'kernel') rather than two separate programs on two separate scheduling queues. Inside this kernel, thread blocks (groups of GPU cores that execute together) are partitioned into dedicated compute groups and dedicated communication groups. The ratio of compute to communication thread blocks is profiled offline for each model configuration and sequence length, then selected at runtime from a pre-built table. This prevents the two from interfering with each other's hardware resources and avoids the scheduling jitter that plagued prior kernel-level approaches.

The key design choice. Fusing everything into one kernel with thread-block specialization is what enables the fine-grained overlap: the communication groups can start moving the next batch of tokens the instant the computation groups free those rows in the shared buffer, rather than waiting for an OS-level kernel launch.

The result. It has been deployed in a production cluster of over ten thousand GPUs, saving millions of GPU-hours.
