# Moving the Gradient: The Interconnect Wall of Distributed AI

## The Problem

Start with a fact about physics, not machine learning. A modern AI model is trained by copying it onto thousands of chips, letting each chip process a different slice of the data, and then forcing all of those chips to agree. "Agree" has a precise meaning. After each training step, every chip has computed a *gradient* — a large array of numbers, one per adjustable parameter in the model, that says which direction to nudge each parameter. For the update to be correct, every chip must end up holding the *sum* of all the other chips' gradients. There is no shortcut. If you have 25,000 chips, the gradient computed on chip #17,000 must physically travel, as electrons or photons, to all 24,999 others, and their contributions must travel back. This movement of numbers between chips is the entire subject of this essay, and it is now the dominant cost of training large models.

Consider the numbers for one training step of a large model. A model with 100 billion parameters, stored at 16 bits each, produces a gradient of 200 GB. The standard way to sum gradients across N chips is the *ring all-reduce*: arrange the chips in a logical ring, and have each chip pass a slice of its data to its neighbor, add the incoming slice to its own, and pass it along. After going around the ring twice, every chip holds the full sum. The math of the ring is unforgiving: each chip must send and receive roughly 2×(N−1)/N times the size of the gradient — for large N, that is very close to *twice* the full gradient tensor crossing each chip's network link. On a 400 Gb/s InfiniBand link, which delivers about 50 GB/s of usable bandwidth, moving 2×200 GB of data takes about 8 seconds. If the actual arithmetic of the forward and backward pass took 1 second, the chips spend 8 of every 9 seconds waiting for the network. The expensive silicon is idle 89% of the time.

This is the wall. Compute has grown faster than the wires that connect compute. A single H100 GPU performs roughly 10^15 floating-point operations per second, but its fastest link to a *neighboring* GPU (NVLink) moves only 900 GB/s, and its link to a GPU in another server (InfiniBand) moves an order of magnitude less. The ratio of "operations you can do" to "bytes you can move between chips" keeps getting worse with every generation of silicon, because transistors shrink but the physical channels — copper traces, connector pins, the reach of a signal through a printed circuit board — do not shrink at the same rate, and the energy to push a bit across a centimeter of copper barely improves at all. A signal at 200 Gb/s loses more than 40 dB of its strength crossing a few inches of package and cable, meaning more than 99.99% of the transmitted power is gone by the time it arrives; recovering the bits requires power-hungry equalization circuits that themselves cost picojoules per bit.

So the field of interconnect research exists because of a single, sharpening tension: the value of a large AI machine comes from having tens of thousands of chips work as one, but the only thing that makes them "one" — the movement of numbers between them — is bounded by the bandwidth, latency, energy, and physical reach of the links between chips, and those links are improving far slower than the chips they connect. Every paper in this theme is an attack on some part of that sentence.

## Why the Obvious Solution Does Not Work

The obvious answer is "add more bandwidth." Just make the wires fatter and faster. This fails at three levels simultaneously, which is exactly why the research splits into distinct subthemes rather than one clean fix.

First, bandwidth is physically capped by pin count and signal integrity. A chip can only have so many pins on its edge, and each pin can only carry so many bits per second before the signal degrades beyond recovery. You can raise the per-pin rate (the SerDes problem, Subtheme 4) or use light instead of electrons (the photonics problem, Subtheme 3), but both are hard physics problems with their own energy and reliability limits. Second, even infinite link bandwidth does not help if the *topology* — the graph of which chip connects to which — forces data to take long paths or fight for shared links (Subtheme 2). A ring all-reduce over a badly-matched topology wastes most of the bandwidth you paid for. Third, even a perfect network is useless if the *algorithm* driving it moves more data than necessary, or blocks the compute engine while it waits (Subtheme 1). The gradient sum is a fixed communication requirement, but *when* and *in what order* and *in what compressed form* you move it is a software design space worth multiples of speedup. Because the constraint is simultaneously algorithmic, topological, and physical, no single layer can be fixed in isolation — a win at one layer just relocates the bottleneck to another.

## Subtheme 1: Making the Network Disappear Behind Computation

### What It Is

If the network is going to take 8 seconds while compute takes 1, the first move is not to make the network faster but to make the compute engine *never wait* for it. The idea: while the chip is still doing arithmetic on one slice of data, start sending the finished slices over the network in the background, so that communication and computation happen at the same time instead of one after the other. In the best case, the network time is entirely "hidden" underneath compute time and the step takes max(compute, comm) instead of compute + comm. Achieving this requires slicing a monolithic operation — a large matrix multiply and its following all-reduce — into many small tiles, and carefully choreographing which tiles compute while which tiles are in flight. A second, orthogonal move is to send *fewer bytes*: if you can prove that most of the gradient is near-zero and only a small fraction matters, you transmit only that fraction.

### What the Research Community Is Doing

At MLSys 2025, TileLink introduced a set of "tile-centric primitives" — small operations like `producer_tile_notify` and `consumer_tile_wait` that let a compiler express, at the granularity of a single tile of a matrix, exactly when a piece of data is ready to be sent and when a remote piece has arrived. Hand-writing this choreography in CUDA and NVSHMEM is famously error-prone; TileLink generated overlapping kernels that matched the performance of the expert-written FLUX library using roughly 200 lines of Python instead of 2,000 lines of CUDA, and ran 1.17× to 20.76× faster than the naive non-overlapping baseline on A100 and H100 systems connected by NVLink and InfiniBand. At ASPLOS 2025, Concerto attacked the same problem from the compiler side by formulating communication scheduling as a resource-constrained project scheduling problem — the same class of optimization used to schedule construction projects — and handing it to an off-the-shelf solver, which found near-optimal overlap schedules that generalized across every parallelism strategy without the hand-tuning that Megatron-LM, DeepSpeed, and Alpa require.

The "send fewer bytes" line is equally active. At MLSys 2025, Radius observed that in gradient sparsification — the technique of keeping only the top-k largest gradient values — the *identity* of the largest values becomes temporally stable after the first 15–20% of training. By reusing the same top-k indices for 200 steps, Radius replaced the expensive all-gather (whose cost grows linearly with worker count) with a dense all-reduce over only the sparse subset, cutting communication by 89.7% at 40% sparsity and improving overall training speed by 19% — and, critically, it was the first such scheme compatible with the AdamW optimizer, which had previously diverged under top-k sparsification. At ISCA 2025, Chimera took a structural view: when tensor, pipeline, sequence, and data parallelism are combined, their collective operations contain provable redundancy, and fusing adjacent communication operators into a single semantically-equivalent one delivered 1.32–1.58× on the forward pass and up to 7.06× network bandwidth improvement.

### The Tradeoff

Overlap is nearly free when it works, but it is fragile. It depends on there being enough independent compute to hide the communication behind; at very large scale, or for communication-heavy operations like the all-to-all in mixture-of-experts models, there simply is not enough arithmetic to cover the network time, and the step falls back toward being communication-bound. Compression trades exactness for bandwidth: sparsifying or quantizing gradients can slow or destabilize convergence, which is why Radius's compatibility with a real optimizer was the actual contribution rather than the raw compression ratio. And the scheduling complexity is real — tile-level choreography is a combinatorial space that pushes the difficulty into the compiler, which is why the winning papers are as much about compiler abstractions as about the network.

## Subtheme 2: Choosing the Shape of the Network

### What It Is

Given N chips, you must decide the *topology*: the graph of which chip has a direct wire to which. This choice is fixed at the moment you build the machine and it constrains everything above it. A ring gives each chip two neighbors — cheap, but data must traverse up to N/2 hops. A 2D mesh (a grid) gives four neighbors and shorter paths. A fat-tree connects everything through layers of switches so any two chips are a few hops apart, but the top-level switch links become a scarce, oversubscribed resource that all long-distance traffic must share. A dragonfly groups chips densely and connects the groups with sparse global links. The topology determines both the *diameter* (worst-case hops between two chips) and the *bisection bandwidth* (how much data can cross an imaginary line cutting the machine in half) — and an all-reduce that maps badly onto the topology will congest a few links while others sit idle. So the research is partly about designing better topologies and partly about designing collective algorithms that match a given topology's shape.

### What the Research Community Is Doing

At HPCA 2025, TidalMesh made the case that the standard practice of taking a ring or tree algorithm and bending it onto a 2D mesh is fundamentally lossy — the logical pattern and the physical grid disagree, wasting bandwidth. Their all-reduce, designed natively for the mesh, pushes data in a wave-like ("tidal") pattern that saturates the mesh's bidirectional links, yielding up to 24% faster all-reduce on a 64-node 8×8 mesh. At SC 2025, Bine Trees addressed oversubscribed networks — where internal links are dense but the global links between groups are sparse — with a "binomial negabinary" tree family that reduced global-link traffic by up to 33% and delivered up to 5× on collectives across dragonfly, fat-tree, and torus systems. On the topology-design side, ISCA 2025's Zettafly proposed a two-layer family that decouples the size of a "non-blocking region" from the global scale, so a zettascale machine can be partitioned into non-blocking sub-networks sized to match the small-to-medium jobs that actually dominate real clusters, while keeping any inter-group path to at most one global-router hop.

The same battle recurs at every scale of the hierarchy. At ISCA 2025, NetCrafter targeted hierarchical multi-GPU systems where intra-group links are fat and inter-group links are thin, shaping traffic with flit-stitching (coalescing partially-filled network flits headed to the same place), flit-trimming (dropping speculative prefetches not yet needed), and priority sequencing to squeeze the scarce inter-group bandwidth. For chiplet-scale networks, ISCA 2025's evaluation of Ruche Networks showed that adding uniform long-range bypass links to a 2D mesh preserves the mesh's clean physical scalability while cutting hop counts, beating both mesh and torus on RTL-verified power and area. And SC 2025's report on "Large-Message All-to-All at Frontier Scale" found that at 8,192 nodes (65,536 tasks), even large-message all-to-all becomes latency-dominated, requiring latency-centric strategies that beat the vendor MPI implementation — a reminder that at extreme scale the topology's diameter, not its bandwidth, sets the floor.

### The Tradeoff

Topology is the least flexible layer: it is soldered into the machine. A topology tuned for one communication pattern (say, the all-reduce of data-parallel training) can be actively bad for another (the all-to-all of mixture-of-experts routing). Richer topologies with more links per chip cost more, draw more power, and are harder to route physically on a wafer or board — which is why so many papers cling to the humble 2D mesh and try to augment it (Ruche, TidalMesh) rather than replace it. And every topology forces a co-designed algorithm: the elegance of TidalMesh or Bine Trees is inseparable from the specific graph they assume, so a topology choice locks in an algorithmic commitment for the life of the hardware.

## Subtheme 3: Replacing Electrons With Light

### What It Is

A bit sent as a voltage on a copper wire loses energy to resistance and radiates into neighbors; over a few centimeters at 200 Gb/s it is nearly destroyed, and the recovery circuitry burns picojoules per bit. A bit sent as light in a silicon waveguide suffers almost none of this loss over the same distance, and — crucially — you can send many bits at once on different colors of light through the same physical channel, a trick called wavelength-division multiplexing (WDM). The physics strongly favors photons for anything beyond a few centimeters. The catch is that computation happens in electrons: every optical link needs a *modulator* to turn an electrical signal into light and a *photodetector* to turn it back, and integrating those onto or beside a compute die without wrecking yield, cost, or thermals is the entire engineering fight. The prize is enormous bandwidth density at a fraction of the energy per bit, which is why the largest players are betting on it.

### What the Research Community Is Doing

Hot Chips 2025 read like a photonics coming-out party. Celestial AI presented what it billed as the world's first SoC with in-die optical I/O — photonic modulators, detectors, and waveguides integrated directly on the compute die so accelerators talk to each other in light without going through external optics. Lightmatter's Passage M1000 took the interposer route: a 3D photonic interposer, the slab beneath the chiplets, that routes optical signals between them with WDM to pack massive bandwidth onto a single optical layer. Broadcom and others presented co-packaged silicon photonics *switches* for "gigawatt AI factories," moving the optics inside the switch package to kill the long electrical traces between switch silicon and its optics, cutting the power and latency of data-center-scale switching. And a UCIe optical I/O retimer chiplet showed the pragmatic middle path — a standardized chiplet that bridges the electrical UCIe die-to-die standard to an optical link, so scale-up systems can go optical without redesigning the compute die.

Underneath the product announcements, the analysis papers are doing the sobering accounting. At ICCAD 2025, "Waferscale Silicon Photonics Systems: A Cost-Benefit Analysis" found that photonics helps only for specific applications and specific topologies, and that microring-resonator power limits and waveguide overheads make high-connectivity optical implementations outright infeasible at wafer scale — the benefit is real but narrow. Tooling is catching up: ICCAD 2025's WROXIM built the first network-level simulator for wavelength-routed optical networks-on-chip, and FAB, at the same venue, produced a graph-matching method for allocating optical bandwidth (core-to-port mapping plus microring radii) that ran 500,000× faster than prior methods while cutting worst-case latency by 87.5%. At the device level, ISSCC 2025 showed a 0.9 pJ/b 108 Gb/s PAM-4 VCSEL-based direct-drive optical engine that eliminates the DSP re-timer entirely for co-packaged optics, and a 1.54 pJ/b 64 Gb/s coherent 16-QAM optical receiver that doubles throughput per wavelength using an analog phase detector instead of a power-hungry digital one.

### The Tradeoff

Photonics wins on distance and energy but loses on integration cost, thermal sensitivity, and maturity. Microring resonators — the tiny rings that pick out one wavelength — drift with temperature and must be actively tuned, spending power and control complexity. Lasers add a component that can fail and that runs hot. The ICCAD cost analyses are the field's conscience: for short links, copper is still cheaper and simpler, so the crossover point where light wins keeps moving with each SerDes advance in Subtheme 4. Photonics is not replacing electrical interconnect wholesale; it is being surgically inserted exactly where the electrical wall is highest — scale-up links between accelerators and data-center switching — and the research is largely about finding and widening that crossover.

## Subtheme 4: Squeezing the Last Bits Through Copper and Across the Die Gap

### What It Is

Before you replace copper with light, you push copper as far as it will go — because copper is cheap and mature. Two frontiers matter. The first is the *SerDes* (serializer/deserializer): the circuit that shovels bits onto a single wire at the highest possible rate. Getting to 200+ Gb/s on one lane means using multi-level signaling (PAM-4 encodes two bits per symbol as four voltage levels instead of one bit as two levels) and then fighting the resulting signal-integrity nightmare with elaborate equalization. The second frontier is the *die-to-die* link: when a big chip is split into smaller "chiplets" to improve yield and mix process nodes, those chiplets must talk across a millimeter-scale gap through a package or interposer. This is a different regime from long-reach SerDes — extremely short, so you optimize for raw bandwidth density (bits per second per millimeter of die edge) and energy per bit rather than reach. The industry standard here is UCIe (Universal Chiplet Interconnect Express), and getting silicon to hit its spec is a live research problem.

### What the Research Community Is Doing

The long-reach SerDes results at ISSCC 2025 are a direct measure of the copper wall. A 4nm DSP-based PAM-4 transceiver hit 212.5 Gb/s while compensating more than 50 dB of channel loss — recovering a signal after 99.999% of its power is gone — using extended feed-forward equalization with floating reflection-cancellation taps and maximum-likelihood sequence detection. A 5nm counterpart reached the same 212.5 Gb/s at 2.2 pJ/b with 46 dB reach, targeting the 200G-per-lane links that build 1.6 Tb/s network infrastructure. At the switch, ISSCC 2025's Tomahawk5 packed 51.2 Tb/s of switching into a single 5nm monolithic die — double its predecessor — feeding it with SerDes cores flexible enough to drive copper, front-panel optics, linear-pluggable optics, or co-packaged optics from the same silicon.

The die-to-die results show copper thriving at short reach. An ISSCC 2025 UCIe-compliant link in 3nm hit 10.5 Tb/s/mm of bandwidth density at 0.6 pJ/b, and solved a subtle problem: dynamic clock gating in a chiplet link causes a current surge and voltage droop that misaligns data sampling, which they fixed with a matched-delay architecture that keeps clock-to-data timing stable through the power transition. A separate 3nm UCIe link in 2.5D CoWoS packaging reached 5.27 Tb/s/mm at 0.29 pJ/b, and was notable as the first full standard-compliant demonstration proving that a *standardized* chiplet interconnect can match proprietary ones — the whole economic point of UCIe. Pushing further, a 28nm simultaneous bidirectional die-to-die transceiver sent 64 Gb/s in both directions on the same wire at once, cancelling the resulting echo and near/far-end crosstalk in analog and even removing the ground shielding to raise density, at a bit-error rate below 10^-16. And SC 2025's "Implicit Sequence Numbers" work extended these link standards (CXL, NVLink, UALink) to multi-node reliability, detecting silent flit drops without header overhead — because at these rates and scales, an occasional dropped flit is a certainty, not a possibility.

### The Tradeoff

Every bit of copper progress is bought with power and silicon complexity. The equalization needed for 50 dB loss compensation is itself a substantial power and area cost, and PAM-4's four voltage levels are four times more sensitive to noise than simple two-level signaling, demanding cleaner circuits. Die-to-die links win enormous bandwidth density precisely because they are short — that density collapses with reach, which is exactly the gap photonics fills. And chiplets, which create the die-to-die problem, are themselves a response to a different wall (yield and cost of huge monolithic dies), so this subtheme is downstream of a packaging decision made for reasons that have nothing to do with communication. The copper frontier and the photonics frontier are in direct competition for the same links, and each SerDes record pushes the copper/light crossover a little further out.

## How the Pieces Fit Together

The four subthemes are not parallel tracks; they are a stack, and each one's success determines where the bottleneck lands next. Physical-layer advances (Subtheme 4) raise the raw bandwidth and lower the energy of a single link. Topology (Subtheme 2) decides how many of those links a given byte must cross and whether they congest. Algorithms (Subtheme 1) decide how many bytes must move at all and whether the compute engine ever has to wait for them. Photonics (Subtheme 3) is the escape hatch for the specific links where copper has hit its wall. A win at any one layer relocates the constraint: TileLink's perfect overlap only matters until the network is so slow there is no compute left to hide behind it; TidalMesh's topology-matched all-reduce only matters if the SerDes feeding the mesh links can keep them full; a 51.2 Tb/s switch only matters if the collective algorithm can actually use that bisection bandwidth. The strongest papers in this corpus are the ones that grab two layers at once — Moment (SC 2025) co-optimizes physical topology *and* data placement for GNN training with a max-flow formulation, 6.51× over out-of-core baselines; FRED (ISCA 2025) distributes tiny microswitches across a wafer to make the *topology itself reconfigurable* and runs collectives inside the switches, 1.34–1.87× on end-to-end training.

The dominant strategy emerging from the full corpus is *co-design against a moving crossover point*. There is no single winning technology; there is a frontier where electrical gives way to optical, where the general-purpose network gives way to in-network computing, where the reactive interconnect gives way to a proactive one. In-network computing is the sharpest expression of this: rather than move data to compute, move compute into the network. SC 2025's mpitofino ran MPI reduction operations inside a programmable Tofino switch at near-100 Gb/s line rate; Nvidia's ConnectX-8 SuperNIC and Broadcom's Tomahawk Ultra offload all-reduce and all-gather onto the NIC and switch so the sum is computed *as the data flows through the fabric*, never touching a CPU. HPCA 2025's Push Multicast applied the same instinct inside a manycore chip, converting the last-level cache from a reactive responder into a proactive multicast pusher that prunes redundant in-flight requests, saving 43% of network-on-chip bandwidth. The bet, repeated at every scale from on-chip to data-center, is that the network should stop being a dumb pipe and start being a compute substrate.

Where the money is going is legible from the venue split. The systems and algorithms venues (SC, ASPLOS, MLSys, ISCA, HPCA) are betting on software and topology co-design because it ships today on existing silicon and delivers 1.2–7× with a compiler pass or a new collective. The circuits and integration venues (ISSCC, Hot Chips, ICCAD, DAC) are betting on the physical layer — pushing copper SerDes past 200 Gb/s/lane, standardizing chiplet links through UCIe, and staging the transition to co-packaged optics — because those are the moves that raise the ceiling everyone else operates under. The two bets are complementary: the software layer extracts the last drop of value from whatever bandwidth the physical layer provides, and the physical layer keeps raising how much there is to extract.

## What Remains Hard

- **All-to-all for mixture-of-experts remains bandwidth-bound with nothing to hide it behind.** MoE routing sends every token to a different subset of chips, generating an all-to-all whose data volume rivals the compute, so the overlap tricks of Subtheme 1 have insufficient arithmetic to cover it — a topology-and-algorithm co-design specifically for token routing is an open frontier.
- **The photonics crossover point is undefined and application-specific.** No one can yet state, for a given link length, data rate, and cost budget, whether copper SerDes or silicon photonics wins — the ICCAD cost analyses show it depends on topology and workload in ways that resist a clean rule.
- **Microring thermal stability at scale is unsolved.** Wavelength-routed optical networks depend on microring resonators that drift with temperature and must be continuously tuned; the control power and complexity of keeping thousands of rings on-wavelength across a hot wafer has no cheap answer.
- **Reliability at 200+ Gb/s and 25,000 chips makes silent errors statistically certain.** Implicit-sequence-number and CRC/FEC schemes detect flit drops, but the field lacks a unified, low-overhead reliability model spanning die-to-die, intra-node, and inter-node links simultaneously.
- **Topology is frozen at build time but workloads are not.** A machine soldered for data-parallel all-reduce is mismatched to the next model architecture; reconfigurable fabrics like FRED and EIGEN's active interposer point at an answer but at unproven cost, and the general question of how much reconfigurability is worth its overhead is open.
- **In-network computing has no portable programming model.** Offloading collectives onto switches and NICs today means bespoke P4 or vendor firmware; there is no abstraction that lets a training framework target "the network as a compute device" the way it targets a GPU.
- **Fault detection and communication scheduling are still separate systems.** C4 (HPCA 2025) showed that collective-communication traffic patterns are a diagnostic oracle for hardware faults, but detecting a failed link, isolating it, and re-optimizing the collective schedule around it in real time — without restarting a 25,000-GPU job — remains a hand-built, fragile pipeline rather than a principled one.
