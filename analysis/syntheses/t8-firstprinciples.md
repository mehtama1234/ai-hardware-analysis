# When Silicon Lies: Building Trust into Hardware That Cannot Be Trusted

## The Problem

Start with a single transistor at a 3nm process node. Its gate — the switch that decides whether current flows — is now on the order of ten silicon atoms across. You cannot manufacture ten atoms precisely. When a fabrication line deposits dopant atoms into the channel to set the transistor's switching voltage, the number that actually land is governed by chance: a target of, say, 100 atoms will vary from device to device by the square root of that count, roughly ten atoms, and that is enough to shift the threshold voltage by 20% between two transistors that the design says are identical. A modern processor has tens of billions of these switches. The IBM Telum II described at ISSCC 2025 packs 43 billion transistors into 600mm² of silicon threaded by 38 kilometers of wire. Every one of those switches is a slightly different switch than the blueprint promised, and the chip has to work anyway.

That is the first of three distinct reliability problems, and it exists before the chip has ever run a single instruction. It is a *manufacturing* problem: the physical object diverges from the design. The second problem appears once the chip is powered on. A transistor that was correct at fabrication degrades under use — trapped charge accumulates in the gate oxide (the mechanism engineers call negative bias temperature instability), high-energy carriers damage the channel, and metal atoms in the interconnect physically migrate under current density until a wire thins and opens (electromigration, governed by Korhonen's equation, a diffusion PDE that ICCAD 2025 papers are still finding faster ways to solve). Layered on top of slow aging is instantaneous corruption: a cosmic-ray neutron or an alpha particle strikes a memory cell and flips a bit, at a sea-level rate of roughly one error per 4GB of DRAM per month, rising with altitude and rising sharply in orbit, where DAC 2025's REMU work builds radiation emulators specifically because commercial GPUs in space have no other way to predict how a stray particle will corrupt a neural network's weights.

The third problem is the strangest, because it is not about physics at all — it is about knowledge. Before a chip is fabricated, the design exists only as register-transfer-level (RTL) code: a description, in a language like Verilog, of what every logic gate should do on every clock cycle. Fabricating that design costs millions of dollars and takes months, and if the RTL contained a logic bug, the entire batch of silicon is scrap. So the design must be *proven correct in advance* — verified against its specification without ever running it on real hardware. This is design-time verification, and it is where the majority of a chip project's engineering effort actually goes. The core difficulty is combinatorial: checking whether two circuits compute the same function (combinational equivalence checking) is co-NP-complete, which in practice means the work can grow exponentially with circuit size, and modern designs are large enough that a naive check simply never finishes.

These three problems — variation, in-operation faults, and pre-fabrication verification — look unrelated, and the research communities that attack them (test engineers, computer architects, formal-methods theorists) barely overlap. But they are three faces of one underlying fact: at atomic scale, computation is no longer deterministic. The silicon lies. It gives wrong answers, silently, at rates that grow with every process generation and with every additional chip in a data center. In a cluster of 100,000 GPUs, faults are not a rare event to be handled by a footnote — HPCA 2025's study of 4 million jobs across 150 million GPU-hours found failures frequent enough that mean-time-to-failure shrinks toward the runtime of a single large training job, so that the largest jobs are almost never left alone. The reliability theme exists because the machine we compute on has become a machine that must be continuously interrogated to find out whether it is telling the truth.

## Why the Obvious Solution Does Not Work

The obvious answer is redundancy: do everything twice and compare. Run the computation on two cores, and if they disagree, you have caught the fault. This is real and it is used — but it doubles your silicon, your power, and your cost, which at data-center scale is a catastrophe. When CGO 2025's Parallaft and DAC 2025's MEEK re-examine "run it twice," their entire contribution is fighting that 2x tax: Parallaft offloads the redundant re-execution to small, power-efficient cores and cuts energy 50% versus naive duplication; MEEK does the same in hardware with microsecond detection latency and "light design overhead." Nobody who understands the economics proposes plain doubling.

The deeper reason a single clean fix fails is that the three problems have incompatible shapes. Manufacturing variation is *static and per-device* — you want to catch it once, at test time, before shipping. In-operation faults are *dynamic and rare* — you cannot test for them in advance, you have to detect them live, and 99% of the transient faults that occur are harmless (they hit a value that gets overwritten anyway), so blanket protection wastes almost all of its effort. Design verification is *combinatorial and offline* — no amount of running the chip helps, because a bug that triggers one time in 2^40 inputs will never show up in testing but will absolutely show up across a billion shipped units. A technique tuned for one of these is nearly useless for the others. That is why the field is not one method but four largely separate research programs, each navigating its own version of the same tension: how much coverage can you buy, and how little can you pay for it?

## Subtheme 1: Proving the Design Correct Before It Exists

### What It Is
Design verification asks a yes/no question about a circuit that has not been built: does this RTL do what the specification says, on *every* possible input and state, not just the ones a test happens to try? The exhaustive approach is formal verification — encode the circuit and the property you want as a logical formula and ask a solver whether any assignment of inputs violates it. The workhorse is the Boolean satisfiability (SAT) solver: given a giant AND/OR/NOT formula, find an input that makes it true, or prove none exists. Equivalence checking — did my optimization change the circuit's behavior? — reduces to SAT by building a "miter": wire the two circuits' inputs together, XOR their outputs, and ask SAT whether that XOR can ever be 1. If it cannot, the circuits are identical for all inputs. Model checking extends this over time, asking whether a bad state is reachable across any sequence of clock cycles, which is where the "state explosion" problem bites: the number of reachable states can be exponential in the number of flip-flops.

### What the Research Community Is Doing
The dominant move is to attack the combinatorial blowup with parallelism, partitioning, and better proof bookkeeping. At DAC 2025, PDP-CEC partitions a datapath equivalence problem at critical circuit nodes into independent subproblems with dynamic load balancing, and reports 5.11x to 125.27x speedups by keeping one hard instance from stalling the whole check. Also at DAC 2025, a simulation-based combinational equivalence checker throws the problem onto a GPU — exhaustively simulating test patterns across thousands of GPU cores rather than reasoning with SAT — and hits 88x over a CPU SAT solver on large cases, falling back to the classic ABC tool only when brute simulation stalls. On the model-checking side, DAC 2025's property-driven parallel LTL checker partitions the nested fixpoint calculation across a 32-core machine for up to 17.19x, and "Leveraging Critical Proof Obligations" makes the IC3 algorithm smarter about *which* unreachability facts to prove first, since IC3 wastes most of its time chasing low-value proof obligations. Formal methods also reach beyond gate-level netlists: ASPLOS 2025's "Formalising CXL Cache Coherence" encoded the CXL.cache protocol as a state machine in the Isabelle proof assistant — 20 components, 68 transition rules — and mechanically proved the single-writer-multiple-reader property via a 796-conjunct inductive invariant and 53,332 auto-generated lemmas, which directly surfaced five real specification bugs that vendors accepted. ElasticMiter (ASPLOS 2025) does the analogous thing for the strange world of latency-insensitive dataflow circuits from high-level synthesis, where the usual notion of "same behavior" breaks because tokens can arrive in any order; it builds a handshake-aware miter to prove graph-rewrite optimizations sound. And the hardware itself is now being recruited: ISSCC 2025's SKADI is a 28nm ASIC K-SAT solver with SRAM-based clause macros that claims 100% solvability with a genuine unsatisfiability proof, turning the verification engine into silicon.

### The Tradeoff
Formal verification buys certainty — a proof covers all inputs, closing the gap that testing can never close. The cost is that it either does not scale (state explosion, co-NP-completeness) or requires enormous human effort to structure the proof: the CXL work is 211,000 lines of proof taking 3–5 hours to build, and someone had to design that 796-conjunct invariant by hand. Every technique in this subtheme is really a bet on which shortcut — partition, parallelize, prioritize proof obligations, or offload to a GPU or ASIC — recovers the most scale before the exponential wins. None of them makes the exponential go away.

## Subtheme 2: Catching the Fault While the Machine Runs

### What It Is
Some faults cannot be verified away or tested out because they happen during operation: a cosmic ray flips a bit, an aging transistor misses a timing deadline, a manufacturing defect in an arithmetic unit produces a wrong product for certain operand pairs. The most dangerous of these is the *silent* data corruption (SDC) — silent because the hardware raises no flag; the wrong answer simply flows downstream. Detection is a live problem: you must notice the error as computation proceeds, ideally cheaply, ideally without a second full copy of the machine. The physics behind it is now being measured directly. HPCA 2025's Veritas built a gem5-based fault injector modeling defects in the integer and floating-point units of five real x86 microarchitectures and combined it with fleet defect data to estimate actual SDC incident rates — the first characterization to look inside the arithmetic units rather than just memory arrays, because the field had learned that ECC-protected memory is no longer where the scary corruptions come from.

### What the Research Community Is Doing
The central strategy is *selective* redundancy — protect only what matters, because full duplication is unaffordable and most faults are benign. SC 2025's Protego duplicates only the instructions whose corruption would actually propagate to output, and crucially makes that choice input-aware, since the same program has different vulnerable instructions on different inputs. DAC 2025's GraphFI exploits the structure of graph workloads to cut the fault-injection search space by up to 100x, testing representative iterations and topologically-similar vertex communities rather than every fault site. For the specific and enormous case of large-model computation, the community has found that neural networks are *partially* self-protecting — but not as much as hoped. SC 2025's end-to-end LLM inference study is blunt: aggregate accuracy metrics make LLMs look resilient to random bit-flips, but text quality degrades semantically in ways those metrics hide, and sensitivity scales with task complexity. That finding motivates targeted schemes: SC 2025's FT-Transformer wraps the entire attention module as one fault-tolerant kernel with strided algorithm-based fault tolerance for the linear layers, avoiding inter-thread synchronization and beating operation-level protection by 7.56x at 13.9% overhead. DAC 2025's ReaLM goes further, characterizing the *statistical* fault tolerance of LLM inference via large-scale error injection and building error-detection circuits that only pay for recovery when the statistics say it matters, holding perplexity degradation to near zero at 1.42% area and 1.79% power overhead. At the architecture layer, DAC 2025's MEEK and FlexStep make redundant checking dynamic — FlexStep reconfigures which cores act as checkers per-task, ending the over-provisioning of static lockstep — while ASPLOS 2025's Hardware Sentinel detects a defective CPU purely from software-visible symptoms (segfaults, core dumps, log anomalies) correlated against hardware defect signals, achieving 1.74x the coverage of Fleetscanner without any dedicated test hardware. And ISSCC 2025's IBM zNext dynamically shrinks its voltage guard band — the safety margin of extra voltage held in reserve against droops, heat, and aging — by combining fast droop mitigation with robust error recovery, spending less power precisely because it can catch and recover from the errors that reduced margin would otherwise cause.

### The Tradeoff
Live detection buys coverage against faults no amount of pre-fabrication effort could remove. But every detector is silicon and energy that does no useful computation, and the tighter you tune it to "only what matters," the more you depend on your model of what matters being right — Protego's input-awareness, ReaLM's error statistics, FlexStep's per-task reliability classes are all bets that the workload behaves as characterized. When those bets are wrong, a fault slips through silently, which is the exact failure mode the whole subtheme exists to prevent.

## Subtheme 3: Surviving Faults at Fleet Scale

### What It Is
When a single machine cannot be made reliable enough, you make the *system* reliable by absorbing failures without losing work. This is the domain of checkpoint/restart and failure management, and its logic is purely statistical: if a job spans thousands of GPUs and each GPU fails occasionally, the job's mean-time-to-failure shrinks below its runtime, so the job *will* be interrupted, guaranteed, and the only question is how much progress you lose and how fast you recover. HPCA 2025's cluster study formalizes this with the Effective Training Time Ratio — the fraction of wall-clock time actually spent making progress — and shows how it collapses as GPU count grows unless mitigation improves. SC 2025's comparison of H100 and A100 fleets over 2.5 years and 11.7 million GPU-hours quantifies the trend the hard way: the newer H100 has 3.2x worse memory-error mean-time-between-errors than the A100, so each hardware generation can be *less* reliable per device even as it is faster, forcing 5% overprovisioning to hold availability at scale.

### What the Research Community Is Doing
The checkpointing work is a sustained assault on one tension: frequent checkpoints mean fast recovery but high overhead; infrequent checkpoints mean cheap operation but expensive restarts. ASPLOS 2025's PCcheck overlaps checkpoint I/O with ongoing training using copy-on-write snapshots so durable checkpoints can be taken often without proportional slowdown. SC 2025's LowDiff makes the sharp observation that distributed training already computes compressed gradients — so it reuses them *as* differential checkpoints, holding overhead under 3.1% while allowing per-iteration checkpointing. SC 2025's LLMTailor attacks checkpoint *size*, merging layers from different checkpoints into composite ones for 4.3x size reduction on Llama3.1-8B and 2.8x faster checkpointing on Qwen2.5-7B. The same idea reaches down to microcontrollers: ICCAD 2025's FLASC does asynchronous differential checkpointing for intermittently-powered devices that lose power unpredictably, cutting hardware cost to 49.1% of prior work while scaling 6.9x better with memory size. Beyond checkpointing, the fleet must diagnose and route around failures: SC 2025's automated failure management on the Aurora supercomputer cut mean-time-to-repair by up to 84x versus manual servicing through correlated event analysis and multi-strike repair policies; DAC 2025's MemSeer predicts DRAM failures before they cause downtime in mixed x86/ARM clusters, exploiting architecture-specific failure signatures for a 42.7% reduction in VM interruptions; and SC 2025's PACE uses lag-aware Granger causality on datacenter telemetry to trace a cascading failure back through cooling, power, and network subsystems to its physical root cause. Even the network layer joins in: SC 2025's SDR-RDMA rebuilds the RDMA reliability stack in software with a receive-buffer bitmap to handle planetary-scale link latencies that break traditional selective-repeat.

### The Tradeoff
System-level fault tolerance is the only answer that works when the components genuinely cannot be perfected — it converts unavoidable hardware failure into recoverable inefficiency. What it costs is storage, bandwidth, and duplicated state, and it is fundamentally reactive: it does not prevent the fault, it pays after the fact to survive it. The frontier is shrinking that payment — LowDiff's 3.1%, PCcheck's overlap — but the payment can never reach zero, and as the H100 data shows, the underlying failure rate is moving the wrong way faster than device reliability is improving.

## Subtheme 4: Testing Silicon and Modeling Its Decay

### What It Is
Before a chip ships, it is tested against manufacturing defects; while it runs, its aging must be predicted so it can be retired before it fails. Testing means generating input patterns — Automatic Test Pattern Generation, ATPG — that expose a defect by making a faulty gate produce a different output than a good one. Classically this uses the "stuck-at" model (a wire jammed at 0 or 1), solved by SAT. The problem is scale and realism: SAT-based ATPG assigns every input in a gate's fanin cone, inflating the pattern count until test time explodes, and the stuck-at model misses the failures that actually dominate at advanced nodes — *timing* failures, where a signal arrives slightly too late because process variation shifted many transistors' thresholds at once. Aging modeling is the temporal cousin: predict, from temperature and usage, when negative bias temperature instability or electromigration will push a device past its margin.

### What the Research Community Is Doing
The test-generation work is getting sharper about both compaction and realism. DAC 2025's PastATPG builds the first SAT solver supporting *partial* assignments — leaving inputs unspecified where they do not matter — to shrink test cubes and compact patterns, staying competitive with commercial tools. DAC 2025's invited work on timing marginalities argues for abandoning single-defect stuck-at models in favor of variation-aware testing that explicitly targets threshold-voltage variation affecting many devices together. DAC 2025's asymmetric SRAM aging test exploits a physical asymmetry — cells that store zeros age toward read-1 failures — to test read-0 and read-1 at different voltage aggressiveness, cutting over-testing (good chips wrongly failed) by 3–5x. Fault-injection campaigns, which stress a design against transient faults, are being made tractable: ICCAD 2025's Tenpura uses FPGA emulation with scan-based activity tracing to narrow the fault scope, discarding 88–96% of faults as provably harmless and turning months-to-years of injection into about a month. Aging modeling has turned to learned physics: ICCAD 2025's electromigration solver replaces finite-element solving of Korhonen's equation with a physics-informed message-passing neural network for 20x speedup at 0.5% error, and SC 2025's aging-aware supercomputer scheduler reconfigures node frequency to trade performance for lifetime, since aging is strongly temperature-dependent and extending GPU lifetime has both economic and environmental value. Memory-device decay gets device-specific fixes too: DAC 2025's DBC encodes DNN weights so that phase-change-memory conductance drift always pushes values toward less-error-prone cell levels, for a 55.18x drift-tolerance improvement, and ICCAD 2025's Securer tracks threshold-voltage drift in selector-only memory analytically to hold error rate below 10^-13.

### The Tradeoff
Better testing and aging models directly convert into yield and lifetime — every good chip wrongly scrapped is money lost, every bad chip shipped is a field failure, and the two errors trade against each other along the exact axis these papers tune. The cost is that the models must track physics that keeps changing: a stuck-at model that worked at 28nm misses the variation-driven timing failures at 3nm, so the test methodology has to be re-derived each node, and a drift model calibrated to one memory technology does not transfer to the next. Testing is a treadmill that speeds up with every process generation.

## How the Pieces Fit Together

The four subthemes are not independent — they hand faults to each other. What design verification fails to catch becomes a live fault that runtime detection must catch. What runtime detection cannot afford to catch becomes a corruption that fleet-scale checkpointing must survive. What manufacturing test lets slip becomes an early-life failure that aging models were supposed to predict. The reliability of the whole is a chain, and the research is really about where to place each defense so the total cost is minimized — because every defense is redundant work that produces no useful output, and the entire game is buying the most coverage per unit of wasted silicon, energy, and time.

Two forces are visibly reshaping every subtheme at once. The first is the collision with machine learning as a *workload*: LLMs are simultaneously the thing being protected and, it turns out, a partial protector of themselves. The corpus is full of work exploiting neural networks' statistical fault tolerance — ReaLM, FT-Transformer, DBC, PoP-ECC's tunable ECC for multi-bit upsets in DNN accelerators — because a workload that can absorb a small error rate lets you spend far less on protection than a workload that cannot. But SC 2025's inference study is the cold-water counterweight: that tolerance is real in aggregate and illusory in the details, so building on it is a bet that must be measured, not assumed. The second force is machine learning as a *tool* for reliability itself. GNNs now predict fault-propagation criticality for ISO 26262 automotive safety (ICCAD 2025's explainable-GNN framework, VLSID 2025's GCNII-based fault injection); XGBoost predicts IR-drop 88x faster than SPICE simulation; reinforcement learning generates in-field self-test libraries (DAC 2025's STL work, 57.57% fault-coverage improvement) and test stimuli; and a wave of LLM-assisted verification has arrived — LiK localizes Verilog bugs with 90.54% success beating GPT-o1 and Claude-3.5, UVM2 generates testbenches for a 38.82x setup-time reduction, COTIA uses LLM agents to steer concolic path exploration, and Wit-HW generates witness test cases for bug localization. The bet here is that the combinatorial walls of verification and test — the co-NP-complete cores no algorithm has beaten — can be *approximated* well enough by learned models to be practically useful, trading formal guarantees for tractable speed.

That trade defines where the dominant bets are placed. On the verification and test side, learned approximation and hardware acceleration (GPU equivalence checking, ASIC SAT solvers) are absorbing the combinatorial cost. On the runtime and fleet side, selective and statistical protection is absorbing the redundancy cost, increasingly by exploiting the fault tolerance of the ML workloads that now dominate the machines. And underneath both, a widening frontier — quantum error correction, where ISCA 2025's SWIPER borrows classical branch prediction to speculate across decoding windows for 40% latency reduction, ISCA 2025's synchronization policies cut logical error rate 3.4x, and HPCA 2025's movement-free atom-array architecture sidesteps the physical-qubit-shuffling bottleneck — shows the same reliability problem reappearing one abstraction level down, in machines whose *entire* purpose is to fight an error rate the physics hands them for free.

## What Remains Hard

- Silent data corruption in arithmetic units, not memory: ECC protects arrays, but the wrong-multiply defects Veritas measures inside integer and floating-point units have no cheap detector, and fleet-scale rates remain estimated rather than known.
- Verifying that a learned reliability tool is itself reliable: when a GNN predicts which faults are safety-critical for ISO 26262 or an LLM localizes an RTL bug, the approximation has no soundness guarantee, so it can silently miss the one case that matters.
- Quantifying real LLM fault tolerance beyond aggregate accuracy: SC 2025 shows accuracy hides semantic degradation, but there is no accepted metric that tells a system designer exactly how much bit-flip rate a given model-and-task can absorb before output quality fails.
- Making formal verification scale without hand-built invariants: the CXL proof needed a 796-conjunct invariant designed by an expert, and no method reliably synthesizes invariants of that complexity for arbitrary large protocols.
- Per-node re-derivation of test models: variation-driven timing failures make stuck-at ATPG obsolete at advanced nodes, and there is no test methodology that transfers across process generations without expensive recalibration.
- Closing the fleet reliability gap that is widening, not narrowing: with H100 memory errors 3.2x worse than A100, device-level reliability is degrading per generation faster than checkpointing overhead is falling, so effective training time will keep dropping at the largest scales.
- Reliable, low-latency quantum error correction at logical-qubit scale: decoders must keep pace with syndrome generation across heterogeneous codes and fabrication defects, and speculative and synchronization tricks buy factors of 2–3x against a requirement that grows with every added logical qubit.
