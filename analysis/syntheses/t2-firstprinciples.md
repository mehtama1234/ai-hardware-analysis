# Where a Neural Network Can Afford to Be Wrong

## The Problem

A large language model is, physically, a pile of numbers. A model with 70 billion parameters stores 70 billion of them, and if each number is a 32-bit floating-point value — the default precision most models are trained in — that pile weighs 280 gigabytes. To generate a single word of output, the machine must read essentially all of those numbers out of memory, multiply them against the running state of the sentence so far, and add up the results. Then it does that again for the next word, and the next.

This is the wall. The arithmetic itself is not the bottleneck: a modern GPU can perform hundreds of trillions of multiply-adds per second, and it has silicon to spare. The bottleneck is *moving the numbers*. An NVIDIA A100 has 40 to 80 gigabytes of high-bandwidth memory (HBM) and can stream roughly 2 terabytes per second out of it. A 280-gigabyte model does not even fit; it must be split across several GPUs, and even then, streaming 280 GB per generated token caps you at a handful of tokens per second. The compute units sit idle most of the time, starved, waiting for weights to arrive. One paper in this corpus (ISSCC 2025, "Slim-Llama") measures the cost directly: external memory access for a billion-parameter model runs 26 to 222 picojoules *per parameter*, and that energy — not the multiplication — dominates the power budget.

Quantization is the response to this physical fact. The idea is disarmingly simple: store each number in fewer bits. Instead of 32 bits per weight, use 8 (INT8), or 4 (INT4), or in the extreme, 2, or even 1. A 70-billion-parameter model in 4-bit integer form is about 35 GB — it fits on a single GPU, and streaming it per token is eight times cheaper. The compression is close to free in principle, because a trained neural network is enormously redundant: most weights are small, clustered near zero, and individually contribute almost nothing to any given output.

The catch is that "fewer bits" means "rounding." A 4-bit integer can represent only 16 distinct values. Forcing a smooth spread of real numbers onto 16 rungs introduces error at every single weight, and a neural network is a tower of matrix multiplications stacked 80 or more layers deep. A small rounding error in an early layer is multiplied by the next layer's weights, added to its neighbors, passed through a nonlinear function, and handed to the layer above — where it happens again. Errors that are individually invisible can compound into a systematic drift that, by the top of the tower, has changed which word the model chooses. The entire research area exists inside one question: **where in this tower can you be imprecise without the network noticing, and where must you spend bits to keep it from falling over?**

## Why the Obvious Solution Does Not Work

The obvious move is to round everything uniformly — pick a scale factor, divide every weight by it, snap to the nearest integer, done. This is called round-to-nearest, and for 8-bit precision it mostly works. Below 8 bits it collapses, and the reason is a phenomenon the corpus returns to again and again: **outliers**. In a trained transformer, the vast majority of values are small and well-behaved, but a tiny fraction — often in specific channels of the activations, the intermediate numbers computed between layers — are enormous, tens or hundreds of times larger than their neighbors. These rare giants set the scale. To represent a value of 200 and a value of 0.3 with the same 16 integer rungs, the rungs must be spaced about 13 apart, which means the 0.3 rounds to zero and every ordinary weight is destroyed. Keep the giants and you lose everyone else.

So there is no single clean fix, and that is why this field splits into distinct research programs rather than converging on one algorithm. You can hunt down the outliers and treat them separately. You can redesign the *number format* itself so it spends its bits more cleverly. You can decide, layer by layer, how many bits each part of the network actually needs. You can attack the specific tensors — the key-value cache, the activations, the weights — that dominate a specific workload. Each of these is a coherent line of attack with its own hardware consequences, and each, when pushed, creates new problems for the others. The rest of this essay walks through the four that the 2025 corpus is most invested in.

## Subtheme 1: Taming the Outliers

### What It Is

An activation outlier is a single number, computed midway through the network, that is far larger in magnitude than the others in its group. It matters because quantization error is set by the largest value in whatever block of numbers shares a scale factor: one giant forces coarse spacing on everyone. The whole outlier program is about *localizing* these giants so they do not poison their neighbors — either by storing them separately at higher precision, or by mathematically transforming the tensor so the giants shrink and the small values grow, spreading the difficulty around until every value fits comfortably in low precision.

The reason this is subtle is that the outliers are not noise — they carry real information, often the most important information in the layer. You cannot simply clip them away. You have to *keep* them, precisely, while still quantizing the 99% of ordinary values aggressively enough to win the memory savings.

### What the Research Community Is Doing

At ASPLOS 2025, **COMET** made 4-bit activations (the "A4" in W4A4KV4) practical by identifying outlier activations and retaining them at 8 bits in a fine-grained mixed-precision format, while the bulk of activations drop to 4-bit — reaching a 2.02x end-to-end throughput gain over TensorRT-LLM on an A100 serving LLaMA-3-70B, with negligible accuracy loss. At HPCA 2025, **Panacea** attacked a hardware-level consequence of outlier handling: asymmetric quantization (needed because activation distributions are lopsided) produces bit patterns that a "bit-slice" accelerator cannot skip the way it skips zeros, so Panacea's AQS-GEMM compresses and skips those patterns, yielding 1.88x throughput and 1.97x energy efficiency over the prior Sibia accelerator on OPT-2.7B. At DAC 2025, **DuoQ** took the opposite philosophy: rather than store outliers separately, it learns an equivalent mathematical transformation that *eliminates* them, enabling plain per-tensor 4-bit quantization and up to 23.45x energy efficiency on FPGA. And **OutlierCIM** (DAC 2025) confronted outliers inside compute-in-memory arrays, where a large value cannot simply be flagged because the computation happens in the memory itself; it clusters outliers into dedicated tiles and reaches 4.54x energy efficiency at 28nm.

### The Tradeoff

Every outlier scheme buys accuracy at low bit-width by adding irregularity, and irregularity is expensive in hardware. Storing giants separately means two data paths, two memory layouts, and logic to route each value to the right one — Oaken (below) notes that naive outlier storage costs 23 bits per outlier before optimization. Transforming the tensor to erase outliers avoids that hardware cost but pushes the work into a calibration or training step and risks the transform not generalizing to inputs it was not tuned on. The field is split between "handle the giants" and "make the giants go away," and neither is free.

## Subtheme 2: Redesigning the Number Format

### What It Is

An integer with a fixed scale spaces its representable values evenly, like a ruler. But the values in a neural network are not spread evenly — they cluster densely near zero and thin out toward the extremes. A format that matches that shape wastes fewer bits. This is the insight behind an entire zoo of number formats: floating-point (which spaces values logarithmically, dense near zero), block floating-point (a group of numbers sharing one exponent), microscaling or MX (small blocks, typically 32 elements, each with a shared scale), and more exotic schemes like posits, logarithmic number systems, and vector-scaled quantization. The design question is: for a fixed budget of bits, which arrangement of representable values minimizes the total rounding error across a real network — and can you build hardware that computes on it without paying to convert back to normal floats first?

### What the Research Community Is Doing

The scale of this search is captured by a DAC 2025 study, "Finding the Pareto Frontier of Low-Precision Data Formats," which swept **over 35,000 multiply-accumulate designs** across INT, FP, posit, LNS, MX, and VSQ formats, scoring each on area, energy (TOPS/W), and quantization noise — and found the emerging block formats delivering 1.8–2.2x the energy efficiency of plain floating-point. The dominant format story of 2025 is microscaling. At MICRO 2025, **MX+** noticed that in an MXFP4 block the single largest element (the "Block Max") is encoded with only one mantissa bit, so it corrupts the whole 32-element block; MX+ repurposes that element's now-redundant exponent field as extra mantissa bits, cutting Llama-3.1-8B perplexity from 27.69 down to about 9.5 on WikiText-2 with a 0.25-bit-per-element storage overhead and a 0.38% slowdown. At DAC 2025, **BBAL** introduced "bidirectional" block floating-point, which stops forcing every value to align to the block's maximum exponent (the flaw that loses small and moderate values), improving accuracy 22% over prior outlier-aware accelerators. Others make the format *adaptive*: HPCA 2025's **Anda** gives each group of activations a shared exponent and a dynamically allocated number of mantissa bits, tuned per module by a precision search, for 2.4x speedup and 3.1x energy efficiency; **M-ANT** (HPCA 2025) pushes adaptivity down to individual quantization groups within a tensor, reaching 2.99x average speedup; and **BitMoD** (HPCA 2025) assigns a *different data type* to every 128-weight group and processes them all through one bit-serial datapath, beating the ANT and OliVe accelerators by 1.69x and 1.48x.

### The Tradeoff

A cleverer format lowers quantization error for the same bit count, but it fractures the hardware. GPU tensor cores understand INT8, FP16, and a small menu of standard formats; a posit or an adaptive per-group type needs custom silicon, and that silicon must decode the format on the fly without eating the energy savings the format was supposed to deliver. The recurring theme in these papers — "decoding-computation fusion" (M-ANT), "unified representation" (BitMoD), the drop-in framing of MX — is the constant fight to keep a smart format from becoming a slow format. And the more formats a chip must support, the larger and less efficient it grows, which is exactly the cost that pure INT8 avoids.

## Subtheme 3: Spending Bits Where They Matter — Mixed Precision and Extreme Low-Bit

### What It Is

Not every layer of a network is equally sensitive to rounding. Some layers can tolerate 2-bit weights with no measurable effect; others fall apart below 8 bits. Mixed-precision quantization assigns a bit-width per layer (or per group, or per channel) to spend the bit budget where it does the most good. The hard part is that the sensitivities are *coupled*: rounding layer 10 harder changes what layer 11 sees, so you cannot score each layer in isolation. At the far end of this axis lies extreme quantization — 1-bit (binary) or ternary (−1, 0, +1) weights — where a multiply degenerates into an add or a sign flip, and the memory cost nearly vanishes, at the price of a training regime that must teach the network to survive with almost no numerical resolution at all.

### What the Research Community Is Doing

On the assignment problem, DAC 2025's **CLADO** rejects the usual assumption that layer errors are independent: it learns the pairwise cross-layer error interactions from a small data sample and solves an integer quadratic program for the bit-widths, setting a new accuracy frontier on ImageNet. ICCAD 2025's **MiCo** and DAC 2025's **BLOOM** both operationalize the "outliers at INT8, everyone else at INT4" split at the hardware level — BLOOM interleaving the bit-slices so the high-precision components expose skippable sparsity, for 1.2–4.0x speedup. At the extreme end, **Slim-Llama** (ISSCC 2025) runs a billion-parameter Llama with binary and ternary weights at **9 picojoules per parameter and 4.69 milliwatts total** — a two-order-of-magnitude drop in the external-memory energy that dominates LLM inference. **COBRA** (ICCAD 2025) builds a fully binary transformer on FPGA with a 1-bit multiplier, claiming 311x energy efficiency over a GPU, and **XShift** (DAC 2025) replaces multiplications entirely with XNOR-and-shift operations, cutting DSP usage 10–15x. A distinct and important variant comes from MLSys 2025's **MiLo**, which quantizes Mixture-of-Experts models to 3-bit but then adds tiny low-rank "compensators" — small full-precision correction matrices whose rank is chosen per layer from the weight statistics — recovering 87%+ of accuracy at 22% compression and, notably, delivering measured speedup at batch sizes above one, which most INT3 kernels fail to do.

### The Tradeoff

Mixed precision extracts the most accuracy per bit, but it turns a uniform, regular computation into a heterogeneous one, and hardware hates heterogeneity: a systolic array wants every cell doing the same operation. The papers that succeed (Precon, PISA, BitMoD) all pay for reconfigurable or bit-serial datapaths that can switch precision cheaply — silicon overhead that a fixed-precision design avoids. Extreme low-bit escapes this by being uniform again (everything is 1-bit), but it buys that regularity with a brutal accuracy cliff that, so far, only holds up for specific model families and requires quantization-aware *training* rather than a cheap post-hoc conversion — which is the subject of the next subtheme.

## Subtheme 4: When to Quantize — Post-Training, Training-Aware, and the KV Cache

### What It Is

There are two moments at which you can quantize. **Post-training quantization (PTQ)** takes an already-trained full-precision model and rounds it, using at most a small calibration set to pick scales — cheap, fast, no gradients, but limited in how far down it can push before accuracy breaks. **Quantization-aware training (QAT)** simulates the rounding *during* training, so the network learns weights that are robust to it — far more accurate at low bit-width, but it requires the full training apparatus and the model's memory footprint balloons because the optimizer must hold gradients and simulated-quantized copies simultaneously. A separate and increasingly dominant target is the **key-value (KV) cache**: to generate each new token, an attention model must compare it against every previous token, and to avoid recomputing them it caches a key and value vector for each. That cache grows linearly with context length and, at long contexts and large batches, *exceeds the model weights themselves* in memory and bandwidth cost — making it the single most valuable thing to quantize.

### What the Research Community Is Doing

The 2025 flagship for training-time compression is HPCA 2025's **eDKM**, which makes differentiable weight-clustering — normally too memory-hungry for billion-parameter models — feasible by deduplicating identical tensors and sharding the backward pass, cutting per-layer train-time memory 130x and shrinking a LLaMA-7B from 12.6 GB to 2.5 GB at 3 bits per weight. For the fine-tuning case, MLSys 2025's **HyC-LoRA** observed that during LoRA fine-tuning the *buffered activations* needed for backpropagation — not the weights — dominate memory at 69.7% in Llama-2-7B, and it calibrates which channels of hard-to-compress operators (RMSNorm, RoPE) hold outliers, keeping those in FP16 and crushing the rest to INT2. On the KV cache, ISCA 2025's **Oaken** splits the online/offline dilemma cleanly: it profiles per-layer outlier thresholds *offline* over ~100 inferences, then at runtime partitions each token's KV vector into outlier and non-outlier groups without any expensive online sorting, reaching 1.79x throughput over vLLM at batch size 256 with 0.54% accuracy loss and up to 70% KV bit-width reduction. DAC 2025's **MILLION** applies *product* quantization to the KV cache — encoding vectors by indices into a learned codebook rather than by rounding each element — with an asynchronous pipeline that hides dequantization, for 2.09x speedup at 32K context. And the highest-integration result, MLSys 2025's **QServe**, co-designs the whole stack as W4A8KV4: it uses "progressive group quantization" with a protective numeric range so INT4 weights can feed INT8 tensor-core GEMMs without overflow, and folds attention-key scales into weights via a RoPE commutativity trick, delivering 2.4x Qwen1.5-72B throughput on an A100 and — strikingly — higher throughput on a cheaper L40S than FP16 achieves on an A100.

### The Tradeoff

PTQ is nearly free but hits an accuracy floor; QAT breaks through the floor but costs a training run and the memory to do it, which the eDKM and HyC-LoRA papers spend their whole effort clawing back. KV-cache quantization is the highest-leverage target because the cache is where long-context inference actually spends its bandwidth — but the KV vectors are generated *at runtime*, so unlike static weights they cannot be quantized offline, forcing every scheme to solve outlier handling *online* without stalling generation. Oaken's offline-threshold trick and MILLION's asynchronous pipeline are two different answers to the same tight constraint: you must quantize a distribution you have not yet seen, fast enough not to lose the speedup you came for.

## How the Pieces Fit Together

The four subthemes are not parallel tracks; they are four faces of one optimization, and pushing on any face deforms the others. The whole enterprise is governed by a single accounting identity: **error is set by dynamic range, and dynamic range is set by outliers.** Outlier handling (Subtheme 1) attacks that range directly by isolating or erasing the giants. Format design (Subtheme 2) attacks it by arranging representable values to match the network's real distribution, so the same range costs fewer bits. Mixed precision (Subtheme 3) attacks it by refusing to pay for range in the layers that do not have it. And the timing choice (Subtheme 4) determines how much error you can *train away* before it ever reaches inference. Every paper in the corpus is choosing a point in this shared space, and the most complete systems — QServe's W4A8KV4, COMET's W4A4KV4 — visibly combine all four: a redesigned progressive format, per-tensor outlier absorption, a mixed weight/activation/cache bit-budget, and a training-informed calibration.

The dominant strategy that emerges is **algorithm-hardware co-design**, and it is a direct consequence of the tradeoffs. Nearly every accuracy win in this corpus comes from irregularity — outliers stored apart, formats that vary per group, bit-widths that vary per layer — and irregularity is worthless if the hardware cannot execute it without stalling. So the papers arrive in matched pairs: a quantization scheme and the silicon that runs it natively. The single most common technical obsession across venues is *avoiding dequantization* — the wasted work of expanding a compressed number back to full precision before computing on it. This is why look-up-table accelerators recur so insistently (LUT Tensor Core at ISCA 2025 reaching >20x the compute density of an FP16 tensor core at the same 28nm node; FIGLUT, LUT-DLA, VQ-LLM): a LUT replaces the multiply entirely by pre-computing every possible product, turning arithmetic into memory lookup — which is precisely the right move when your problem was memory-bound to begin with.

Where are the bets being placed? The center of mass in 2025 has moved decisively toward **4-bit weights with 8-bit activations and 4-bit KV cache** as the pragmatic sweet spot for serving (QServe, COMET, LiquidGEMM's W4A8 kernel), toward **microscaling formats** as the emerging standard for hardware-friendly sub-8-bit (MX+, MXBLAS, and the DAC Pareto study), and toward **the KV cache as the highest-value frontier** now that context lengths of 32K–128K make the cache, not the weights, the bandwidth bottleneck. Extreme 1–2-bit quantization remains a specialist bet, spectacular on power (Slim-Llama's 4.69 mW) but confined to edge deployment where the accuracy cliff is tolerable. This same struggle is not confined to machine learning: the scientific-computing community at SC 2025 is fighting the identical memory wall with the identical weapon — HPG-MxP mixing double and single precision on an exascale machine for 1.6x, INT8 matrix engines *emulating* higher-precision GEMM for 3x on SGEMM, and careful studies of whether emerging 8-bit floats (OFP8, posit, takum) can hold up under iterative eigenvalue solvers. The physics is universal: moving bits costs more than computing on them, so use fewer bits wherever the answer survives.

## What Remains Hard

- **Activation quantization below 4 bits is still unsolved for general models** — weights compress to 2 bits routinely, but activations, generated at runtime and riddled with input-dependent outliers, resist anything below 4-bit without model-specific tricks; a general W4A4 that holds across model families and tasks does not yet exist.
- **KV-cache outliers must be handled online**, on a distribution the system has not yet observed, fast enough not to erase the speedup — Oaken's offline thresholds and MILLION's async pipeline are partial answers, but a low-overhead, fully-online, accuracy-preserving KV quantizer for arbitrary inputs is open.
- **Cross-layer error coupling has no tractable exact solution** — CLADO's pairwise approximation captures two-layer interactions, but true sensitivity is many-body across 80+ layers, and no one can yet assign globally optimal per-layer bit-widths without exhaustive search or crude independence assumptions.
- **Mixed-precision and adaptive formats fracture the hardware** — every per-group data type or per-layer bit-width demands reconfigurable or bit-serial silicon whose control overhead can swallow the savings; the design point where format flexibility stops paying for itself is not characterized.
- **Extreme (1–2 bit) quantization requires quantization-aware training and still falls off a cliff** — it works for specific architectures at the edge but has no PTQ path and no reliable recipe for large generative models; why some models survive ternarization and others shatter is not understood.
- **Emerging architectures break the assumptions** — Mixture-of-Experts models degrade sharply at INT3 (MiLo needs low-rank compensators to recover), diffusion models have per-timestep, per-channel sensitivity that static schemes cannot capture (SQ-DM, Ditto, DM-Tune), and state-space and other post-transformer models are almost entirely unstudied for quantization.
- **There is no portable, format-agnostic software stack** — MXBLAS and the SYCL portability study (SC 2025) show that every new number format currently means new hand-tuned kernels per vendor; the field lacks a compiler abstraction that lets a quantization scheme target INT4, MXFP4, and a custom LUT format without rewriting the kernel three times.
