# The Memory Wall and How to Climb It

## The Problem

Consider what it takes to multiply two numbers. Not the arithmetic — that is trivial, a fraction of a nanosecond on any modern chip. Consider getting the two numbers to the place where the multiply happens. Each number lives somewhere in memory, as a pattern of charge in a capacitor or the resistance of a transistor. To multiply them, a wire must carry that pattern from the memory cell to an arithmetic unit. The wire has length. Charging it takes energy and time. And there is only a finite number of such wires leaving the memory.

This is the whole problem, stated physically. An NVIDIA H100 GPU can perform 1,979 trillion 16-bit floating-point multiply-accumulate operations per second. But it draws its data from HBM — High Bandwidth Memory, a stack of DRAM dies bonded on top of the compute die — at 3.35 trillion bytes per second. A single 16-bit multiply-accumulate consumes two inputs, four bytes. So the memory can deliver at most 3.35 trillion / 4 = 838 billion operands' worth of work per second. The arithmetic units can consume more than twice that. The chip starves. Before it ever touches 43% of its rated compute, it runs out of things to compute *with*. This gap — arithmetic that outpaces the wires feeding it — is the memory wall, and it is the organizing constraint of nearly every paper in this corpus.

The wall is not a fixed height; it depends brutally on the workload. The relevant number is *arithmetic intensity*: how many arithmetic operations you do per byte you fetch. Multiply two large matrices together and each number you load gets reused across an entire row of outputs — high intensity, the chip runs near peak. But run the *decode* phase of a large language model, where the network generates one word at a time, and the intensity collapses. To produce a single next word, the model must read every parameter of the network exactly once and multiply each by one number. There is no reuse. Worse, it must also read the *KV cache* — a stored summary of every previous word in the conversation, one entry per word per layer per attention head — and compare the new word against all of them. For a 70-billion-parameter model at a 32,000-word context, that is over a hundred gigabytes of memory read to produce one word, with almost no arithmetic per byte. On this workload the H100 runs at roughly 20% of its rated compute, sometimes far less. One paper in this corpus measures exactly 21% compute utilization on Llama2-70B decoding. The expensive silicon sits idle, waiting for wires.

Two more facts sharpen the constraint. First, moving a byte costs far more energy than computing on it: reading an operand from off-chip DRAM burns on the order of a hundred times the energy of the multiply it feeds. So the memory wall is simultaneously an energy wall — data centers are power-limited, and most of the power goes to moving data, not transforming it. Second, capacity has its own wall. A 70-billion-parameter model in 16-bit precision is 140 gigabytes; an H100 holds 80. The model does not fit. You either split it across many GPUs (paying for interconnect and idle silicon) or you stream it from slower, larger memory (paying in bandwidth). Bandwidth, energy, and capacity form a three-way vise, and this research theme is the sustained effort to loosen it.

## Why the Obvious Solution Does Not Work

The obvious answer is: make the memory faster. Add more wires. Widen the bus. This is exactly what the memory industry does, and it is not enough. The papers here show the frontier being pushed hard — a 42.5 Gb/s-per-pin GDDR7 DRAM at ISSCC 2025, an LPDDR5X chip crossing 12.7 Gb/s per pin with AC-coupled equalization to survive lossy channels — but each generation buys single-digit multiples while model sizes grow by orders of magnitude. The reason the wire count cannot simply grow is physical. Every wire off a memory chip needs a pin, a solder ball, a trace on the board. Chip perimeter is fixed by geometry, and signal integrity degrades as you push more bits per second down each wire. HBM already cheats by going vertical — stacking DRAM dies and connecting them with thousands of through-silicon vias straight down — but stacking traps heat: an ICCAD 2025 paper shows that adding HBM layers creates inter-die junctions with poor heat conductivity, forming hotspots that trigger *thermal throttling*, so the memory slows itself down to avoid cooking. You cannot win the bandwidth war by pushing bytes faster across a fixed boundary.

So the field splits along a deeper insight: if you cannot bring the data to the compute fast enough, move the compute to the data. And if you cannot do that cleanly, at least manage which data lives where, so the scarce fast bandwidth is spent only on bytes that matter. These two strategies — collapsing the memory-compute distance, and intelligently tiering the memory that remains — generate the four subthemes below. They are not alternatives; papers routinely combine them, and each creates new constraints for the others.

## Subtheme 1: Processing-in-Memory — Doing the Arithmetic Where the Data Lives

### What It Is

A DRAM chip is not a monolithic pool of bits. Internally it is a grid of *banks*, each bank a grid of *subarrays*, each subarray a matrix of cells whose rows are read out onto *sense amplifiers* thousands of bits wide. The bandwidth *inside* the chip — across those internal sense amplifiers — is enormous, far larger than the trickle that escapes through the external pins. Processing-in-memory (PIM) exploits this: bolt small arithmetic units directly onto the banks, and the data never has to leave the chip at all. The multiply happens millimeters from the cell instead of centimeters away across a starved bus. One paper measures the aggregate internal bandwidth of a rack of PIM-enabled GDDR6 chips at 512 terabytes per second — over a hundred times what those same chips could export through their pins.

The natural fit is exactly the workload that hurts most: matrix-vector multiplication, the operation at the heart of LLM decoding, where each weight is read once and touched once. There is no reuse to exploit, so the only lever is to stop paying the transport cost. PIM does precisely that. The catch is that a bank is not a general processor — it can host a small multiply-accumulate tree, maybe a lookup table, but not a flexible pipeline. So PIM is a scalpel, not a hammer: it wins overwhelmingly on the memory-bound kernels and does nothing for the compute-bound ones.

### What the Research Community Is Doing

The most aggressive statement of the strategy is CENT ("PIM Is All You Need") at ASPLOS 2025, which builds a GPU-*free* LLM inference system: 32 CXL-attached devices, each holding 16 GDDR6-PIM chips with near-bank multiply-accumulate trees, delivering 2.3x the end-to-end throughput of four A100 GPUs at 2.9x lower energy on Llama2-70B, and 4.6x lower latency at batch size one. At the finer grain, AttenPIM (DAC 2025) recognizes that attention has *two* distinct matrix-vector shapes — scoring the new token against past keys, then blending past values — and gives each its own PIM mode, winning 1.13x–5.26x over uniform PIM baselines. PIMPAL (DAC 2025) replaces arithmetic entirely with in-DRAM lookup tables for edge devices, cutting area 40% while running 17.8x faster than prior lookup-based PIM. And CGO 2025's PIM-LLM shows the software side of the problem: a naive PIM deployment is throttled by the cost of shuttling operands from host to PIM, but a PIM-tuned tiled matrix-multiply library reaches 45.75x over the TVM baseline once that transfer is minimized.

Scaling PIM past a single die is its own research front. McPAL (DAC 2025) spreads unstructured sparse LLM weights across multiple HBM-PIM chiplets, routing the non-skipped multiplies through an in-memory butterfly network, reaching 1.57x–3.12x over an A100 at 10–36x the energy efficiency. SplitSync (DAC 2025) attacks a subtler cost: in bank-parallel PIM, every bank must first *activate* a row before it can compute, and that activation can take longer than the compute itself — so it lets bank *groups* run asynchronously to hide activation latency while staying synchronous inside a group to avoid extra accumulators, for 1.70x over conventional PIM. Even the instruction set gets attention: a DAC 2025 paper adds register-based addressing modes to in-DRAM PIM, cutting the control overhead of describing memory access patterns for 1.94x.

### The Tradeoff

PIM trades generality for bandwidth. The arithmetic units it can fit near a bank are primitive, and the surrounding logic must not disturb the delicate analog timing of the DRAM array — WISEDRAM (DAC 2025) spends its whole design budget preserving standard DRAM read reliability while adding bitwise compute. Because PIM only helps memory-bound kernels, a PIM-only machine is lopsided: give it a compute-heavy phase (the *prefill* that ingests a long prompt) and it stalls exactly where a GPU would fly. This is why the pure-PIM systems remain research statements and why most of the corpus hedges toward heterogeneity, which is the next subtheme.

## Subtheme 2: Heterogeneous Compute — Splitting Work Between Memory and a Real Processor

### What It Is

If PIM is fast on memory-bound work and slow on compute-bound work, and a GPU is the reverse, the obvious architecture is to have both and route each piece of work to whichever engine suits it. The difficulty is that "memory-bound" versus "compute-bound" is not a fixed property of a kernel — it shifts at runtime with batch size, sequence length, and sampling strategy. A single attention operation can be bandwidth-limited for one request and compute-limited for another moments later. So heterogeneity is not just a hardware question (put a PIM unit next to a compute unit) but a scheduling question: decide, per operation, per instant, where it should run, and pay the cost of moving data across the boundary when you move it.

### What the Research Community Is Doing

PAPI (ASPLOS 2025) makes the scheduling dynamic: it profiles each LLM-decoding kernel at runtime and remaps it between PIM units and GPU-like accelerators as its memory-versus-compute character shifts, rather than committing to a static assignment that is wrong half the time. PAISE (HPCA 2025) takes the more surgical route on real AMD MI100 hardware, offloading only the memory-bound attention layers to HBM-PIM while the GPU keeps the compute-bound layers, and reorders the KV-cache layout to exploit bank-level parallelism — cutting attention-layer time up to 48.3%. For Mixture-of-Experts models, where over 95% of the parameters sit in "expert" sublayers that are read sparsely, PIMoE (DAC 2025) splits work between an NPU and PIM with *throttle-aware* offloading that reacts to real-time throttling signals, and adds a near-memory data condenser to translate sparse layouts across the NPU/PIM boundary, for 4.5x over an A100.

The boundary itself is being redrawn physically. Stratum (MICRO 2025) stacks monolithic-3D DRAM with near-memory-processing logic via copper-to-copper hybrid bonding on a logic die, then tiers expert weights *inside* the stack by access speed, hitting up to 8.29x decoding throughput over a GPU baseline for MoE serving. A DAC 2025 near-memory processor uses 3D DRAM-to-logic hybrid bonding with a *centralized* controller (rather than one controller per bank, which wastes area) to reconfigure between the matrix-vector shape of decode and the matrix-matrix shape of prefill, for 2.9x over an NPU. And AsyncDIMM (HPCA 2025) solves a plumbing problem that blocks all of this: when both the host CPU and a near-memory accelerator want the same memory bus, they contend and can corrupt DRAM; AsyncDIMM adds a offload-schedule-return protocol that lets them share an unmodified bus safely, for 1.19x–1.92x.

### The Tradeoff

Heterogeneity buys the best of both engines but pays in the seam between them. Every operation routed to PIM may need its operands re-laid-out first; every result may need to travel back. The scheduler that decides the routing is itself overhead, and a wrong decision is worse than no PIM at all. The physical integration — hybrid bonding, shared buses, near-memory controllers — is expensive and thermally constrained. The field is essentially betting that the seam can be made cheap enough that the win survives, and much of the cleverness in these papers is spent hiding the seam rather than doing the compute.

## Subtheme 3: Spending Bandwidth Only on What Matters — KV Cache, Tiering, and Sparsity

### What It Is

If you cannot widen the pipe, stop pushing junk through it. A large fraction of the memory traffic in modern inference is spent on data that barely affects the answer. In attention, most past tokens contribute almost nothing to the next word; in a memory hierarchy, most pages are cold; in a sparse weight matrix, most entries are zero. The strategy of this subtheme is to identify, cheaply and at runtime, which bytes are worth their bandwidth — and skip, evict, compress, or demote the rest. The hard part is that the identification must cost less than the traffic it saves, or the cure is worse than the disease.

### What the Research Community Is Doing

The KV cache is the richest target because it grows without bound as a conversation lengthens. VEDA (DAC 2025) uses a voting scheme to evict unimportant key-value entries, shrinking the cache while a dataflow-flexible accelerator handles the resulting irregular shapes — turning an O(N) problem into O(1) per step. Sella (DAC 2025) predicts, using cluster-based associative arrays, which key-value pairs a query will actually correlate with, then fetches only those — a 66% reduction in off-chip memory access with under 1% accuracy loss and no retraining. UniCAIM (DAC 2025) goes further and fuses a *content-addressable memory* (hardware that finds matching entries in one step) with compute-in-memory on FeFET devices, doing both static and dynamic KV pruning in place for 8.2x–831x energy-delay improvement. A DAC 2025 accelerator predicts query-key correlation with a cluster-based associative array to selectively access the cache at all.

At the system level, the same "spend bandwidth on hot data" logic drives CXL memory tiering. CXL (Compute Express Link) lets a machine attach large pools of DRAM over a serial link, but that memory sits 140–410 nanoseconds further away than local DRAM — a range measured directly by the Melody characterization framework at ASPLOS 2025 across four real CXL devices. HybridTier (ASPLOS 2025) tracks both long-term access frequency and short-term "momentum" to keep genuinely hot pages in fast local DRAM and demote the rest to CXL, using probabilistic counters to keep the tracking cheap — 2.0x–7.8x less metadata overhead than prior tiering. M5 (ASPLOS 2025) pushes the access tracking into hardware inside the CXL controller, counting every 4KB page *and* every 64-byte word so it can migrate at sub-page granularity and avoid mistaking a warm page for a hot one, for 14% over the best CPU-driven scheme. On the DNN-accelerator side, SoMa (HPCA 2025) observes that once on-chip buffers are large enough to hold a whole layer, the remaining bandwidth pressure comes from *when* you prefetch and store across fused layers, and it schedules that communication for 2.11x and 37% less energy.

### The Tradeoff

Skipping data is a bet that the skipped bytes did not matter, and the bet is probabilistic. Aggressive KV eviction can drop the one past token that turns out to be decisive; the papers guard this with accuracy budgets (under 1% loss) but the guard is workload-dependent and can fail on adversarial inputs. Tiering and prediction add tracking machinery — counters, associative arrays, hot/cold classifiers — that consume the very resources (SRAM, energy, latency) they are trying to save, so every design lives on a knife-edge where the metadata must stay an order of magnitude cheaper than the traffic. And sparsity makes memory access *irregular*, which fights the bandwidth-friendly sequential access patterns that DRAM is built for — several papers spend their hardware budget just re-regularizing the sparse traffic.

## Subtheme 4: Emerging Memory as a Compute Substrate

### What It Is

Everything above assumes the memory cell stores a bit and nothing more. But some device physics let the cell *compute*. In a resistive crossbar — a grid of wires with a programmable-resistance device (RRAM, PCM, or a magnetic tunnel junction) at each crossing — applying a voltage vector to the rows produces, by Ohm's law and Kirchhoff's current law, a current on each column equal to the dot product of the input voltages with the stored conductances. The entire matrix-vector multiply happens in one analog step, in the time it takes current to settle, with no data movement at all. This is compute-in-memory (CIM) in its purest form: the memory *is* the arithmetic unit. The prize is enormous energy efficiency — ISSCC 2025 macros in this corpus report 123.8 TOPS/W (YOCO, DAC 2025), 188.4 TOPS/W (a gain-cell CIM macro), and 60.81 TFLOPS/W for a diffusion-model accelerator — one to two orders of magnitude beyond digital GPUs.

The price is that analog is imprecise. The stored resistances drift and vary between devices; the summed current is corrupted by noise; and reading it back through an analog-to-digital converter is itself a major cost in area and energy. So the entire subtheme is a fight to get the efficiency of analog computation without letting its imprecision destroy the answer.

### What the Research Community Is Doing

Much of the work is device- and circuit-level robustness engineering. CREST-CiM (DAC 2025) cross-couples two magnetic tunnel junctions per cell to store the +1/-1 weights of a binary network with an 8100:1 current ratio, making the difference between states easy to sense despite process variation, at 7.9% area overhead and near-software ResNet-18 accuracy. Guarder (DAC 2025) uses 3-transistor-2-RRAM cells for reliable multiply-accumulate despite RRAM's stochastic programming, and doubles the reliability circuit as a DNN IP-protection key. BiNeuroRAM (DAC 2025) cuts RRAM read power 14.7x–58.2x with a low-power sense amplifier so it can run spiking networks accurately. ISSCC 2025 shows the technology reaching integration: a monolithic MRAM-embedded microprocessor runs *end-to-end* DNN inference with 1.1 Mb of co-located weights and no external weight fetch — the whole network lives and computes in the memory.

The other half of the work pushes the imprecision problem up to the algorithm. SAGE (ICCAD 2025) reorders the weight and input channels of an LLM by statistical kurtosis so that the salient, hard-to-quantize values do the least damage to the analog signal-to-noise ratio — training-free. YOCO (DAC 2025) mixes charge-domain multiply with time-domain accumulation and pairs RRAM with SRAM to trade density against precision, reaching 8-bit sub-petaops-per-watt. A DAC 2025 embedded-DRAM design ("333-eDRAM") stacks three different transistor materials — IGZO for retention, carbon nanotube for drive current, silicon for peripherals — to escape the single-material tradeoff between density, speed, and leakage, for 5.15x energy-area-delay improvement.

### The Tradeoff

CIM offers the best energy efficiency in the entire corpus and the worst precision guarantees. It excels at low-precision, error-tolerant workloads — binary and spiking networks, quantized inference — and struggles the moment full floating-point accuracy is required. The analog-to-digital converters that read the crossbar output can dominate area and energy, clawing back much of the promised win; several papers (OptiRange at ICCAD 2025) exist solely to optimize converter resolution. And the exotic devices — RRAM, FeFET, MRAM — are not yet manufacturable at the density and yield of standard DRAM, so CIM today lives mostly at the edge (always-on sensors, mobile) where its efficiency is decisive and its precision limits are tolerable, not in the datacenter where the memory wall bites hardest.

## How the Pieces Fit Together

Read across the four subthemes and a single strategic logic appears. The memory wall cannot be climbed by any one move, because each move trades one scarce resource for another. Widening the external bus hits pins and heat. Moving compute into memory (PIM/CIM) recovers the huge internal bandwidth but sacrifices generality and, for analog, precision. Skipping data recovers bandwidth without new hardware but bets on the data being skippable and pays in irregularity. Tiering recovers capacity at the cost of latency and tracking overhead. The field is not choosing among these — it is stacking them, and the interesting engineering is in the interactions.

Those interactions are mostly frictions. Push compute into memory and you inherit memory's constraints: DRAM PIM must respect row-activation timing (hence SplitSync), preserve read reliability (hence WISEDRAM), and share the bus with the host (hence AsyncDIMM). Adopt sparsity to save bandwidth and you destroy the sequential access that DRAM and PIM both want, forcing butterfly networks and data condensers to re-regularize traffic. Stack memory in 3D to shorten the compute-data distance and you concentrate heat, throttling the very bandwidth you built the stack to gain. Each solution to the memory wall erects a smaller wall behind it, and a large fraction of these papers are really about the second wall.

Where are the bets? The dominant one, visible across ASPLOS, DAC, HPCA, and MICRO, is *heterogeneous, disaggregated, tiered* systems glued together by CXL and 3D hybrid bonding: keep a flexible compute engine, surround it with PIM for the memory-bound kernels, pool capacity over CXL, and tier aggressively so the fast bandwidth is spent only on hot data. This is a datacenter bet, and it is where the LLM-serving papers cluster. The second bet is analog CIM at the edge, where the energy win is decisive and low precision is acceptable — this is where ISSCC's chip-level results live. The two bets share a foundation of device and packaging innovation (GDDR7, HBM thermal management, monolithic-3D DRAM, hybrid bonding, new transistor materials) that neither could exist without, and which quietly determines how high the wall is in the first place.

## What Remains Hard

- Analog compute-in-memory delivers 100x the energy efficiency but cannot yet guarantee floating-point accuracy; no one has shown a purely analog CIM system that trains or serves a full-precision transformer without accuracy-recovering digital scaffolding that erodes the efficiency win.
- KV-cache eviction and sparsity prediction bet that skipped tokens do not matter, but the accuracy guarantee is empirical and workload-dependent; there is no method that bounds the worst-case error of a pruned cache before running the input.
- 3D stacking shortens the compute-data distance but concentrates heat into un-coolable inter-die junctions; thermal throttling now caps achievable HBM bandwidth, and no packaging or scheduling approach reliably extracts the full stacked bandwidth without thermal collapse.
- PIM units are primitive by necessity and helpless on compute-bound phases like prompt prefill; a design that serves both prefill and decode near-optimally on the same silicon, without a second engine and an expensive seam between them, does not yet exist.
- CXL memory pools trade capacity for a 140–410 ns latency penalty, but tiering systems still misclassify warm pages as hot and pay for migrations that do not repay; sub-page hot/cold tracking that is both precise and nearly free remains open.
- Emerging non-volatile memories (RRAM, FeFET, MRAM) are individually promising but manufacturable only at densities and yields far below standard DRAM, so datacenter-scale CIM — where the memory wall is tallest — has no viable substrate today.
- The scheduler that routes each operation to PIM, CIM, or a compute engine is now a first-class bottleneck: deciding correctly at runtime, as a kernel shifts between memory-bound and compute-bound within milliseconds, without the decision itself costing more than the routing saves, is unsolved.
