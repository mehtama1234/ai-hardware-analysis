# One Resource Tension. Eleven Responses. 2,412 Papers.

## The Central Constraint

One ratio gives a useful starting point for many papers in this corpus. It is not a universal explanation, and it is not a benchmark score. It is the amount of arithmetic performed for each byte moved: operations per byte.

Call it **arithmetic intensity**. When a processor executes a computation, it moves data to the arithmetic units and performs arithmetic on that data. Intensity measures how much arithmetic the computation gets from each byte transferred. A matrix multiply with reused operands can have a high ratio. A streaming operation that reads each value once and uses it once has a ratio near one or below. The ratio is a property of the computation and the chosen memory boundary; changing the cache, reuse pattern, or precision can change it.

Intensity matters because a workload can be limited either by available arithmetic rate or by the rate at which required data crosses a chosen boundary. A simple roofline calculation illustrates the distinction. If a device sustains 1,979 trillion 16-bit operations per second and its HBM supplies 3.35 trillion bytes per second, the arithmetic rate divided by the bandwidth is about 590 operations per byte. Under those stated peak numbers, a workload below that ratio cannot use all arithmetic units while it waits for HBM. This is a bound for that device, precision, and boundary—not a claim that every workload reaches either peak or that every delay is caused by HBM.

Now consider one common inference phase. Generating one next token for a large dense model may require reading a large fraction of its weights, depending on batching, reuse, caching, and implementation. A 70-billion-parameter model stored at two bytes per parameter contains about 140 GB of weight data. If a single-token calculation streams that data once, its weight-only arithmetic intensity is low compared with the device threshold above. That calculation explains why weight movement often matters; it does not justify saying that all arithmetic units are idle, because batching, cache reuse, other model phases, and additional traffic change the actual balance.

The key-value (KV) cache adds another form of traffic. During generation, the model stores key and value vectors for earlier tokens and reads some of them to compare the new token with its context. Its size depends on layer count, head dimensions, precision, sharing scheme, and token count. A precise cache-size calculation must state those values; the cache is not automatically read in full by every implementation. Long-context papers in this corpus therefore study several distinct choices: compressing the cache, selecting which entries to read, moving it between memory tiers, changing the attention calculation, or scheduling requests so the traffic fits available capacity.

The recurring problem is therefore a mismatch between the work a design wants to perform and the rate at which its required state can move, arrive, or be trusted. Arithmetic intensity is one lens on that mismatch. Other papers begin with a different binding constraint: a deadline, a failure, an authorization rule, a physical error rate, a compiler limitation, or a manufacturing condition. The 2,412-paper corpus spans sixteen venues and several evidence levels; it should not be read as if every paper makes the same argument. The sections below connect the papers where their mechanisms share a constraint, then state the places where the analogy breaks.

## How to read the evidence

The paper count describes the collected corpus, not the number of claims this essay has independently checked. Read each statement at the strength of its source:

- A final paper or full extracted paper supports a detailed account of its mechanism, experiment, and stated limits, subject to the paper's own reporting.
- An official abstract or structured record supports the problem, high-level mechanism, and reported headline result. It usually does not support details about implementation, omitted costs, or every workload.
- A local review or theme map supports how the papers were grouped and what to read next. It is not a substitute for the primary paper.
- A teaching calculation is invented to make a dependency or failure case visible. Its numbers are not measurements from a named paper.

When this synthesis compares venues, it compares the questions and mechanisms visible in the reviewed material. It does not turn abstract coverage into a full proceedings review, and it does not make results from different devices, workloads, or measurement boundaries directly additive.

## The Eleven Responses

The research themes in this corpus are not eleven identical problems. They are eleven responses to related limits, operating at different layers of the stack—from the physics of a memory cell to the scheduler deciding which GPU job runs next. Some directly reduce data movement; others make movement predictable, preserve correctness, or make a design possible under power and manufacturing limits.

**Attention (T1)** confronts the KV-cache directly. The 207 papers in this theme use several mechanisms: compress the cache (TurboAttention quantizes keys and values to 2–4 bits; DeepSeek-V3's multi-head latent attention reports a smaller per-token footprint); keep only selected entries (ClusterKV groups tokens and recalls a bounded subset); reduce the amount read (SampleAttention reports lower time to first token at million-token context); or fuse stages so data does not cross a slow boundary twice (LeanAttention uses an associative softmax-rescaling operation to expose more parallel work). Other designs move the calculation nearer to the stored data, including SSD-based and memory-stacked proposals. Speculative decoding takes a different route: it proposes several tokens and verifies them together, so some cache and model work is shared across the accepted tokens. These reported speedups come from different systems and workloads; they are examples of mechanisms, not one common ranking.

**Quantization (T2)** reduces the number of bits carried by each value. If the operation and representation remain compatible, fewer bits mean fewer bytes to move and a higher arithmetic-intensity estimate. The saving is not free: the system must represent outliers, convert values, and show that the changed numbers still satisfy the application's quality rule. The 134 papers use mixed precision, transformations, and group formats to handle those cases. Their reported numbers belong to particular models, devices, and baselines; they should not be combined into one universal energy or speed claim.

**Memory architecture (T3)** asks whether some calculation can happen near the data rather than after the data has crossed a long connection. The papers use several physical locations: beside a memory bank, inside a storage device, in a CXL-attached module, or in an analog array. The possible internal bandwidth is only one part of the case. The design must also supply operands, handle precision and noise, move partial results, and support the required software interface. CENT's reported throughput and energy comparison, for example, belongs to its 32-device system, selected workload, and four-A100 baseline. Analog crossbars report high operation-per-watt figures under their own operation definition and measurement boundary; those figures are not directly comparable with a complete digital serving system.

**Interconnect (T4)** scales the data-movement problem from one chip to many. Training a large model often exchanges gradients among participating GPUs after each step. A simple estimate—200 GB over a 400 Gb/s link, before protocol and contention costs—takes about four seconds at the raw bit rate, not eight; the actual collective time also depends on topology, concurrency, and how many links carry the data. The 161 papers in this theme use several mechanisms: overlap communication with calculation, compress data before transfer, change the physical link, and choose shorter or less contended routes. A link rate is therefore only one input to a synchronization-time claim; the collective algorithm and shared paths must be named too.

**Sparsity (T5)** reduces arithmetic only when the hardware can identify and skip the omitted work cheaply. A lockstep vector unit may still spend cycles on zero values if the representation gives it no way to skip them. The papers use at least three approaches: place zeros in patterns the hardware recognizes, build units that handle irregular surviving values, or route each input to a subset of model components as in mixture-of-experts. Each approach trades arithmetic for metadata, routing, load balance, or quality risk. A reported reduction in calculated values is therefore not yet a speedup; the implementation must show the index work, movement, and resulting accepted output.

**Compilers (T6)** connect a mathematical operation to a legal physical schedule. The compiler decides, or helps search, where values live, when transfers occur, which operations overlap, and which hardware instruction implements each step. A claimed kernel time therefore needs its device, input shape, precision, setup cost, and measurement method. MLIR preserves information across compilation levels; auto-tuners measure candidate schedules; equality-saturation systems search among equivalent program forms. High-level synthesis adds a related problem for FPGAs: translating a program into a circuit whose storage, timing, and routing must all fit. The common principle is not that one compiler is faster; it is that a transformation is useful only when the generated execution preserves the required result and its complete cost is measured.

**Security (T7)** adds a different requirement to the same physical system. Memory accesses can leave observable traces in timing, power, or electromagnetic behavior. A cache-timing attack uses the delay difference between a hit and a miss; the exact difference depends on the processor and measurement setup. Isolation and memory encryption can reduce some exposure, but they add metadata checks, storage, and latency that must be included in the system cost. The papers in this theme therefore ask two separate questions: what information can an observer infer, and what overhead does the protection add? A larger cache, near-memory processor, or compute-in-memory array changes both the performance path and the set of observable resources; none is automatically secure or insecure without a threat model.

**Reliability (T8)** asks what happens when a component, value, or assumption is wrong. The answer depends on the failure model: a memory bit may be corrected, a worker may be restarted from a checkpoint, a circuit may be screened before shipment, or a design may be proved against a stated model. Error rates and fault counts must retain their hardware, environment, workload, and observation interval. ECC, checkpointing, formal verification, and test generation address different failure points; none by itself proves that a complete deployed system survives every failure.

**Specialized domains (T9)** change the hardware or representation when a general-purpose processor cannot meet the required cost, privacy, timing, or energy rule. FHE, quantum control, photonic links, and FPGA dataflow designs have different constraints and should not be treated as one category of solution. An FPGA trades fixed-function efficiency for post-fabrication reconfiguration; whether that trade wins depends on reconfiguration time, tool cost, workload lifetime, and measured device power. The FCCM papers show several low-precision and on-chip-storage designs for edge inference, but they do not establish that one FPGA configuration is best for all deployments.

**Systems software (T10—OSDI + USENIX ATC)** decides how hardware components are shared and connected during a real workload. A device's peak bandwidth is not the service's bandwidth if assignment, queueing, cache policy, or communication order prevents the workload from using it. The papers in these venues study those decisions: mapping work to links, placing data in tiers, scheduling requests, and recovering from failure. A software result therefore needs the workload, service target, baseline, and complete path from request to accepted answer. The common lesson is that a fast component can still be underused when the surrounding policy makes the wrong state or path available.

**Reconfigurable computing (T11—FCCM, integrated with T9 and T6)** trades some fixed-function efficiency for the ability to change the circuit after fabrication. That trade is worthwhile only when the workload lasts long enough to repay compilation, configuration, and tool costs, and when the resulting circuit meets timing, capacity, and quality rules. FCCM's edge-inference papers explore low-precision arithmetic, on-chip storage, and dataflow pipelines under particular power and model assumptions. They show why an FPGA can fit a deployment that cannot use a datacenter GPU; they do not establish a universal ASIC-to-GPU-to-FPGA ordering.

## The Tensions

The eleven responses create constraints for one another. Reducing one cost can add work, change the access pattern, or weaken a property that another layer must preserve.

Quantization reduces bytes per weight but can add conversion and irregularity. A 4-bit weight and an 8-bit activation need a defined multiplication path; if the hardware does not provide one, dequantization or custom instructions add work. The DAC format study and MICRO's MX+ work illustrate the same design rule from different angles: a number format cannot be judged by bit width alone. Its grouping, outliers, conversion path, compiler support, and output error all belong in the comparison.

Security countermeasures consume resources. Encryption and integrity checking may add metadata reads, computation, and latency; the exact cost depends on the protection scheme and access pattern. Near-memory processing can reduce one data path while placing computation outside an existing trust boundary. A performance result is incomplete if it omits those checks, and a security result is incomplete if it does not name who can observe or control the new location.

Sparsity and memory regularity conflict. A sparse KV-cache eviction strategy that retains only 10% of tokens makes memory access irregular — the surviving tokens are scattered across the cache — and DRAM is designed for sequential access, not scatter-gather. The engineering cost of re-regularizing that access can exceed the bandwidth saved.

The systems layer adds another boundary. Splitting prefill and decode can place each phase on better-suited hardware, but the intermediate state must cross the split and remain correctly associated with the request. CXL memory, RDMA, and PIM also expose latency, ordering, and ownership rules that older software may not model. The safe conclusion is narrower than “hardware arrives faster than software”: each new interface requires a matching placement, scheduling, and recovery policy, and its benefit depends on whether the added path costs less than the work it removes.

HLS adds a toolchain constraint: an FPGA can implement a workload-specific dataflow, but the generated circuit must still meet timing, storage, routing, and power limits. A comparison with hand-written HDL is meaningful only when both designs implement the same work under the same clock, resource, and measurement rules. Improving HLS therefore requires schedule inference, timing-aware transformations, and resource mapping—not only a faster source-to-bitstream step.

## What This Field Is Betting On

The 2025 corpus suggests several working assumptions. These are patterns in the reviewed papers, not forecasts or proof that the field has settled on one design.

For inference, many papers investigate a stack containing low-bit weights and activations, reduced or tiered KV state, separate treatment of prefill and decode, and compiler support for the resulting formats. The papers do not all use the same bit widths or acceptance rules. The common idea is to treat representation, movement, kernel schedule, and serving policy as one chain; a gain at one step can disappear at another.

For training, the reviewed work often assumes data and tensor parallelism, collective communication, and networks whose topology is part of the schedule. Gradient compression and optical links are two responses to the communication cost, but neither removes the need to measure synchronization, model quality, and failure recovery on the target cluster.

The emerging bet at the edge is **FPGAs running aggressively quantized transformer models**, serving the deployments where GPU power budgets are prohibitive. FCCM 2025 shows early convergence on INT4/FP8 FPGA inference pipelines; the question is whether HLS tools improve fast enough to make these deployments practical without HDL expertise.

One gap suggested by the corpus is **correctness-preserving systems software for heterogeneous hardware**. The venues contain work on scheduling, caching, communication, verification, and approximate computation, but fewer end-to-end contracts connect those pieces. That observation does not prove that no such work exists; it identifies a useful reading question: which output, deadline, privacy property, and recovery rule remain guaranteed after the representation and execution path change?

## The Open Problem

One question connects several of the responses:

**For a stated workload and acceptance rule, how can a system bound the effect of compressed or selectively read attention across the model, cache policy, scheduler, and hardware path?**

Average benchmark accuracy is not enough to answer that question. The MLSys compression study compares compression with an existing serving stack and reports cases where average gains disappear or individual samples degrade. Systems papers add another boundary: a policy that raises mean throughput may change the delay or cache contents of a particular request. Those observations motivate a stronger record of per-request quality, cache decisions, timing, and rejected or failed cases; they do not by themselves prove a universal failure of compression or scheduling.

Answering it would require at least three linked pieces: an error bound or test rule for the model transformation, a record of which cache entries and precision choices were used, and a serving contract that states what happens when the bound no longer applies. The contract could be exact equivalence, a task-level quality bound, a deadline bound, or a safe fallback; the application must choose. This makes the question useful across quantization, attention, compilers, and systems without pretending that one metric can certify every deployment.
