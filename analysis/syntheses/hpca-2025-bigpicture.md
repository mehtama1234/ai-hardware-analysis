# HPCA 2025 — Big Picture Essay

## What HPCA is

The International Symposium on High-Performance Computer Architecture is where systems designers bring the hard constraints of the physical world into dialogue with the demands of actual workloads. This venue exists because good chips are built at the intersection of what software needs and what silicon can deliver—and that intersection keeps moving. HPCA admits papers that ask: given this workload's behavior, what hardware choices unlock the next order of magnitude? What bottleneck, once solved, cascades improvements through the whole system? Researchers here care about things like: why does this workload touch memory in this particular pattern, and what architectural change makes that pattern pay? It's where computer architects meet fabrication reality, where theory meets power budgets, and where single-threaded performance die-hards coexist with distributed systems engineers optimizing for 100,000-node datacenters. The people who submit here are building accelerators for ML, redesigning CPU memory hierarchies, or rethinking what "processor" means when compute lives inside DRAM. They measure success in execution time, energy per operation, and dollars per year of production silicon.

## The one problem this community is organized around

The core problem is the gap between where your data lives and where you can process it—call it the **locality-bandwidth crisis**. Here is the raw physics: moving a byte of data from DRAM to a compute core costs orders of magnitude more time and energy than actually computing on it. A modern CPU can execute a multiply-accumulate in a few nanoseconds and consume single-digit picojoules; fetching that operand from main memory takes hundreds of nanoseconds and burns hundreds of picojoules. When you have a billion-parameter language model, a sparse neural network, or a terabyte-scale graph, the math is unforgiving: you will move data far more often than you will compute on it.

The second constraint is **that silicon area and power budgets are finite**. You cannot build a processor with enough on-chip storage to hold a working set. You cannot afford to put computation everywhere. Every transistor spent on caching, or prefetching, or routing data is a transistor you cannot spend on compute units. Every watt you burn moving data is a watt you cannot spend computing. Datacenters have power envelopes; mobile devices have battery constraints; edge systems have thermal limits.

The third constraint flows from the first two: **workloads are heterogeneous and irregular**. The memory access pattern for attention mechanisms in transformers looks nothing like the pattern for sparse matrix multiplication, which looks nothing like the pattern for graph traversal. A CPU cache hierarchy designed for one looks terrible for another. A scheduling algorithm that works well under low contention fails catastrophically under contention. The bottleneck shifts between memory bandwidth, compute throughput, and communication latency depending on the problem and its input size.

HPCA is organized around the question: how do you architect silicon to move data efficiently *enough* to computation that needs it, burn as little energy as possible in the process, and scale that design to workloads of radically different shapes? The answer is never "just add more cache." It is always a combination of: moving computation closer to data (near-memory processing), predicting what data you will need before you need it (prefetching, compiler-driven scheduling), compressing data to fit more into the same physical space (quantization, sparsity), and redesigning the dataflow so fewer bytes move per result (exploiting structure in the problem).

## The main approaches in 2025

### Memory System Redesign (61 papers)

The dominant approach is to rethink memory hierarchy from first principles for the workload at hand. Rather than building general-purpose caches, papers this year optimize for specific access patterns: DRAM-based caches with on-die tag comparison (eliminating redundant DRAM reads), adaptive prefetching that learns temporal correlations within application footprints, latency-hiding buffers that stage graph data without partitioning, and in-DRAM storage of KV-cache tensors for LLM attention. The core insight is that you get better results if you stop pretending memory is uniform and start building specialized memory systems for the problem. Papers like "FHENDI: A Near-DRAM Accelerator for Compiler-Generated Fully Homomorphic Encryption" build hierarchies with multiple compute levels inside DRAM itself, using bank-local permutation networks to route data without expensive inter-bank communication. Others like "PAISE: PIM-Accelerated Inference Scheduling Engine for Transformer-based LLM" partition operations between GPU and HBM-PIM to move attention computation where the data already sits. The thread is: stop moving data through generic hierarchies; instead, embed processing at the level of memory where the access pattern is hottest.

### Scheduling and Dataflow Optimization (54 + 40 papers)

Nearly half the conference is techniques for deciding *when* to move data and *how* to move it. Contention-aware scheduling (dynamically switching between eager and lazy execution of atomic instructions based on predicted cacheline contention) squeezes performance by exploiting the different performance slopes of contended vs. non-contended synchronization primitives. Compiler-driven scheduling maps DNN layers to DRAM communication patterns, fusing operations to reduce intermediate tensor sizes. Dataflow approaches like automatic spatial accelerator RTL generation from affine loop representations promise to make it cheaper to build custom hardware pipelines that minimize data motion. The common thread: the optimal way to execute is not fixed; it depends on the specific workload, the specific hardware, and the specific state of contention at runtime. A static policy leaves half the benefit on the table; you must adapt.

### Accelerator-Specific Designs: Quantization, Sparsity, and Custom Precision (11 + 13 papers)

Rather than execute models at full precision on commodity hardware, papers this year push on reducing the amount of data that must move and compute. "BitMoD: Bit-serial Mixture-of-Datatype LLM Acceleration" observes that not all weights of a model need the same precision, so per-group (128-weight) data-type adaptation can choose whether to use INT4, FP4, or NF4 for each group to minimize quantization error. The hardware then unpacks all types through a single bit-serial processing element, amortizing the cost of supporting heterogeneous formats. Joint bit-slice sparsity (exploiting structure in both activation and weight matrices simultaneously) and transposable structured sparsity (formats that reorganize how data is stored so matrix operations can skip zeros efficiently) multiply the benefits of compression: if weights are 4-bit and half-sparse, you move one quarter the data. Quantization combined with sparsity is the workhorse of this year's LLM inference papers.

### Near-Data and In-Memory Processing (24 papers)

When moving data is expensive, move computation instead. Near-memory processing (NDP) places simpler compute elements inside or adjacent to DRAM controllers; in-memory computing builds processing inside SRAM arrays or even DRAM subarrays. Papers this year push from toy examples toward end-to-end workloads: FHENDI uses conflict-free permutation networks within DRAM banks to execute FHE's element-wise and butterfly operations without leaving the memory subsystem. A programmable PIM-based MCMC accelerator with accept/reject prediction moves probability inference into the memory hierarchy. The challenge is that in-memory computing is awkward (arithmetic is hard with limited analog headroom, or consume huge area if fully digital), so these designs must laser-focus on the specific bottleneck operations that stay resident in memory and will genuinely save bandwidth by not moving.

### Heterogeneous Architecture and Co-Design (15 + 22 papers)

No single compute fabric wins on all workloads. Papers this year build systems with multiple kinds of processors (CPUs, GPUs, PIM, specialized arrays) and task their compilers and schedulers with deciding what runs where. "CogSys: Efficient and Scalable Neurosymbolic Cognition System via Algorithm-Hardware Co-Design" builds ASIC tiles with reconfigurable processing elements that switch between neural and symbolic compute, with a workload-aware scheduler choosing which kernel type runs on which tile. The pattern repeats: algorithm (factorization of symbolic reasoning), hardware (tiles with two compute modes), and system (scheduler making runtime decisions). Co-design extends to the whole stack: compilers like HElayers identify parallelism in FHE workloads that can be pipelined across die-to-die interconnects; memory systems are designed around compiler-observed access patterns; security properties flow from both hardware (e.g., memory-side channel protection via compute-based DHE) and software (compiler-driven isolation via speculative MPK permissions).

### Reliability, Security, and Physical Constraints (14 + 13 papers)

Rowhammer attacks remain a live problem in DRAM; this year's papers improve on-DRAM-die mitigation with concurrent counter updates and adaptive refresh strategies. Silent data corruptions in x86 arithmetic units get characterized at the microarchitecture level, enabling targeted error correction. Memory-side channels leak embedding table lookups; compute-based data home extension (DHE) masks the leaked information. Low-voltage compute-in-memory accrues MAC-level errors; run-time error correction maintains reliability. This is the price of pushing silicon to its limits: you cannot ignore the physical world. As we quantize weights, reduce voltage to save energy, and pack computation denser, reliability becomes an explicit design cost, not an afterthought.

## How it connects to the broader field

HPCA is the systems-level implementation venue where the rubber meets the road. Machine learning conferences (NeurIPS, ICML) propose algorithms and assume infinite compute; HPCA asks how to run those algorithms on real silicon. Compiler conferences (PLDI, CGO) optimize code; HPCA asks what architectural changes would make those compiler transformations unnecessary, or enable new ones. Interconnect and NoC researchers (NoCSys) propose new topologies; HPCA tests whether they actually improve end-to-end performance on real workloads or just move the bottleneck elsewhere. HPCA depends on transistor scaling trends from ISSCC and process technology papers, on novel materials and devices from ISQED, and on workload characterization from systems and AI conferences. In return, HPCA produces designs that drive datacenter procurement decisions, influence ISA extensions (e.g., ARM SVE, x86 AVX), and set the terms for what future accelerator designs must compete against. It is not a basic science venue; it is the venue where engineering and science meet under the constraints of power, area, latency, and accuracy.

## What's open

The single largest unsolved problem is **generalization**: every paper in this conference builds an accelerator (ASIC, GPU kernel, compiler pass, dataflow engine) optimized for a specific workload class or a narrow range of problem shapes. A quantization scheme optimal for 4-bit weight-only inference on transformers is wrong for sparse CNN inference on edge NPUs. A dataflow for GCN training does not scale to GNN recommendation engines. A scheduling policy that wins on contended cache coherence loses on latency-hidden prefetching. Researchers have not cracked the code on building a single piece of hardware that is simultaneously competitive on wildly different workloads without sacrificing significant performance on each. This is why datacenters must buy different hardware for different jobs. This is why edge inference requires custom chips. The field is still in the era of purpose-built systems, and generalizing has proven harder than expected—likely because generalization, in hardware, means being locally suboptimal everywhere.
