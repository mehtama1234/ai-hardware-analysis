# The Zeros You Cannot Skip

## The Problem

Start with a physical fact about how a processor multiplies. A modern GPU does not multiply one number at a time. It multiplies in lockstep — a block of, say, 128 numbers is fed through 128 arithmetic units in a single tick of the clock, all doing the same operation on different data. This is what makes the chip fast: the control logic that decides *what* to do is amortized across a wide slab of *data*. The unit that fetches the instruction, decodes it, and issues it costs the same whether one lane or all 128 lanes are busy. To get the advertised throughput — an A100 does roughly 300 trillion floating-point operations per second — you must keep those lanes full and regular.

Now consider what a trained neural network actually contains. A neural network is a stack of *layers*; each layer is, at its core, a matrix of *weights* — a large grid of numbers learned during training. To run the network, you multiply an input vector (the *activations*, the intermediate numerical values passed from one layer to the next) against this weight matrix. A 70-billion-parameter language model is 70 billion such weights. And here is the empirical scandal that this entire research theme is built on: most of those weights do not matter. You can set 90 to 95 percent of the weights in a trained ResNet to exactly zero, retrain briefly, and watch accuracy barely move. The network was carrying enormous dead mass. The information it needed lived in a small fraction of its connections.

So the temptation is obvious. If 95 percent of the weights are zero, and multiplying by zero is a wasted operation, then throw the zeros away and do one-twentieth of the work. Twenty times faster, twenty times less energy, twenty times less memory traffic. Free lunch.

The lunch is not free, and the reason is the lockstep. A zero weight still occupies a lane. When you multiply a dense 128-wide block and three of the values happen to be zero, the hardware still spends the full clock tick — it has no way to *not* process a lane. To actually skip a zero, you must first find it, encode where it was, gather the surviving nonzeros into a dense packed block, and route each one to the correct accumulator. That bookkeeping — the *metadata* describing which positions survived — costs memory bandwidth and control logic. When the surviving nonzeros are scattered unpredictably (this is called *unstructured* sparsity), the gathering is so irregular that the overhead often exceeds the compute you saved. A GPU running an unstructured 95-percent-sparse matrix multiply frequently runs *slower* than the dense version it replaced. Mathematically the work shrank by 20x; physically the machine got no faster.

This is the wall. Sparsity is abundant, provably present, and nearly free to create. Converting it into speed is where all the difficulty lives. The 107 papers in this theme are, almost without exception, attacks on the gap between *the zeros exist* and *the hardware runs faster because of it*.

## Why the Obvious Solution Does Not Work

The obvious engineering fix is to build hardware that handles arbitrary sparsity — a processor that reads a compressed matrix, skips every zero natively, and only ever touches nonzeros. People have built these. The trouble is that "skip every zero natively" means the hardware must, at runtime, decode variable-length lists of nonzero positions, resolve which nonzeros in one operand line up with which in the other, and feed a multiplier array whose lanes now finish at different times because different rows have different numbers of nonzeros. This is a load-balancing nightmare: one processing element sits idle while its neighbor grinds through a dense row. Papers in this corpus measure the damage directly — SparseWeaver (HPCA 2025) identifies "severe warp-level load imbalance" as the dominant cost on graph workloads, and Pipirima (DAC 2025) frames the whole problem as "load imbalance from unpredictable non-zero value distributions." The irregularity that makes sparsity valuable is the same irregularity that makes it slow.

The field's response is not one fix but a spectrum of bargains, and that is why there are multiple subthemes rather than a single clean answer. At one end, you can force the sparsity into a rigid pattern the hardware likes — accepting some accuracy loss to buy regularity. At the other, you can build ever-more-clever irregular machines and pay for the bookkeeping. In between sit the learned forms of sparsity — mixture-of-experts, activation prediction — that decide *at runtime* which computation to skip entirely, dodging the packing problem by never materializing the work. And running underneath all of it is a separate, older world: scientific sparse linear algebra, where the zeros come not from a trained network but from the physics of the problem, and where the same wall has been studied for fifty years.

## Subtheme 1: Forcing Sparsity Into a Shape the Hardware Likes

### What It Is

The cleanest way to make sparsity fast is to constrain *where* the zeros are allowed to be. The dominant constraint is called **N:M sparsity**: in every contiguous group of M weights, exactly N are allowed to be nonzero. The most common flavor is 2:4 — of every four weights, two must be zero. This sounds arbitrary until you see what it buys the hardware. Because the *count* of survivors per group is fixed, the packing is trivial: you always keep two, always drop two, and a tiny fixed-size index (which two survived, out of six possible pairings) tells the multiplier array where each nonzero belongs. No variable-length decoding, no load imbalance — every group takes the same time. NVIDIA built this directly into the A100's Sparse Tensor Cores in 2020, and it is the reason 2:4 is the default target: the silicon to accelerate it already ships in tens of millions of GPUs.

The catch is accuracy. Forcing exactly two-of-four to survive is a straitjacket. The *best* zeros to keep — the ones an unconstrained magnitude prune would select — rarely fall into a neat two-per-four arrangement. TB-STC (HPCA 2025) quantifies the damage precisely: naive one-dimensional N:M structure costs "more than 6% accuracy on OPT-6.7B and Llama2-7B at 50% sparsity," because the rigid pattern "diverges far from the unstructured optimum." So the research problem becomes: how do you get the regularity of N:M without paying its accuracy tax?

### What the Research Community Is Doing

TB-STC (HPCA 2025) attacks the accuracy tax by widening the pattern from a line to a block. Its insight is that during training, the forward pass uses a weight matrix and the backward pass uses its transpose — so it searches for a *2D block-wise* N:M pattern that stays structured whether read row-wise or column-wise, recovering "up to 2.58% accuracy" over prior structured methods while keeping hardware efficiency, at a measured 3.82x improvement in energy-delay product.

A parallel line accepts that real pruning produces *unstructured* sparsity and builds bridges back to structured hardware. TASD (MLSys 2025) decomposes any unstructured sparse tensor into a *series* of N:M structured tensors using the distributive law of matrix algebra — several structured passes summing to the unstructured result — letting an unmodified RTX 3080 with 2:4 Tensor Cores run arbitrary sparse networks with "no fine-tuning" at 39% speedup. At SC 2025, MP-SpMM ("Bridging the Gap between Unstructured SpMM and Structured Sparse Tensor Cores") reframes the same conversion as a graph maximum-matching problem, pairing nonzeros to fill 2:4 slots with minimal padding, hitting 2.42x average speedup over the best dense-Tensor-Core baseline. And SparStencil (SC 2025) carries the trick out of AI entirely, morphing irregular scientific stencil grids into 2:4 form to run on Sparse Tensor Cores at 3.1x average speedup across 79 kernels — reusing AI silicon for physics.

When the target is not a GPU but custom silicon, teams design the N:M unit from scratch. LLM-Barber (ICCAD 2025) rebuilds a pruned model's sparsity mask "in one shot" into block-structured form matched to a target accelerator's block size, no retraining. DANCE (ICCAD 2025) puts N:M extraction circuitry on *both* the weight and activation sides of a compute-in-memory macro, reaching 4.36x energy efficiency on ResNet-18. And for the smallest devices, N:M Sparse DNN Kernels for RISC-V MCUs (MLSys 2025) adds a single custom instruction, `xDecimate`, that performs the N:M select-and-multiply in one operation at "5% area overhead," delivering 3.21x on ResNet-18 at an aggressive 1:16 sparsity.

### The Tradeoff

Structured sparsity is the only form that reliably turns zeros into speed on commodity hardware, and the corpus shows it working from datacenter GPUs down to microcontrollers. What it costs is accuracy and generality. Every N:M scheme is a bet that the network's important weights can be redistributed into the allowed pattern without losing what matters, and that bet gets harder as sparsity climbs past 50 percent. The decomposition approaches (TASD, MP-SpMM) escape the accuracy tax but reintroduce work — several structured passes instead of one — so their speedup is bounded well below the sparsity ratio. Structured sparsity trades the packing problem for a placement problem, and placement is only sometimes solvable for free.

## Subtheme 2: Building Machines That Tolerate Irregularity

### What It Is

The opposite bet is to refuse the straitjacket and build hardware that eats unstructured sparsity directly. This is the harder engineering road, and it splits into two problems. The first is *finding and packing* the nonzeros fast enough that the bookkeeping does not eat the savings. The second is *load balancing* — keeping every lane of a wide array busy when the rows feeding it have wildly different nonzero counts. A general-purpose GPU handles neither well, so this subtheme is dominated by custom accelerators and by micro-architectural tricks that make the irregular access patterns predictable enough to pipeline.

### What the Research Community Is Doing

The recurring move is to *predict* the irregularity so the hardware can prepare for it. Pipirima (DAC 2025) borrows the branch predictor from CPU design: simple counters watch the stream of a compressed matrix and forecast where the next nonzeros will land, letting the accelerator prefetch and balance ahead of time — 6x over Tensaurus on sparse-dense multiply, up to 40x over ExTensor. SparseWeaver (HPCA 2025) adds a tiny functional unit called Weaver to each GPU core that converts irregular graph operations into balanced dense ones on the fly, killing the warp imbalance at "0.045% area overhead" for a 2.36x speedup. Misam (MICRO 2025) trains a lightweight decision tree to pick, per input matrix, which dataflow the accelerator should use, then reconfigures an FPGA to that choice — up to 10.76x.

Others attack the memory hierarchy, since irregular access is really a caching failure. SeaCache (MICRO 2025) observes that variable-length sparse rows do not fit fixed-size cache blocks, wasting SRAM, and packs them to fit — 2.8x over prior sparse caches. Bootes (MICRO 2025) reorders matrix rows with spectral clustering so that structurally similar rows sit together and reuse cached data, cutting off-chip traffic up to 2.31x. RTSpMSpM (ISCA 2025) makes the strangest leap: it notices that finding matching nonzeros in two sparse matrices is structurally identical to finding where a ray hits a triangle, and reuses the GPU's dedicated ray-tracing hardware to do sparse matrix multiply, 3.06x over cuSPARSE. Quartz (MICRO 2025) and HYTE (ISCA 2025) go fully reconfigurable — a substrate whose dataflow and tiling retune at runtime to whatever sparse kernel arrives (HYTE reports 3.3–6.2x geomean by jointly tuning tile size, shape, order, and buffering).

### The Tradeoff

Irregular machines can exploit *all* the sparsity, not just the fraction that fits a pattern, and they handle workloads — graphs, scientific matrices — that have no natural N:M structure at all. The price is complexity and specialization. Most of these are custom ASICs or FPGA designs, not commodity hardware; their speedups are measured against other accelerators, and the ones that run on GPUs (SparseWeaver, RTSpMSpM) win by hijacking existing units for unintended uses. The deeper cost is that irregularity-tolerant hardware is only as good as its prediction: when the sparsity pattern is genuinely random, the predictors miss, the load rebalances poorly, and the machine degrades toward its dense throughput. These designs bet that real-world sparsity is *structured-but-unlabeled* — that a hidden pattern exists to be discovered — and that bet is workload-dependent.

## Subtheme 3: Sparsity as a Runtime Decision — Mixture-of-Experts and Activation Skipping

### What It Is

The two subthemes above accept a fixed sparse matrix and try to run it fast. This one changes the game: it decides *at runtime* which computation to do at all, so the skipped work is never materialized and there is no packing problem to solve. Two mechanisms dominate. The first is **Mixture-of-Experts (MoE)**: instead of one large feed-forward block, the network holds many parallel blocks ("experts"), and a small *router* network reads each token and sends it to just a few experts. A model may hold 8x more parameters than a dense one but activate only two of eight experts per token — so it has the knowledge of a huge model at the compute cost of a small one. The sparsity is in *which experts run*, decided per token, learned during training. The second is **dynamic activation sparsity**: predicting, before you compute a layer, which of its outputs will be zero (or negligible) and skipping those specific multiplications for this specific input.

The problem MoE creates is not compute — it is memory and communication. All the experts must live *somewhere*, and only a few are used per token, so most of that parameter mass sits idle, consuming precious GPU memory or forcing constant shuffling of experts between devices.

### What the Research Community Is Doing

The MoE memory problem drives a large cluster of systems papers. MoE-Lightning (ASPLOS 2025) streams expert weights between CPU and GPU in a pipeline overlapping compute with prefetch, running Mixtral 8x7B on a single 16GB T4 at 10.3x the throughput of prior offloading systems. Klotski (ASPLOS 2025) batches tokens across concurrent requests that route to the *same* expert, amortizing the cost of loading that expert's weights and eliminating pipeline bubbles. Diff-MoE (SC 2025) builds a priority cache that keeps globally-popular experts resident and demand-loads cold ones — 2.74x over DeepSpeed. Hydra (DAC 2025) maps experts across chiplets by popularity to cut all-to-all traffic, and uses content-addressable memory to skip the gating math for unused experts, reaching 14.2x over a GPU. The communication tax is itself a target: COMET (MLSys 2025) reports that inter-device all-to-all shuffling occupies "up to 47% of execution time" and fuses computation with communication at fine grain to hide 86.5% of it. At the frontier of scale, X-MoE (SC 2025) trains a 545-billion-parameter MoE across 1,024 GPUs on Frontier, ten times larger than prior methods could.

Activation-sparsity work runs in parallel. Grasp (DAC 2025) predicts which MLP outputs will be zero in a ReLU-based LLM, grouping values to handle outliers, and skips the corresponding memory loads for 1.85x over dense. Dynamic Input Pruning (MLSys 2025) selects only the largest input activations per token on a phone, pruning the matching weight columns with no predictor network — 40% throughput and 46% less DRAM traffic on an Apple A18, at under 0.1 perplexity cost. Neuralink (ASPLOS 2025) attacks the storage angle: it reorders neurons in flash memory offline so that neurons which *co-activate* sit contiguously, turning the random reads of activation-sparse inference into sequential ones, 1.49x on-device. EXION (HPCA 2025) and HuMoniX (ISSCC 2025) exploit *temporal* activation sparsity in diffusion models — successive denoising iterations produce nearly identical intermediate outputs, so one dense iteration is computed and the next several reuse it, with EXION reporting up to 379x over a server GPU on some workloads.

### The Tradeoff

Runtime sparsity is the most powerful lever because it skips work at the granularity of *what this input actually needs*, and it never has to pack a sparse matrix — the skipped experts and dead activations simply never execute. But it moves the entire cost from arithmetic to data movement. An MoE model's problem is not FLOPs; it is that hundreds of gigabytes of expert weights must be stored, cached, and shuffled, and the routing is data-dependent so you cannot prefetch perfectly. Almost every MoE paper in this corpus is really a memory or communication paper wearing a sparsity hat. Activation prediction, meanwhile, is a gamble against the predictor's accuracy: a wrong prediction that skips a nonzero output silently corrupts the result, so these methods live or die on how tightly they can bound prediction error (Grasp's contribution is literally "11% higher prediction accuracy").

## Subtheme 4: Sparsity From Physics — Scientific Linear Algebra and Spiking Networks

### What It Is

Not all zeros come from pruning a network. In scientific computing, the zeros are *given* by the problem. When you discretize a physical system — heat flow across a plate, current through a circuit, stress in a bridge — each point interacts only with its immediate neighbors, so the resulting matrix is almost entirely zero: a million-by-million matrix with only a handful of nonzeros per row. Solving these systems (SpMV, sparse matrix multiply, LU and Cholesky factorization, triangular solve, conjugate gradient) is the oldest sparse-computing discipline, and it hits the *same* wall — irregular access, load imbalance, dependency chains — with none of the freedom to reshape the pattern, because the pattern is dictated by physics and reordering it changes the answer's cost structure but not its zeros. A cousin lives in **spiking neural networks (SNNs)**, brain-inspired models where neurons emit binary spikes (0 or 1) and are silent most of the time, giving extreme, dynamic, physically-grounded activation sparsity.

### What the Research Community Is Doing

The scientific-solver papers, concentrated at SC and in the DAC/ICCAD circuit-simulation track, mostly fight *dependency* and *locality* rather than packing. Telos (ISCA 2025) accelerates sparse triangular solve — whose loop-carried dependencies "severely limit parallelism" — by exploiting the stencil structure of PDE matrices, 61x over CPU and 8x over GPU. Caracal (SC 2025) is a GPU-resident sparse LU solver with fine-grained scheduling, 7x over SuperLU_DIST across 4 A100s; SubtreeLU (ICCAD 2025) restructures the dependency tree of circuit-simulation LU to expose parallelism. On the pure-throughput side, HSMU-SpGEMM (HPCA 2025) rethinks GPU hash accumulation to fit more nonzeros in shared memory; a large SC 2025 study asks bluntly "how effective is matrix reordering for SpMV" and finds the answer platform-dependent and inconsistent — a rare honest negative result. Sparsified Preconditioned CG (SC 2025) *deliberately drops* nonzeros from the preconditioner to break iteration dependencies and expose GPU parallelism, trading a few extra iterations for 3.73x end-to-end.

The SNN accelerators show the most creative sparsity paradigms in the corpus, because a spike train is binary and structured. Prosperity (HPCA 2025) introduces "product sparsity": if two matrix rows have *identical* spike patterns, their dot products with the same weights are identical, so you compute one and copy — 7.4x over a prior SNN accelerator and 193x the energy efficiency of an A100. Phi (ISCA 2025) decomposes spike activations into a few precomputed patterns plus a sparse residual, precomputing the bulk offline for 3.45x. Bishop (ISCA 2025) bundles spikes across tokens and time, routing dense bundles to a dense core and sparse ones to a sparse core, 5.91x. These designs exploit a form of redundancy — repeated binary patterns — that dense floating-point networks simply do not have.

### The Tradeoff

Physics-given sparsity cannot be reshaped, so this world lives entirely in the irregular-machine regime and cannot borrow the N:M shortcut — except where it is forcibly retrofitted, as SparStencil does by morphing stencils into 2:4. Its speedups are hard-won, often single-digit, and frequently bottlenecked by dependency chains rather than raw compute, which is a fundamentally different wall than the AI side faces. The SNN work is more optimistic precisely because binary spikes create *reusable structure* the pruning world lacks — but SNNs remain a research curiosity in accuracy terms, so their spectacular energy numbers (193x versus A100) come attached to models that do not yet match dense networks on hard tasks.

## How the Pieces Fit Together

The through-line across all 107 papers is a single tension: **regularity versus fidelity**. Every technique is somewhere on the line between "force the zeros into a hardware-friendly shape and eat the accuracy cost" and "preserve the true sparsity and eat the irregularity cost." Structured N:M sits at the regularity end; unstructured accelerators and scientific solvers sit at the fidelity end; MoE and activation prediction dodge the whole line by never materializing the skipped work. Solving a problem at one end tends to create a problem at the other — the recurring pattern in this corpus is a *conversion* between the two. TASD, MP-SpMM, and SparStencil all convert unstructured (or physical) sparsity *into* structured 2:4 form to reuse existing silicon. LLM-Barber and BitPattern convert irregular pruned masks *into* block patterns for custom hardware. This bridge-building is arguably the dominant research strategy of 2025: rather than choosing an end of the spectrum, translate between them, because NVIDIA's 2:4 Sparse Tensor Cores are a fixed, ubiquitous target that everything wants to run on.

The second unifying observation is that **sparsity has stopped being a compute-reduction technique and become a memory-and-communication technique**. The dense-network pruning story — fewer multiplies — barely appears without a memory qualifier. The center of gravity has shifted to MoE, where the challenge is storing and shuffling idle experts (MoE-Lightning, Klotski, Hydra, COMET, X-MoE, Diff-MoE — six-plus systems papers all fighting data movement, not arithmetic), and to activation sparsity, where the win (Dynamic Input Pruning's "46% DRAM reduction," Neuralink's flash reordering, the bit-separable Hot Chips accelerator's DRAM-access focus) is bytes not moved. This tracks the broader hardware reality: arithmetic is cheap and getting cheaper, while memory bandwidth is the binding constraint, so the value of a zero is increasingly the *load you avoid*, not the *multiply you avoid*.

Where are the bets being placed? On co-design and on prediction. Nearly every accelerator paper is an explicit algorithm-hardware co-design — the sparsity pattern is engineered *for* the silicon (SSFT's 51x, BitPattern, the RISC-V `xDecimate` ISA extension), because unstructured sparsity thrown at general hardware simply does not pay. And a striking number of designs are betting that irregularity is *predictable* — Pipirima's branch-predictor counters, Misam's and SSpMV's ML dataflow selectors, Grasp's activation predictor, the MoE hot-expert predictors. The field's implicit thesis is that real-world sparsity is not random noise but structured signal, and whoever predicts its structure cheaply wins.

## What Remains Hard

- **High-ratio structured sparsity without accuracy collapse.** N:M reliably works to about 50 percent (2:4); beyond that, forcing the pattern costs several points of accuracy, yet the sparsity actually present in trained networks is 90–95 percent. No structured scheme in this corpus safely captures that gap, and closing it — a 10:1 structured pattern that matches unstructured accuracy — would unlock an order of magnitude the hardware could actually use.

- **Data-dependent MoE routing that can be prefetched.** Expert selection is decided per token at runtime, so the weights to load are unknown until the router fires, defeating prefetch; a routing scheme that is both accuracy-preserving and predictable-in-advance would eliminate the pipeline bubbles that every MoE-offloading paper works around rather than solves.

- **Bounding the error of activation prediction.** Skipping a predicted-zero activation that is actually nonzero silently corrupts output; current methods manage this statistically, but there is no method to *guarantee* a prediction-error bound, which blocks activation sparsity from safety-critical or high-precision deployment.

- **One accelerator for the full sparsity spectrum.** Reconfigurable designs (Quartz, HYTE, Misam) approximate this, but a single chip that is simultaneously optimal for dense GEMM, 2:4 structured, unstructured DNN, and physics-given scientific matrices does not exist — every design still pays a penalty at the regimes it was not tuned for.

- **Dependency-bound scientific solvers on massively parallel hardware.** Sparse triangular solve and LU factorization are limited by loop-carried dependencies, not compute, so GPUs with thousands of idle cores stall; the sparsified-preconditioner trick (drop nonzeros to break dependencies) trades numerical convergence for parallelism, and the right point on that trade-off is still unknown per problem.

- **Sparse training, not just sparse inference.** Most wins here are inference-time; keeping a network sparse *during* training (so the backward pass and gradient communication are also cheap) faces the transpose problem TB-STC and PacTrain only partially solve, and dynamic-workload imbalance that DynMo must rebalance after the fact.

- **Making spiking networks accurate enough to deserve their efficiency.** SNN accelerators report 193x the energy efficiency of an A100 by exploiting binary spike-pattern reuse, but the underlying models still trail dense networks on hard benchmarks, so the physics of spike sparsity has outrun the learning theory that would make it worth deploying.
