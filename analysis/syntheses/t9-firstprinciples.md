# When Silicon Runs Out of Road: Building Machines for the Computations That General-Purpose Chips Cannot Afford

## The Problem

A general-purpose processor is a compromise machine. It is built to run any program tolerably rather than any one program well, and it pays for that flexibility in a currency that is easy to see once you know where to look: it spends most of its energy not on the arithmetic you asked for, but on figuring out *what* arithmetic to do. Fetching an instruction, decoding it, checking whether it depends on the last one, renaming registers, predicting a branch, moving operands in and out of a cache — all of that overhead surrounds a single multiply. Measurements over the past decade put the arithmetic itself at a few percent of the energy a CPU burns to execute it. The rest is the tax of generality.

For most of computing history that tax was worth paying, because transistors kept getting smaller, faster, and cheaper on a predictable schedule, and a general-purpose chip inherited those gains for free. That schedule has broken. Transistors still shrink, but they no longer get proportionally faster or lower-power, because the physical dimensions are now close enough to the size of individual atoms that leakage current and heat density set the limit rather than lithography. The consequence is that you can no longer make an old computation faster by waiting for a better chip. If you want a computation to run ten or a hundred times more efficiently, you have to build hardware whose data paths, memory layout, and number formats are shaped to that specific computation — you have to spend generality to buy efficiency.

That is where this corpus lives. The 324 papers in this theme are what happens when a particular computation becomes important enough, and expensive enough on general silicon, that the community decides to build it a custom home. The pattern repeats across wildly different domains. Fully homomorphic encryption — arithmetic on data that stays encrypted the entire time — runs roughly four to five orders of magnitude slower than the same computation on plaintext, so a BERT language-model inference that takes a fraction of a second normally takes seventeen hours encrypted on a CPU (ASPLOS 2025, *Cinnamon*). Zero-knowledge proofs, which let one party convince another that a computation was done correctly without revealing the inputs, are dominated by field arithmetic so heavy that a dedicated 366 mm² chip beats a server CPU by 801x (ISCA 2025, *zkSpeed*). Quantum computers need classical control electronics fast enough to react to a qubit measurement before the qubit forgets its state — microseconds — and analog-circuit design, genome alignment, and real-time 3D rendering each hit their own version of the same wall.

The unifying constraint is not any single physical quantity. It is an economic and physical mismatch: general-purpose hardware is optimized for the *average* of all programs, and each of these workloads is a violent outlier — arithmetic-heavy in a way silicon dislikes (huge modular multiplications, enormous polynomials), or memory-bound in a way caches cannot help, or built on a physical substrate (a qubit, a photon) that silicon does not natively speak. When a workload is that far from the average, the tax of generality stops being a tax and becomes a wall.

## Why the Obvious Solution Does Not Work

The obvious answer is: build one custom chip per problem. If FHE is slow, tape out an FHE ASIC; if quantum control is slow, tape out a quantum controller. And indeed much of this corpus does exactly that. But a modern ASIC in a leading process costs tens of millions of dollars in mask sets and design effort and takes two years from concept to silicon. During those two years the algorithm you froze into transistors keeps moving. FHE is the sharpest example: the field's cryptographers keep inventing faster key-switching methods (hoisting, KLSS gadget decomposition), so an accelerator that hard-wires last year's algorithm ships already behind the software (ISCA 2025, *FAST*, which exists specifically to fold both new methods into one datapath). You cannot win a race against algorithmic progress if your vehicle takes two years to build and cannot be repainted.

So the field splits into several strategies that trade off differently against that dilemma, and that is why this theme has *subthemes* rather than a single clean fix. One camp keeps the substrate silicon but attacks the arithmetic — building reconfigurable accelerators, borrowing the matrix-multiply units already sitting idle inside GPUs, or moving computation into the memory itself. A second camp abandons silicon for a fundamentally different physics — photons, superconducting loops, spiking analog circuits — betting that some computations are cheaper in a medium that was never digital to begin with. A third camp turns the tools inward, using machine learning to design the chips themselves, because the design problem is now the bottleneck. These are not competing answers to one question; they are answers to different questions the wall poses. The rest of this essay walks through each.

## Subtheme 1: Encrypted and Verifiable Computation — Making Arithmetic on Secrets Affordable

### What It Is

Two cryptographic constructions dominate this part of the corpus, and both share a physical signature: they replace ordinary numbers with enormous ones and then do an enormous amount of arithmetic on them. Fully homomorphic encryption (FHE) lets a server compute on data it cannot read. The trick is that an encrypted number is not a single value but a high-degree polynomial — think of a list of tens of thousands of coefficients — and adding or multiplying two encrypted numbers means adding or multiplying these polynomials. Multiplication of two degree-N polynomials naively costs N² coefficient multiplies; the standard fix is the Number Theoretic Transform (NTT), a discrete-Fourier-style change of representation that turns polynomial multiply into cheap element-wise multiply, at the cost of doing the transform itself. Every FHE operation is dominated by NTTs and by "key-switching," a bookkeeping step that keeps the ciphertext decryptable. Worse, each multiplication makes the ciphertext noisier, and periodically you must run "bootstrapping" — an expensive refresh that resets the noise budget — or the data becomes undecryptable.

Zero-knowledge proofs (ZKPs) are the mirror image: instead of hiding data from the computer, they let a computer prove to a skeptic that it ran a program correctly, revealing nothing but the proof. Under the hood this becomes arithmetic in a finite field (numbers modulo a large prime), dominated by two operations — the multi-scalar multiplication (MSM), summing millions of scaled points on an elliptic curve, and the NTT again. The reader should notice that FHE and ZKP, despite doing opposite things, bottleneck on the *same primitives*: modular multiplication of big numbers, and NTTs. That shared bottleneck is why they share hardware ideas.

### What the Research Community Is Doing

The field is attacking the cost from every architectural angle at once. On GPUs, the insight is that a GPU already contains "tensor cores" — units built to multiply small matrices for neural networks — sitting idle during cryptography; *WarpDrive* (HPCA 2025) rewrites the NTT to use both the tensor cores and the ordinary CUDA cores in the same kernel, reaching 1,218 thousand NTT-operations per second on an A100 and a 13.4x throughput gain, while *Neo* (ISCA 2025) similarly recasts FHE polynomial arithmetic as tensor-core matrix operations. On custom silicon, *Cinnamon* (ASPLOS 2025) breaks the FHE accelerator into a scale-out array of chiplets so it can hold a whole BERT model, cutting that seventeen-hour encrypted inference to 1.67 seconds — a 36,600x speedup over CPU. A different camp argues the real bottleneck is not the NTT at all: *Anaheim* (HPCA 2025) measured that element-wise operations, not transforms, stall the GPU, and moved those operations into the DRAM itself (processing-in-memory) to stop shuttling data. And silicon has now been proven in a fabricated 28 nm chip that does FHE at 4.05 µJ per encryption (ISSCC 2025, paper 17.2). On the ZKP side, *zkSpeed* (ISCA 2025) shares one set of modular multipliers across all three proof sub-checks to save 48.9% of the area, hitting 801x over an EPYC CPU; *LegoZK* (HPCA 2025) instead makes the field-arithmetic units reconfigurable so one chip serves every proof stage, beating a fixed-function ASIC by 31.96x while using half its area.

### The Tradeoff

Every one of these wins buys throughput with specialization, and specialization is fragile. The GPU approaches inherit the GPU's flexibility but leave energy on the table — a tensor core doing modular arithmetic is still a compromise. The ASICs win on energy and speed but freeze one cryptographic scheme into silicon, which is precisely the wrong bet in a field where the cryptography keeps changing; the reconfigurable designs (*LegoZK*, *FAST*, the multi-scheme ISSCC processor) exist to hedge that bet, paying an area and complexity premium to stay current. And there is a deeper cost the hardware cannot fix: the constant factor is enormous. Even at 36,600x, encrypted inference is still slower and costlier than plaintext, so FHE remains confined to the narrow set of applications where privacy is worth thousands of times the compute. The hardware is buying down a mountain, not leveling it. A sobering reminder came from *ZK-Hammer* (DAC 2025), which showed that a Rowhammer memory-fault attack can extract secrets from zk-SNARK provers — the very act of building fast specialized hardware opens new physical side channels.

## Subtheme 2: Quantum Computing — The Classical Machinery Around the Qubit

### What It Is

A quantum bit is a physical system — a superconducting loop, a trapped ion, a neutral atom held by lasers — whose state is fragile in a specific, quantifiable way: it decays in microseconds and every operation on it has an error rate around 10⁻³ to 10⁻⁴. To compute usefully you need error rates near 10⁻¹⁵, and the only known route there is quantum error correction (QEC): spread one "logical" qubit across many physical ones and continuously measure parity checks to detect and correct errors. This creates a brutal real-time constraint. Every QEC cycle, the hardware produces a stream of measurement outcomes (a "syndrome"), and a classical decoder must figure out which error occurred and issue a correction *before the next cycle* — within roughly a microsecond — or errors pile up faster than they are fixed. So a huge fraction of quantum-computing systems research is not about qubits at all. It is about the classical silicon that surrounds them: decoders, controllers, readout circuits, and compilers that must all run at cryogenic temperatures or at microsecond latency.

The second half of the problem is that today's quantum hardware has a fixed, sparse connectivity — you can only directly operate on qubits that are physically adjacent — while algorithms assume any qubit can interact with any other. Bridging that gap means inserting SWAP operations to shuttle quantum states into position, and every extra SWAP adds error. Compiling a quantum program is therefore an optimization problem: minimize the SWAPs, minimize the gate count, respect the hardware's topology.

### What the Research Community Is Doing

On the decoder side, *Micro Blossom* (ASPLOS 2025) is the first hardware to run the exact minimum-weight-perfect-matching algorithm — the gold-standard but expensive decoder — in FPGA logic with a processing unit per vertex and edge, cutting decoding latency 8x at code distance 13. Others trade exactness for speed: *SOME* (ICCAD 2025) reformulates decoding as a one-hot optimization that shrinks the variable count 99.9x and runs in microseconds on a commodity CPU while still correcting up to a 10.5% physical error rate. *Micro-2025-078* adds "leakage speculation" — spotting when a qubit has leaked out of its computational states — using at most 10 lookup tables per qubit and cutting logical error rate 16%. On control, *ARTERY* (ISCA 2025) borrows branch prediction from classical CPUs: it speculatively runs quantum gates before a mid-circuit measurement resolves, guessing right over 90% of the time and cutting feedback latency 2.07x. The cryogenic-electronics community fabricates the physical interface: an 18.5 µW-per-qubit cryo-CMOS readout chip that uses QAM modulation to read many spin qubits on one wire (ISSCC 2025, 13.5), and a cryo-BiCMOS controller for trapped ions reaching ~10⁻⁶ single-qubit error (ISSCC 2025, 13.3). On compilation, *S-SYNC* (ISCA 2025) co-optimizes ion shuttling and SWAP insertion jointly rather than sequentially to cut shuttle operations 3.69x, and graph-based reinforcement learning frameworks (ICCAD 2025) trim SWAP overhead 5.8–17.6%. Meanwhile the SC papers document the messy reality of bolting a 20-qubit machine onto an HPC cluster (SC 2025, *First Practical Experiences*) and orchestrating hybrid quantum-classical workflows.

### The Tradeoff

The quantum subtheme is buying a future that does not yet exist. Almost none of these results run a useful quantum algorithm faster than a classical computer today; they are lowering the overhead of the *scaffolding* so that when enough good qubits exist, the classical control will not be the bottleneck. That is a defensible bet, but it means the metrics are indirect — latency of a decoder, density of qubit memory (*LSQCA* pushes memory density from ~50% to ~90%, HPCA 2025), microwatts per qubit — rather than end-to-end speedups. The field is also fragmenting across incompatible physical substrates (superconducting, trapped-ion, neutral-atom), and hardware tuned for one may be useless for another; the routing-aware placement work for neutral atoms (ICCAD 2025) does not transfer to the shuttle-and-swap problem of trapped ions. The community is placing many small bets because it does not yet know which qubit will win.

## Subtheme 3: Post-Silicon Substrates — Computing with Light, Physics, and Spikes

### What It Is

Some computations are cheap in a physical medium that silicon has to emulate expensively. Three appear repeatedly in the corpus. Photonic computing performs a matrix-vector multiply — the core operation of every neural network — by sending light through a mesh of interferometers: the multiplication happens at the speed of light with almost no energy, because you are not toggling transistors, you are steering photons. Analog "physics-based" solvers map a hard optimization problem (like Boolean satisfiability) onto a network of coupled oscillators or a circuit that obeys a physical inequality, and let the system *settle* into its lowest-energy state, which encodes the answer — the physics does the search for free instead of a digital algorithm iterating. Neuromorphic and spiking hardware represents information as the *timing* of discrete pulses rather than as numbers held in registers, mimicking how neurons communicate, so that a value that is zero costs nothing because no spike fires.

The common thread is that each of these substrates gets one operation nearly free by giving up something silicon takes for granted — exact digital repeatability, easy storage of intermediate values, or the ability to represent an arbitrary program.

### What the Research Community Is Doing

Photonics is the most mature. *LightML* (ISCA 2025) pairs a photonic crossbar with a purpose-built memory system to keep the optical core busy above 80% utilization, reaching 325 TOPS at 3 watts — roughly 108 TOPS per watt, an order of magnitude beyond digital accelerators. A photonic tensor core with an integrated optical SRAM and a one-hot electro-optic analog-to-digital converter reaches 3.02 TOPS/W (DAC 2025), and *Opto-ViT* (ICCAD 2025) runs a vision transformer near the image sensor with under 1.6% accuracy loss. On the physics-solver side, an oscillator-based mixed-signal engine solves 50-variable 3-SAT problems with 100% success in 31.7 microseconds (ISSCC 2025, 25.1), where prior hardware solvers stalled below 20 variables; *KLIMA* (Hot Chips 2025) solves SAT directly inside memory in single-digit nanoseconds; and a separate ISSCC session (paper 25.4) presents circuits that "solve optimization problems by exploiting physics inequalities" without running any algorithm at all. On the neuromorphic side, *Single Spike ANNs* (ISCA 2025) encode neural-network weights as circuit delays and activations as pulse-arrival times, achieving 11x better energy than conventional spiking networks and 3.5x better than an 8-bit digital systolic array, while memristor and printed-electronics spiking devices (VLSID 2025, ICCAD 2025 *SpikeSynth*) push the physical substrate further from silicon.

### The Tradeoff

These substrates trade generality and precision for efficiency in a way that is even more extreme than a custom ASIC. A photonic core multiplies matrices beautifully but cannot store a result — light does not sit still — so every photonic system is really a hybrid, ringed by electronic converters (digital-to-analog and analog-to-digital) whose power often dominates the whole system; *P-DAC* (DAC 2025) exists solely because the digital-to-analog converters, not the optics, are the bottleneck in photonic transformers. Analog and physics-based solvers are fast but noisy and hard to scale: an oscillator network that settles perfectly at 50 variables may not at 5,000, and the answer is approximate. Neuromorphic hardware is efficient only for workloads that are naturally sparse and event-driven, and the software ecosystem to program it barely exists. Each of these is a scalpel, brilliant on the one workload it was shaped for and useless outside it — which is exactly why they remain research substrates rather than replacements for the GPU.

## Subtheme 4: Machine Learning for Chip Design — Turning the Tools Inward

### What It Is

There is a recursion hiding in this whole theme. Building any of the specialized chips above requires electronic design automation (EDA) — the software that places millions of transistors, routes the wires between them, and closes timing so signals arrive when they should. These are combinatorial optimization problems on graphs of enormous size, and they are exactly the kind of problem where a human-written heuristic leaves value on the table and a learned model might do better. Analog circuit design is the sharpest case: unlike digital logic, an analog circuit's behavior depends on continuous transistor sizes interacting non-linearly with process, voltage, and temperature variation, and there is no clean automation — it has traditionally been slow, expert-driven handcraft. So the community is now pointing machine learning, reinforcement learning, diffusion models, and large language models at the design process itself, trying to compress months of expert effort into hours of search.

### What the Research Community Is Doing

For analog sizing — choosing transistor dimensions to hit performance targets — reinforcement-learning frameworks now dominate: *GLOVA* (DAC 2025) uses risk-sensitive RL with ensemble critics to handle variation, reaching 80.5x better sample efficiency and 76x less design time, while *PPAAS* (ICCAD 2025) uses goal-conditioned RL that generalizes across PVT corners at 4.1x fewer simulations. Generative models attack topology, not just sizing: *DiffCkt* (ICCAD 2025) uses a diffusion model to generate transistor-level analog circuits outright, and *EVA* (DAC 2025) uses a decoder-only transformer to invent novel circuits pin-connection by pin-connection, fine-tuned with the same PPO/DPO methods used to align chatbots. Large language models have arrived as design agents: *ASTRA* (ICCAD 2025) pairs reasoning agents with Bayesian optimization to cut simulation iterations 4.35x, *AnaFlow* (ICCAD 2025) makes the agent explain its reasoning and learn from prior mistakes, and *AI Analog Circuit Design Agents* (ICCAD 2025) builds knowledge graphs so insight from one topology transfers to another. The same turn shows up in placement and routing — differentiable timing-driven FPGA placement with an ML delay model (ICCAD 2025), GPU-accelerated routing-aware placement for photonic circuits at 94.79% routing success (*Apollo*, ICCAD 2025) — and even in modeling: *ThermoPhoton* (ICCAD 2025) replaces a slow numerical thermal solver with a learned operator at 0.07% error, and LLM multi-agent systems now generate quantum code with a 50% accuracy gain (DAC 2025).

### The Tradeoff

Using ML to design chips inherits every weakness of ML. The models are only as good as the simulations they are trained against, and a design that looks optimal to the model can fail in silicon if the training distribution missed a corner case — which is why so much of this work is obsessed with generalizing across PVT variation rather than just hitting nominal targets. Reinforcement learning is sample-hungry, and each sample is a slow circuit simulation, so the headline "80x sample efficiency" is a fight against a cost that is still crippling. Generative topology models can produce circuits that are novel but not manufacturable, or that no engineer can trust because they cannot be explained — hence the push toward explainable agents and knowledge graphs. And there is a chicken-and-egg dependency: ML-for-EDA is what will make the *next* generation of specialized accelerators cheaper to design, but it needs the current generation of accelerators to run its own training. The tools that build the wall-climbing gear are themselves being built out of the same scarce compute.

## How the Pieces Fit Together

Step back and the four subthemes are not four separate fields; they are four responses to one question — *what do you do when general silicon can no longer afford your computation?* — arranged along a spectrum of how radically they depart from the status quo. Encrypted and verifiable computation keeps the silicon and reshapes the arithmetic. Machine-learning-for-EDA keeps the silicon and reshapes the *design process* that produces it. Post-silicon substrates abandon digital silicon for photons and physics. And quantum computing abandons the whole classical model of computation, then spends most of its effort rebuilding classical scaffolding to make the new model usable. Read in that order, the theme is a map of increasing desperation and increasing potential payoff: the further left, the safer and more incremental; the further right, the larger the prize and the further from working.

The subthemes also constrain each other in concrete ways. The shared arithmetic primitive — big-integer modular multiplication and the NTT — links FHE, ZKP, and post-quantum cryptography so tightly that a hardware idea in one migrates immediately to the others; the same reconfigurable finite-field unit that accelerates zero-knowledge proofs (*LegoZK*) is a cousin of the polynomial-multiplier arrays built for lattice cryptography (*RePM*, ICCAD 2025) and FHE. The recursion is even sharper: post-quantum cryptography exists because *quantum computers*, if built, will break today's encryption (a DAC 2025 paper optimizes the quantum circuits that would attack RSA-2048), so one subtheme is racing to build the machine that another subtheme is racing to defend against — and ML-for-EDA is the tool that will design the accelerators for both. Photonics needs EDA tools that do not exist for its curvy analog waveguides, which is why *Apollo* had to invent photonic-aware placement; quantum needs its own compilers; every exotic substrate drags a new toolchain behind it.

The dominant strategy emerging from the full corpus is *co-design under uncertainty*: because no one knows which algorithm, cryptographic scheme, or qubit technology will win, the winning designs are those that specialize enough to get the 10–100x efficiency win but stay reconfigurable enough to survive the next algorithmic turn. The pure fixed-function ASIC is losing ground to the reconfigurable accelerator, the multi-scheme processor, the elastic array. The bet the field is placing is that specialization is now mandatory but premature commitment is fatal — so the art is in choosing exactly which axis to freeze into silicon and which to leave soft.

## What Remains Hard

- **The FHE constant factor is still four orders of magnitude too large.** Even after a 36,600x hardware speedup, encrypted computation costs thousands of times more than plaintext; closing that gap needs a co-designed advance in cryptography and architecture together, not either alone, and no one has shown a path to within 10x.
- **Quantum decoders must scale sub-microsecond to millions of qubits.** Today's fastest exact decoders (*Micro Blossom*) are demonstrated at code distance 13 on one FPGA; a fault-tolerant machine needs thousands of logical qubits decoded in parallel within the same microsecond budget, and the classical bandwidth and interconnect to do that does not exist.
- **Photonic systems are strangled by their data converters.** The optical core is nearly free, but the digital-to-analog and analog-to-digital converters that feed it can dominate total power; a photonic accelerator whose converters are as efficient as its optics has not been built.
- **Analog and physics-based solvers do not have a scaling theory.** A 50-variable 3-SAT oscillator works; whether the same architecture holds its 100% solvability at 5,000 variables, or degrades gracefully, is unknown, and there is no analog analog of a digital error-correction guarantee.
- **ML-designed circuits cannot yet be trusted at tape-out.** Generative and RL-designed analog circuits optimize against simulators, but silicon-verified, variation-robust, explainable designs that an engineer will sign off on remain rare — the gap between "the model likes it" and "it works in 3 nm across all corners" is a research frontier in itself.
- **Every exotic substrate needs a toolchain it does not have.** Photonic, quantum, and neuromorphic hardware each require placement, routing, compilation, and thermal modeling built from scratch, and the absence of mature EDA for these substrates is now a bigger bottleneck than the devices themselves.
- **Specialization multiplies the side-channel attack surface.** Purpose-built cryptographic and secure accelerators create new physical leakage paths — *ZK-Hammer*'s Rowhammer attack on zk-SNARKs, power-analysis attacks on masked post-quantum accelerators (ICCAD 2025) — so the very hardware built to protect secrets can become the channel that leaks them, and hardening it without surrendering the efficiency win is unsolved.
