# The Deep Read: One Constraint, Nine Answers

*A master narrative for 946 AI-hardware papers from 2025 — MLSys, ISCA, MICRO, HPCA, ASPLOS, DAC.*

---

## The one constraint

A computer chip does two jobs, and only two. It does arithmetic — adding and multiplying numbers — and it moves numbers between the place they are stored and the place the arithmetic happens. That is the whole machine. Everything a chip has ever done, from spreadsheets to language models, is some arrangement of those two jobs: fetch the numbers, combine them, put the answer back.

For most of computing history, the arithmetic was the expensive part, so engineers poured their effort into faster arithmetic. They succeeded so completely that they inverted the problem. A modern chip can multiply two numbers in a sliver of a billionth of a second, at almost no energy cost. But before it can multiply them, those two numbers have to physically arrive from memory, traveling as electrical charge down metal wires. That trip is slow, and it is expensive — often a hundred times more expensive in time and energy than the multiplication it feeds. The arithmetic became nearly free. The fetching did not. Picture a brilliant accountant who can add any column of figures instantly, but whose ledgers sit in a warehouse across town, delivered one page at a time by a single courier. The accountant is never the bottleneck. The courier is. This is the one fact underneath every paper in this collection: **modern computers are limited by moving data, not by doing arithmetic.**

Why is moving data so costly? Because a number in memory is a tiny puddle of electric charge sitting in a specific physical spot, and to use it you must push that charge down a wire to the arithmetic unit. Wires have a fixed width — only so many numbers can flow per second, a limit called *bandwidth* — and a fixed length, which sets how long each number takes to arrive. Distance costs energy; width caps throughput. You can build an arithmetic unit that could multiply a trillion numbers a second, but if the wire from memory only delivers a hundred billion numbers a second, your magnificent unit sits idle nine-tenths of the time, starving at the loading dock. The arithmetic was never the wall. The doorway to memory is the wall.

Then artificial intelligence arrived and drove this problem from serious to dominant. A large language model — the kind of system that writes and converses — is, at bottom, an enormous pile of learned numbers called *weights*, hundreds of billions of them. To produce even a single word of output, the chip must read essentially every one of those weights out of memory, pass each through a quick multiply-and-add, and move on. The arithmetic per weight is trivial. The hauling is not. Multiply billions of weights by the cost of dragging each one down the expensive wire, and you get a machine whose speed is set almost entirely by how many weights per second it can pull through the doorway. Measurements in these papers are blunt about it: while generating text, the fast arithmetic hardware often sits only about a fifth busy. The rest of the time it waits.

There is a second twist that makes AI uniquely punishing, and it is worth naming precisely because so much of this collection turns on it. As a language model reads a conversation, it does not re-read every earlier word for each new word it writes. Instead, when each word first goes by, the model computes a small summary of it and stores that summary so it never has to recompute it. This growing pile of summaries is the model's *running notes* — one new page for every word the model has ever seen in this conversation. (The field's jargon is "the KV cache," but running notes is the honest name.) The notes are what let a chatbot hold a long thread in its head. They are also a second enormous thing that must live in the chip's scarce fast memory and be hauled past the arithmetic units to generate each word. As the conversation grows, the notes grow, and every new word gets slower to produce because there are more pages to drag across town.

Put those together and you have the constraint the whole field is bending around. The models are too big to fit in fast memory, so their weights must be streamed constantly across the expensive wire. Their running notes grow without bound and must be streamed too. Split the work across many chips to go faster, and now the numbers must also cross the even-slower wires *between* chips. At every level — inside one chip, between neighboring chips, across a room full of machines — the arithmetic units are starving and the supply lines are the constraint. This is not a narrow engineering nuisance. It is where the money goes, where the electricity goes, and where the waiting lives. Everything that follows is a different community attacking this same wall.

## Nine answers to one question

If the enemy is data movement, there are only a handful of fundamental moves against it: move less data, move it a shorter distance, or move the arithmetic to where the data already sits. The nine themes in this collection (plus a tenth catch-all) are nine communities making those moves at different layers of the machine — and they are unmistakably attacking the same constraint.

**Attention and the running notes** (T1) fight the notebook directly: how to keep it, pack it, and read it without hauling every page. **Smaller numbers** (T2) shrink each value so less travels down the wire in the first place. **The memory wall** (T3) is the constraint in its rawest form, and its boldest answer is to move the arithmetic into the memory. **Interconnect** (T4) fights the same battle one level up, between chips talking to chips. **Sparsity** (T5) skips the work that does not matter — the zeros, the sleeping experts, the near-repeats — so it never has to move at all. **Compilers** (T6) rearrange the arithmetic so fewer numbers make the trip. **Security** (T7) discovers that the very shortcuts we use to move data faster leak secrets through their shadows. **Reliability** (T8) asks how we even know the answer is right when the data is in motion, shared, and guessed at. **Specialized hardware** (T9) reshapes the machine around workloads whose data-movement is nothing like a language model's. And **the other drawer** (T0) collects the rest — compression, communication-light training, honest measurement — which turn out to be the same insight in different rooms of the house.

Here is the map, one theme at a time.

## T1 — Keep the running notes in fast memory

The specific face of the constraint: to write each new word, a language model must haul its entire growing notebook of running notes past the arithmetic units, doing a tiny amount of math on a huge amount of data.

The most basic move is to keep the notes rather than recompute them, and reuse them wherever two conversations overlap. If a thousand users all begin with the same long instruction, the model would naively compute and store that identical notebook a thousand times. So systems store the shared opening once and let everyone borrow it — *prefix caching* — organizing the notebook as a tree whose shared trunk is stored once and whose branches are the parts that diverge. Independently, there is the question of how the notebook is laid out in the chip's limited memory: early systems reserved a fixed slab per conversation sized for the worst case, wasting most of it, until the field learned to break the notebook into small pages handed out on demand, exactly the way an operating system manages memory. That single change unlocked serving far more users per chip.

The other half of the theme is the arithmetic of attention itself. A naive computation builds a giant table of how much each word attends to each other word, writes it out to slow memory, then reads it back — pure wasted driving. The breakthrough routine the whole field now builds on never materializes that table: it processes the passage in small tiles that fit in on-chip scratch memory, carrying just enough running totals to stitch the tiles together. On top of this sit the honest-tradeoff moves: store the notes in coarser numbers (four digits instead of sixteen, a quarter the data to move) or skip the pages that barely matter, since in a long passage most earlier words contribute little to the next word. Both trade a little accuracy for a large cut in data moved — and a recurring cautionary finding is that these savings can shrink or vanish once measured inside a real serving stack rather than a clean benchmark.

**Connects to:** This theme is where the whole collection's constraint is most visible, so it borrows from nearly everyone. It leans directly on **smaller numbers** (T2) to shrink the notes, and on **sparsity** (T5) to skip unimportant pages. When conversations get so long the notebook won't fit, it reaches for the **memory wall** theme's most radical answer — computing inside the memory and storage chips where the notes live (T3). And when it splits the arithmetic-heavy first read of a prompt from the memory-bound word-by-word generation onto different pools of chips, it hands the problem to **interconnect** (T4), which must stream the notebook between them without becoming the new bottleneck.

## T2 — Use smaller numbers

The specific face of the constraint: every weight and every note is stored in some number of bits, and the more bits, the more warehouse space it occupies and the more wire it clogs on every trip.

The single most powerful thing you can do to a starving chip is make its numbers smaller. A value stored in four bits instead of sixteen is a quarter the size to store and a quarter the data to move — and for many models this barely dents the output quality. So the workhorse move is to re-express numbers that lived in 16 or 32 bits using just 4 or 8: pick a small ladder of evenly-spaced values and snap each original number to its nearest rung. Almost every serving system now quantizes weights to 4 bits and activations to 8.

The difficulty is that smaller numbers are cruder numbers. With only four bits you have sixteen buckets to represent every value, and while most weights cluster near zero and round cleanly, a few freakishly large *outliers* carry disproportionate importance and degrade badly when crushed. The field's ingenuity is almost entirely about paying the speed savings without the accuracy tax: spend more bits only on the rare outliers and crush the ordinary bulk; give each small group of numbers its own ladder tuned to its spread; or let the very shape of the number format adapt per group. A deeper bet, *vector quantization*, compresses whole little groups of numbers together by matching them to entries in a shared codebook, reaching below one bit per weight — at the cost of awkward, cache-thrashing lookups that only pay off with careful hardware. And a subtle trap haunts all of it: you can shrink the numbers and get no faster, because the chip pays back every saving just unpacking the small numbers into a form its rigid arithmetic understands. One paper found unpacking devouring 95 percent of instructions. So a large share of the work is rebuilding the hardware to consume small numbers natively.

**Connects to:** Smaller numbers is the most broadly enabling theme in the collection — it makes every other data-movement problem easier. It directly shrinks the running notes for **attention** (T1) and cuts the traffic on every wire of the **memory wall** (T3) and the **interconnect** (T4). Its outlier-handling and codebook tricks are a form of **sparsity** (T5) applied to bits. And its recurring lesson — that a compression scheme is only as good as the hardware that consumes it — is the same co-design reflex that drives **specialized hardware** (T9) and forces the **compiler** (T6) to generate code that keeps small numbers small.

## T3 — Move the math to the data

The specific face of the constraint, in its rawest form: the widening gap between how fast a chip can compute and how fast memory can feed it — the *memory wall*.

For seventy years the assumption was fixed: data lives in memory, and when you need to compute, you bring the data to the processor. This theme contains both the conventional defenses against the wall and the one radical idea that inverts that assumption. The conventional defenses are the accumulated craft of hiding the commute: *caches* (small fast pockets of storage holding the data most likely to be needed soon, with clever rules for what to evict), *prefetching* (predicting what the program will want and fetching it early), and *tiering* (stacking fast-small memory over cheap-large memory and keeping the hot data close). For programs that march predictably through memory, these hide the commute so well the wall barely shows.

What is not solved is the hard core: the irregular, scattered, pointer-chasing accesses of graphs and sparse math, the hidden tax of translating a program's made-up addresses into real ones, and above all the sheer volume of weights and running notes that modern AI must haul, which no cache can hold. For these, the boldest answer is to stop moving the data at all. If hauling data to the processor is the whole expense, leave the data where it sits and put small arithmetic units inside or beside the memory chips — *processing-in-memory* and *near-memory processing*. The same logic extends one tier out, to computing inside the storage drives so that a database query filters out the useless records before they ever cross the slow link. The payoff can be spectacular — several-fold speedups at a fraction of the energy — precisely because the data never travels. The cost is real: memory chips have brutally tight room for logic, the hardware and software must be co-designed together, and the programming tools are immature.

**Connects to:** This is the constraint that names the whole collection, so every theme is a special case of it. **Attention** (T1) turns to in-memory and in-storage compute when the notebook overflows; **specialized hardware** (T9) makes near-data processing a first-class design pattern; **sparsity** (T5) pushes its scatter-gather work down into memory to avoid hauling wasted cargo. It depends on **interconnect** (T4) to connect its pools of memory, and it worries **reliability** (T8) and **security** (T7), because densely-packed memory cells that disturb their neighbors are both a correctness hazard and an attack surface — a hazard that compute-in-memory can worsen.

## T4 — Chips that talk to chips

The specific face of the constraint: a single large model is far too big for one chip, so the work is spread across thousands of chips — and the moment you split it, those chips must constantly tell each other what they found.

The cruel arithmetic is that compute scales beautifully and communication does not. Double the chips and you double the arithmetic power nearly for free, but the talking between them tends to grow faster while the wires connecting them do not get fatter. So as you add chips to go faster, a larger fraction of every second is spent with expensive chips idle, waiting for a message from a neighbor. The single most important idea is *overlap*: never let a chip sit idle waiting to talk — arrange things so that while a message travels, the chip is already computing the next piece of work, tucking the communication invisibly underneath the arithmetic. This is fiendishly hard because you must slice the work into pieces that can be sent while others are still cooking, without any chip acting on data that has not arrived.

Beyond overlap, the moves are: send less in the first place (compress the updates, fuse redundant messages, avoid the redundant fetch); match the message pattern to the physical shape of the wiring, or reshape the wiring to fit the messages; split the work so the chips need to talk less; do arithmetic inside the network switches so that many chips' contributions are added up in transit and only the sum comes out; and build faster physical roads — stitching many chiplets densely side by side, or stripping the networking stack down to its lowest layer. The field has won the first battle on well-behaved, regular training runs; the honest gaps are all on the irregular, input-dependent traffic of recommendation and graph workloads, where the clean overlap tricks break down.

**Connects to:** Interconnect is the memory wall (T3) one level up — the same starving-arithmetic problem, now with the network as the doorway. It is what makes **attention's** (T1) split of prefill from decode possible, by streaming the notebook between chip pools. It is essential to **sparsity's** (T5) Mixture-of-Experts models, where just shuffling the sleeping experts between chips can eat nearly half the total time. And its dependence on splitting work well hands a hard combinatorial problem straight to the **compiler** (T6), which must choose how to carve the model across chips to minimize the talking.

## T5 — Skip the work that doesn't matter

The specific face of the constraint: a huge fraction of the numbers in an AI model are zero, or nearly zero, or exact repeats of something already computed — and every one of those the chip dutifully fetches and multiplies is pure wasted data movement.

Multiplying by zero gives zero; adding zero changes nothing. So every zero hauled from memory and pushed through the arithmetic unit cost real energy for no effect. The word for "mostly zeros" is *sparsity*, and the theme's one idea is: if most of the work does not matter, arrange to skip it, and skip the data movement it would have required. The trouble is a mismatch — chips are fastest on big, dense, regularly-shaped blocks marching in lockstep, but zeros are scattered irregularly, like holes in Swiss cheese, and you do not know where until you run the model. The bookkeeping to track "which numbers survived and where do they live" can cost more than the zeros saved. This is the field's central tension: skipping useless work sounds free, but the machinery to find what is useless is not free.

The flavors of "work that doesn't matter" are surprisingly varied. There are literal zeros, which you skip by finding the survivors cheaply — or force into a tidy, predictable pattern the hardware can march through. There are whole sleeping chunks of the model: a Mixture-of-Experts splits the network into many specialists and wakes only a handful per word, so the model holds enormous knowledge while running a small slice — turning a compute problem into a data-movement-and-prediction problem, since the sleeping experts must be fetched from slow memory just in time. And there are near-repeats: a diffusion model refining an image over hundreds of steps changes little between steps, so most of the previous step can be reused. The recurring lesson, stated over and over, is that the arithmetic was never the bottleneck — almost every paper's real contribution is a cheaper way to find, store, move, and schedule the scattered survivors.

**Connects to:** Sparsity is the deepest expression of "move less data." Its outlier and low-bit tricks overlap **smaller numbers** (T2); its skipping of unimportant notebook pages is a pillar of **attention** (T1). Its Mixture-of-Experts models lean hard on **interconnect** (T4) to shuffle experts and on the **memory wall** theme's (T3) tiering to stage them. And its dirty secret — that the bookkeeping around irregular data can dominate everything — is exactly the problem the **compiler** (T6) and **specialized hardware** (T9) exist to solve, deciding per-input whether being sparse even beats doing the dense work.

## T6 — Describe hardware so a machine can build it

The specific face of the constraint: a person writes down *what* they want computed, as a kind of wish, and a translator must turn that wish into the exact ordered steps a specific chip runs — choosing an order that touches far-away memory as little as possible.

That translator is a *compiler*, and for AI its central job is arranging billions of repetitive multiply-and-add operations over huge grids of numbers so the arithmetic units stay fed. The two workhorse moves both reduce to "touch memory less." *Fusion* glues back-to-back operations together so an intermediate result stays on the fast workbench instead of being shipped to memory and immediately fetched back. *Reuse* arranges the work so that once a number is on the workbench, every operation needing it happens before it leaves. The difference between a naive plan and a clever one is not a few percent — it is often the difference between running on a phone versus needing a datacenter — and writing good plans by hand for every model and chip is a soul-crushing amount of labor, which is why automating it is the reason the field exists.

Two complications keep it from ever being finished. First, there is no single "the chip": a plan brilliant on a flexible graphics-style processor is terrible on a custom accelerator, so much of the work searches an astronomical space of possible plans, guided by a fast *cost model* that predicts a plan's performance without running it. Second, and newer, the wish is increasingly not fixed — a language model's work depends on how long your prompt is, a graph program on the shape of its input — so the plan sometimes must be chosen at the last second, when the real data's shape is finally known. The most ambitious end of the theme reads its title literally: the compiler's output is not a plan for an existing chip but the *design of a new chip*, emitting a manufacturable circuit from a high-level description. That corner carries the field's most concrete results, because "we generated a working chip" is hard to fake.

**Connects to:** The compiler is the connective tissue of the whole collection — it is how every algorithmic idea becomes something a real datapath runs. It implements the fusion and reuse that fight the **memory wall** (T3); it chooses how to split a model across chips for **interconnect** (T4); it generates the code that keeps **smaller numbers** (T2) small and routes **sparse** (T5) work to whichever hardware wins. Its hardware-generation end is the tool that builds the **specialized** accelerators (T9), and its reliability-flavored cousins prove that a rewrite preserves meaning, feeding straight into **reliability** (T8).

## T7 — When the shortcuts leak

The specific face of the constraint: the very shortcuts a chip uses to move data faster — remembering where you looked, keeping a private stash of recent numbers, guessing which way a branch will go — each leave a physical mark, and an attacker who cannot read your secret directly can reconstruct it from that mark's shadow.

To move data efficiently, a chip cheats. It keeps a private stash of recently-used numbers close to the arithmetic, and that stash is warm where you touched it and cold where you didn't. It trains a guess-machine on what you did. It runs ahead speculatively on branches, leaving footprints even for work it later throws away. Each cheat is a shortcut, and each shortcut betrays. This is the family of *side channels*: the machine keeps its promise never to hand your secret to a stranger, then leaks it through how fast it runs — which shelf is warm, how long something took, how the guessing behaves.

Beneath the timing leaks sits a brutally physical one. Main memory is a warehouse of microscopic charge buckets packed so densely that repeatedly slamming one row can jostle charge out of its neighbors, flipping a bit in a row you were never allowed to touch — *Rowhammer*. It is not a software bug you can patch; it is the price of density, and it lets permitted code in one corner of a shared machine physically corrupt a co-tenant's data. The defenses are themselves physical machinery bolted onto the memory path — extra counters, extra encryption — and, being physical, they move data and take time, so they acquire their own shadows: several papers show the very shields built to stop one attack open a fresh timing channel of their own. The stakes rose sharply because we no longer own our machines; we rent slices of enormous shared ones, and send our most valuable models and private data to run on hardware we don't control, resting the whole business on a promise that the secret stays safe even there.

**Connects to:** Security is the dark mirror of the entire collection — every data-movement shortcut the other themes celebrate is an attack surface here. The **memory wall's** (T3) dense memory and compute-in-memory schemes are precisely what make Rowhammer worse. **Attention's** (T1) running notes and **specialized hardware's** (T9) accelerators are exactly the valuable secrets that confidential-computing enclaves try to seal — at a heavy tax on the very data movement that dominates a model's runtime. And it shares deep tooling with **reliability** (T8): both hunt for holes that no human can find by reading a design, through fuzzing and formal proof.

## T8 — Knowing the answer is right

The specific face of the constraint: correctness is mostly a property of the elaborate data-moving machinery, not of the arithmetic — and the more we spread data out to keep the arithmetic fed, the more places it can get stale, lost, reordered, or silently corrupted.

The unsettling fact is that a chip does not know when it is wrong. When a microscopic switch has a flaw or gets nudged by heat, the arithmetic can come out subtly wrong while every status light stays green — a *silent data corruption*, silent because there is no crash, just a wrong number flowing downstream into everything computed next. At the scale of modern fleets, even a one-in-a-billion mistake happens constantly. And the hardest correctness problems live not in the arithmetic but in the data-moving machinery: when two chips each keep a private copy of a shared number, a rulebook must guarantee they agree on its current value — and one paper proved that the official industry rulebook the whole field trusted contained genuine holes, leading to five corrections to the standard.

The approaches span a spectrum from statistical to absolute. At one end, watch millions of machines for the ripples a corruption leaves — the crashes and log anomalies that fish out a defective chip — a dragnet that works on real hardware today but by definition cannot catch the corruption that leaves no trace. At the other end, *formal verification*: translate a design into precise logic and prove mathematically that a class of mistake cannot happen, ever, on any input — the strongest assurance there is, but costing person-years and not yet stretching to the largest systems. In between sit the practical arts of surviving failures rather than preventing them (redundancy, graceful degradation, continuous snapshots), getting the ordering right when many parts must agree, and reasoning across ugly boundaries like a power loss mid-write. The stakes grew with AI: training one large model can mean tens of thousands of chips running flat-out for months, where a single silently-wrong number injected early quietly poisons the entire result with no crash to warn you.

**Connects to:** Reliability is the quiet guarantee under everything else. It underwrites **interconnect** (T4) — the coherence rulebooks that keep chips agreeing on shared data, and the fault detection that spots a failing part in a thousand-chip run. It shares its formal-proof and fuzzing machinery with **security** (T7), since both must find holes no human can see by reading. Its silent-corruption worries fall hardest on the dense memory of the **memory wall** (T3) and on the months-long training runs that **interconnect** and **compiler** (T6) systems make possible. And when a **compiler** rewrites a circuit to move less data, it is reliability's job to prove the rewrite preserved the meaning.

## T9 — Build the machine around the problem

The specific face of the constraint: the flexible general-purpose chip is a bargain — ready for anything, perfect for nothing — and for a growing set of workloads its shape is simply wrong, because their data-movement pattern is nothing like the mainstream case the rest of the field optimizes.

The flexible chip was a deal we struck to avoid designing new hardware for every problem. This theme is a catalog of the problems for which that deal no longer pays. Computing on secret data without unlocking it bloats a single number into a bundle of thousands, so movement utterly swamps the math. Drawing lifelike 3D scenes from millions of soft translucent blobs touches memory in a scattered, uneven way that a general chip hates. A quantum computer inverts the whole premise: its enemy is not data movement but that the qubit forgets its value in a flicker, so the machine spends most of its effort correcting errors under a merciless deadline, propped up by a supporting cast of ordinary electronics. What unites these is not the application but the recognition that real gains — tens to thousands of times faster — come only from building the machine around the true structure of the problem, and above all around its data movement.

The moves form a spectrum. Move the computing to where the data sits (in and near memory). Build one machine perfectly shaped for one problem — fastest of all, but frozen and brittle when the algorithm changes. Make the custom machine bendable, so it fits a whole family of related tasks. Rewrite the problem in software so there is less work to move. Co-design the algorithm and the hardware together, reshaping the algorithm into a regular, local form the hardware can run easily. And, at the boldest edge, compute with a different physical medium entirely — light, analog charge, or the timing of pulses — letting the physics perform the arithmetic almost for free, at the cost of the noise and imprecision that digital computing spent decades escaping.

**Connects to:** Specialized hardware is where the collection's co-design reflex is most literal. It builds the accelerators the **compiler** (T6) learns to generate and target. Its in-memory and near-memory designs are the sharp end of the **memory wall** (T3). It bakes **smaller numbers** (T2) and **sparsity** (T5) directly into silicon for the largest efficiency wins. It gives **attention** (T1) its custom serving engines. And its persistent tension — the more perfectly a design fits today's problem, the faster it is orphaned when the problem shifts — is the same durability-versus-specialization bet that runs through the whole field.

## T0 — The other drawer

The specific face of the constraint: the important things that don't fit the other drawers, which turn out — when you lay them out — to be the same one root problem wearing different costumes.

A tool that squeezes scientific data to a third its size is fighting data movement: less to move means less waiting. A system that trains a giant model across a dozen buildings on a slow internet link is fighting data movement: it cannot afford to shout every number to every building on every step, so it lets each site work alone for a long stretch before comparing notes, cutting communication by hundreds of times. A trick that renders a video-game frame at full detail only where your eye happens to be looking is fighting data movement: why compute and shuttle pixels no one will see? A method that stops a training run from keeping three redundant copies of every housekeeping number is fighting data movement: fewer copies fit in fast memory instead of spilling to slow. Even trimming the dead code out of a bloated software library is the same instinct — carry less, this time less program rather than less data.

Running quietly beneath all of it is a second, sharper thread: how do we even know any of this is working? When you test a new machine on many jobs you get many speedup numbers, and to summarize them you must average — and the average nearly everyone has used for decades is, in a precise mathematical sense, the wrong one, capable of ranking a slower machine above a faster one. Alongside it sits an honesty about cost: a chip's true price is not only the electricity it burns running but the pollution baked into manufacturing it, and optimizing only for the running cost lets you "win" by building a bigger, dirtier chip. These papers do not make anything faster; they fix the ruler. But a bent ruler is worse than none, because it makes you confident in the wrong direction.

**Connects to:** This drawer touches everything precisely because it *is* everything, generalized. Its compression and communication-light training are **interconnect** (T4) and **smaller numbers** (T2) seen from the systems level. Its do-less rendering is **sparsity** (T5). And its honest-measurement thread is the meta-layer over the entire collection: every speedup claimed in every other theme is trustworthy only if the ruler is straight — which is exactly the same worry the **reliability** theme (T8) raises about knowing an answer is right.

## Where it all points

Read all 946 papers together and a clear shape emerges. The field has genuinely conquered the crude waste. Keeping and paging the running notes on demand, reusing shared prefixes, computing attention with tiled routines that never spill to slow memory, shrinking weights to 4 bits and activations to 8, hiding communication underneath computation on regular training runs, caching and prefetching the predictable memory streams — these are no longer research. They ship in the systems running today, and they jointly deliver order-of-magnitude gains. The co-design reflex — fusing an algorithmic idea directly into the datapath and its schedule rather than treating them as separable layers — is now the field's universal instinct, and much of what used to demand hand-tuned expert code is automated by compilers and solvers. Formal proof, for the first time, has reached real commercial artifacts and exposed genuine bugs in shipping industry standards.

What is being actively solved right now is the honest accounting. A recurring, healthy skepticism runs through the newest work: quantization and compression speedups that looked spectacular on clean benchmarks shrink or vanish once measured inside a full serving stack at realistic batch sizes; secure-speculation defenses that looked cheap on paper cost more than a third of performance once built down to real circuits; a real compute-in-memory chip is still bandwidth-bound without careful dataflow; and the field's own default statistic for averaging speedups is mathematically wrong. This turn toward measuring the right thing, inside the real system, is one of the most mature signals in the collection.

What remains wide open clusters in a few honest places. The irregular and input-dependent workloads — graphs, sparse math, Mixture-of-Experts, recommendation — break the clean tricks that work so well on dense, regular transformers, and there is still no general theory of how much data movement is truly avoidable or which tactic to reach for. Truly enormous contexts, hundreds of thousands to a million words, still strain memory capacity and push people toward exotic answers like computing inside memory and storage, which work in hand-tuned demonstrations but are not yet commodity. The most radical reframing — moving the math to the data — delivers spectacular wins in narrow niches but has no consensus on which niche will generalize, immature tools, and brutally tight room for logic in memory chips. The exotic physical media — light, analog charge, timing — promise extraordinary efficiency but are still fighting noise, still narrow specialists. And two gaps sit underneath everything: the software supply chain beneath AI models is a wide-open attack surface with demonstrated attacks and no deployed defense, and nobody yet has proof-grade certainty at fleet-grade scale and cost.

The deepest truth the aggregate reveals is that the constraint itself is not going away, and the field has finally, fully accepted that. For the first time in the history of computing, the entire discipline is reorganizing around a single diagnosis: **the bottleneck is the movement of data, not the doing of arithmetic.** The arithmetic keeps getting cheaper; the wires stubbornly do not. Every jump in model size shifts the balance and reopens questions that looked closed. So the work is not converging on a finish line. It is a treadmill run with real purpose — nine communities, one constraint, discovering workload by workload exactly how far the wall can be pushed.
