# Characterizing and Optimizing Realistic Workloads on a Commercial Compute-in-SRAM Device

**Venue:** MICRO  
**Confidence:** high (full-text analysis)

## Problem
Prior compute-in-SRAM evaluations rely on simulators or small prototypes, so the real-world performance and energy efficiency of commercial devices under realistic AI workloads (particularly retrieval-augmented generation over large corpora) is not well understood, and programming principles for exploiting the architecture's 2-million bit-processor parallelism are largely unknown.

## Motivation
Compute-in-SRAM architectures achieve massive on-chip bandwidth (26 TB/s on the GSI APU vs. 7 TB/s for A100 GPU) at far lower power, making them attractive for memory-bound AI workloads, but extracting this potential requires non-obvious data layout and movement optimizations that existing analytical models do not capture.

## Method
The paper tests a commercial GSI APU—a processor with 2 million 1-bit processors, 500 MHz speed, 25 TOPS compute power, 26 TB/s on-chip bandwidth, and a memory hierarchy (12MB L1, 64KB L2, 1MB L3, 16GB main memory)—using realistic AI workloads (Phoenix benchmark suite) and matrix tests. The researchers built a Python tool to identify where data movement creates bottlenecks across the memory hierarchy, then validated three optimizations on real hardware: (1) rerouting expensive data through cheaper scalar-vector operations (multiplying a single value by many values) instead of slow parallel reductions, (2) eliminating redundant data copies that accumulate across loop iterations, and (3) shrinking broadcast lookup table sizes from K×N to N entries to reduce latency. All three were demonstrated on information retrieval tasks over 10-200GB datasets.

## Key Novelty
This is the first comprehensive real-hardware evaluation of a commercial compute-in-SRAM processor (processors that compute directly with stored data: GSI APU) on realistic AI workloads, showing that even with this architecture, data movement becomes the bottleneck without optimization, and demonstrating three optimizations reaching maximum performance.

## Contributions
- First comprehensive real-hardware performance and energy characterization of a commercial compute-in-SRAM device (GSI APU) under Phoenix benchmarks, matrix multiplication, and RAG workloads
- Analytical framework modeling non-uniform data movement costs and hierarchical reduction latencies for general-purpose compute-in-SRAM architectures
- Three data movement and layout optimizations: communication-aware reduction mapping, DMA coalescing, and broadcast-friendly layouts
- 4.8x-6.6x retrieval speedup over CPU and GPU-matching latency for RAG at 54.4x-117.9x lower energy than NVIDIA A6000

## Technique Categories

## Workloads
LLM-inference (RAG), database (Phoenix benchmarks)

## Metrics
- Retrieval speedup: 4.8x-6.6x over optimized CPU baseline
- End-to-end RAG latency: 1.1x-1.8x improvement; matches NVIDIA A6000 GPU latency
- Energy efficiency: 54.4x-117.9x reduction vs. NVIDIA A6000 for RAG
- Phoenix benchmarks: 41.8x speedup over CPU

## Baselines
- Intel Xeon Gold 6230R CPU
- NVIDIA A6000 GPU

## Limitations
The off-chip (DDR4/HBM) memory bandwidth is modeled via simulation rather than measured; results apply to the specific GSI APU architecture and generalization to other compute-in-SRAM designs requires re-profiling the analytical framework.
