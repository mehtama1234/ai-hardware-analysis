MLSys 2025 · 61 papers · from first principles

Feeding the machine

Modern AI models now want far more than any single chip can give. Nearly every paper at this conference is one more way to close that gap.

the one problem

A chip can multiply faster than it can remember

Everything below is a consequence of a single physical fact. To understand the whole field, you only need this.

A language model does one small thing, billions of times: it takes your words and multiplies them through an enormous grid of numbers to guess the next word. Doing that well depends on two very different resources.

The first is arithmetic — how many multiplications the chip can do per second. Modern chips have this in staggering abundance.

The second is memory — both how much fits close to the chip, and how fast numbers can be shuttled in and out. This is the scarce one, and it has barely improved while models exploded in size.

So the deepest truth of the field: AI is starved for data movement, not math. The chip sits idle, waiting to be fed.

Reading a prompt ≠ writing an answer

Reading your whole prompt at once is heavy on arithmetic and keeps the chip busy. Writing the answer happens one word at a time, each word barely any math but a fresh trip through memory — so the chip mostly waits. These two jobs are so different that splitting them apart is one of the biggest organizing ideas in the corpus.

The model's growing notebook

As it writes, the model keeps a scratchpad of everything seen so far so it needn't recompute. That notebook — the KV cache — grows with every word and every extra user, and quickly becomes the single biggest thing hogging memory. A large share of these papers are, at heart, about this one notebook: shrinking it, reusing it, or streaming it elsewhere.

Once you see the problem this way, the 61 papers stop being a list and become two moves, repeated in a dozen costumes: either shrink what must be moved and computed, or get more useful work out of the hardware you already have. Here is how.

Eight ways to close the gap

Each is grounded in the actual papers, with their headline result in plain terms. The pill says which side of the problem the move works on.

Do the core math without wasting the chip

The one operation a model does most is attention — and while writing an answer it left the chip up to 80% idle, with a different hand-written version for every model. These papers rewrite it so the chip stays full, and fold dozens of variants into a single flexible engine.

02

Use smaller numbers

Every number in the model is normally stored in 16 bits. Round them down to 4. There is simply less to move and cheaper to multiply — provided the rounding doesn't corrupt the answer, and the tiny numbers can run on the chip's fast math units instead of its slow ones.

connecting the dots

Four instincts underneath all of it

Strip the eight moves down further and the same four reflexes appear again and again — the shared worldview of the field.

I.

The wall between software and chip is gone

No one optimizes "the algorithm" and "the hardware" separately anymore. The sparsity pattern, the number format, the attention variant are each folded directly into the chip's math units and their scheduling. Co-design is the default, not the exception.

II.

Reading and writing are different machines

Again and again, prompt-reading and answer-writing are pulled apart and handled as two distinct jobs — separate schedules, separate chips, separate parallel strategies — because their appetites for math and memory are opposite.

III.

Always hide the slow thing behind the fast thing

Whether the slow thing is an SSD, the CPU's memory, or the network between chips, the template never changes: overlap the slow transfer with useful compute so its cost disappears into time you were spending anyway.

IV.

Decide at runtime, don't hard-code

Fixed heuristics are the recurring villain. Adaptive skipping budgets, deadline-aware scheduling, plans that reshape mid-flight — the field keeps replacing static rules with decisions made from what's actually happening right now.

the honest part

What the whole field is quietly not doing

A picture that only shows the wins is a brochure. Three blind spots run across the corpus.

It is a one-vendor world

Almost every result is on NVIDIA silicon, benchmarked against other NVIDIA software. There is essentially no TPU, no AMD GPU — one edge NPU and one custom chip in 61 papers. "Portable" mostly means portable between NVIDIA backends.

Energy is the reason, not the measurement

Power is the stated motivation for much of this work, yet only the handful of edge papers actually report joules or energy-per-op. The datacenter work reports speed and throughput and stays silent on the electricity bill it claims to care about.

Reported is not realized

One paper (007) is the field's conscience: it shows compression speedups that look great in isolation can vanish, or turn negative, once dropped into a real serving stack under real load. Many headline numbers come from the gentlest possible setting.
