# DATE 2025: Design, Automation and Test at the Edge of Physical Reality

## What DATE Is

DATE—Design, Automation and Test in Europe—occupies a peculiar and essential position in the hardware research landscape: it is the broadest venue in the chip-design stack. While ICCAD is the premier conference for CAD algorithms applied to physical design (placement, routing, timing closure), DATE is the full-spectrum venue. It spans from transistor-level circuit design, through EDA tool methodology, power management, verification and testing, embedded systems, hardware security, automotive real-time systems, and emerging compute substrates. It is to the chip lifecycle what systems conferences are to software: the place where researchers grapple with the intersection of algorithm, tool, and physics.

DATE is organized by EDAA (European Design and Automation Association), ETS (European Test Symposium), and ACM/IEEE, and it is unambiguously European in character and reach. This matters. The European embedded systems and automotive industries have particular needs—deterministic real-time execution, long product lifetimes, severe power budgets, extreme reliability requirements—that shape what DATE covers. You will find automotive safety papers at DATE that would never appear at ICCAD, because ICCAD is about tool algorithms and DATE is about whether the thing actually works when it ships. You will find neuromorphic and photonic papers at DATE that barely appear elsewhere, because the European research base is unusually deep in emerging substrates. You will find papers on AI chip security at DATE that don't appear at DAC or ICCAD, because the European security research community is stronger than it is in the US.

The tripartite mission—design, automation, and test—is not a slogan; it is a constraint on what DATE accepts. "Design" means circuit-level innovation, architecture choices, or system-level trade-offs. "Automation" means the tools, algorithms, or methodologies that make design tractable at scale. "Test" means verification (before tape-out) and testing (after manufacture), including both correctness validation and quality assurance. A paper that is purely theoretical (a new SAT solver algorithm) might appear at ICCAD or CAV, but only if it closes the loop to physical design practice. A paper that is purely about embedded systems scheduling would go to RTAS or OSDI, but only if it has hardware implications. DATE accepts papers that live at these intersections.

The researchers who publish at DATE are: circuit designers building ASICs and SoCs for specific domains (edge AI, automotive, sensors); EDA tool researchers building design automation infrastructure; hardware security researchers studying side channels and fault injection on real silicon; embedded systems researchers working on real-time properties in the presence of physical variation; and increasingly, researchers pushing into novel compute substrates (photonic, cryogenic, neuromorphic, spintronic) where the design methodology hasn't been formalized yet. It is a venue that bridges industry and academia in a way that is rare in computer science—industry contributors bring real product constraints, and academic researchers bring radical new ideas that won't ship for five years.

## The One Problem This Community Is Organized Around

All of DATE is organized around a single, brutally practical problem: the design-correctness gap. This is the distance between what a designer intends and what a manufactured chip actually delivers.

When you design a circuit, you intend certain behavior under certain conditions. You intend your 7nm ASIC to operate correctly at all process corners (slow-slow, fast-fast, slow-fast, etc.), across all operating temperatures (typically −40°C to 125°C for industrial, −40°C to 150°C for automotive), across the voltage range supplied to the chip (typically 0.75V to 0.95V for low-power SoCs), and across the entire lifetime of the device (often 10+ years for automotive). You intend your design to resist soft errors caused by cosmic rays or alpha particles in the packaging. You intend your AI accelerator to produce bitwise-correct results on every input image, or to degrade gracefully in a way you can control. You intend your system to meet real-time deadlines even when running multiple processes concurrently. And increasingly, you intend your design to resist an adversary who controls the deployment environment: an attacker with physical access, or an attacker who can induce faults via electromagnetic injection or laser-fault injection.

The design-correctness gap arises from three fundamentally different sources, and DATE's research community has developed specialized techniques for each.

**Physical variation** is the oldest source. When silicon is manufactured, no two transistors are identical. The gate oxide thickness varies from chip to chip and within a chip. The dopant density fluctuates randomly. The interconnect resistance and capacitance vary due to line-edge roughness and thickness variation. At 7nm and below, these variations are large enough that nominal (typical-case) simulation severely underestimates the spread of circuit behavior. A circuit that meets timing at typical-case may fail at slow-slow corner. Worse, process variation is increasingly difficult to predict: process design kits (PDKs) provided by foundries have enormous margins because foundries cannot guarantee sub-micron precision. The result is a widespread design practice of "design for the worst case," which means leaving huge margins on the table—power, area, performance—because you're targeting a worst case that may never actually occur. DATE's circuit-design papers (HiFlex, WinAcc, BOSON-1) all grapple with variation: HiFlex dynamically adapts to process corners; WinAcc exploits the observation that many workloads (DNNs with Laplace-distributed activations) don't need worst-case margins; BOSON-1 uses inverse design to find photonic structures that are robust to variation by design.

**Adversarial threats** are newer and increasingly important. A chip in the field might be attacked by an adversary with physical access: an attacker with a laser can induce faults by creating electron-hole pairs at specific locations in the silicon. An attacker with electromagnetic injection can induce faults by coupling energy into the power distribution network or clock distribution. An attacker monitoring power consumption can extract secrets via side-channel analysis. An attacker can plant Trojans in the supply chain. The DATE 2025 corpus has unusual depth in this area: edge-AI processor security papers covering multi-partner projects on side-channel attacks and fault injection; adversarial noise prediction for safety-critical image segmentation (what happens when an attacker adds adversarial perturbations to the image input to a safety-critical neural network?); formal verification of analog neural networks under device mismatch (a form of adversarial perturbation). The adversarial threat model is qualitatively different from soft-error reliability: a soft error is random and rare; an adversarial fault is targeted and can be repeated. This means you need to think about your design differently. A circuit that occasionally fails due to cosmic rays is acceptable if the system can detect and recover. A circuit that consistently fails when attacked by laser injection needs redesign at the algorithmic level.

**System complexity** is the third source, and it is intertwined with the previous two. Modern chips are not monolithic; they are composites of heterogeneous compute elements (CPUs, GPUs, ML accelerators, I/O controllers) connected by elaborate cache-coherence protocols and memory hierarchies. The 2025 corpus includes c2c-gem5, a simulator for cache-coherent chip-to-chip interconnects in chiplet designs; this paper exists because chiplet-based designs have introduced new verification challenges. When you have two dies connected via a high-speed, low-latency interconnect, the cache-coherence protocol must work correctly across the die boundary. This is a new problem that ICCAD doesn't address—ICCAD optimizes a single die, but DATE has to verify that your chiplet system actually maintains cache coherence under all conditions, including when one die is slower than the other or when interposer propagation delay is asymmetric. Modern embedded systems also face strict real-time requirements: automotive systems need to respond to sensor inputs within hard deadlines. When your SoC is running multiple processes concurrently, meeting those deadlines requires discipline in scheduling, cache allocation, and memory bandwidth partitioning. The AUTOSAR Adaptive papers at DATE 2025 address this: how do you guarantee deterministic execution when the OS is running adaptive/dynamic processes (not fixed-priority, not periodic)? This is a problem that operating systems researchers would ignore (they care about average latency), but DATE cares because missing a deadline in an automotive system can kill people.

## The Verification-and-Test Frontier

The defining characteristic that separates DATE from ICCAD or DAC is its emphasis on verification and test. This is not academic: verification and test are the bottlenecks in modern chip design. At ICCAD, the assumption is that a correct RTL design will produce a correct manufactured chip, as long as you apply the right place-and-route algorithms. At DATE, the reality is that RTL correctness is necessary but nowhere near sufficient. You need to verify that the physical circuit, under realistic process variation, will exhibit the correct behavior. You need to test the manufactured chip to filter out defects. And you need to do this increasingly efficiently, because test data volume is exploding.

The 2025 corpus shows four distinct frontiers in verification.

**Formal verification of analog and mixed-signal circuits** is emerging as a distinct subdiscipline. The paper on formal verification of analog neural networks under device mismatch is representative: the problem is that analog circuits are inherently continuous, while formal verification tools are built for discrete, digital systems. The naive approach is to abstract the analog circuit into a digital one, but this loses fidelity and can hide real bugs. The actual approach is to use techniques from reachability analysis and abstract interpretation: model the analog circuit as a hybrid automaton (a system that has both continuous and discrete state), then use set-based reachability to compute the set of voltages/currents that the circuit can reach over all possible device parameters. If the reachable set doesn't include any states that violate the specification, the circuit is proven correct. This requires enormous computational power (reachability in analog circuits is NP-hard), and it only works for small circuits, but it is the only way to prove correctness of analog circuits without simulation.

**SAT sampling and GPU-accelerated verification** represents a different frontier. The paper on "High-throughput SAT sampling via GPU-accelerated gradient circuits" points to a fundamental change in how verification is being done. Instead of traditional SAT solving (which finds a single assignment that satisfies a formula), the problem is to sample uniformly from the space of assignments that satisfy a circuit behavior specification. Why? Because you want to generate test vectors that exercise all corner cases, and true random sampling is too slow. By using GPU-accelerated gradient circuits (essentially, treating SAT as an optimization problem and using gradient descent on analog hardware), you can generate many diverse test vectors per second. This is still a research area, but it points toward a future where verification leverages accelerators in the same way that simulation does.

**Soft-error analysis on vector ISAs** is a third frontier. The 2025 corpus includes papers on soft-error assessment in ARM SVE and RISC-V RVV vector ISA kernels. This is important because vector ISAs are increasingly prevalent (AWS Graviton processors have SVE, RISC-V is gaining traction in embedded systems), and a soft error in a vector register affects multiple elements simultaneously. A soft error in a scalar register flips one bit of one value; a soft error in a 256-bit vector register can flip one bit in each of 32 double-precision values simultaneously. The consequence is that you need to re-analyze the soft-error vulnerability of your code when you vectorize it. The DATE papers on this do architectural soft-error injection (deliberately inject faults into simulated processors running real code, measure what fraction of faults cause incorrect results) and then correlate with code properties (vector width, stride, access patterns). This is necessary because simulation alone is too slow to characterize soft-error behavior for large programs.

**Chiplet verification** via c2c-gem5 is a fourth frontier. Chiplet-based designs (multiple dies connected via interposer) create verification challenges that monolithic designs don't have. The cache-coherence protocol must work correctly when memory accesses can take different latencies depending on whether they're on-die or off-die. The simulation infrastructure must model the interposer propagation delay, the link-level protocol, and the coherence state machine simultaneously. c2c-gem5 extends the gem5 processor simulator to support cache-coherent connections between simulated chips, allowing researchers to verify that their chiplet system maintains coherence under all scenarios.

The deeper point is this: verification is shifting from RTL-level concerns (does my HDL implement my algorithm correctly?) to physical-level and system-level concerns (does my chip, as manufactured and deployed, actually deliver correct results?). This shift is necessary because the sources of incorrectness have changed. Twenty years ago, most failures were due to design bugs (incorrect RTL). Today, most challenges are due to physical variation, soft errors, power delivery noise, and adversarial threats—all of which manifest at the physical level or system level, not at the RTL level. A correct RTL design can still fail in silicon, and DATE is the venue where researchers are developing the tools to catch these failures before tape-out.

## The Security-Reliability Nexus

One of the most striking aspects of DATE 2025 is the convergence of security and reliability research. These are traditionally separate fields. Reliability engineers care about random faults (cosmic rays, thermal noise, electromigration). Security researchers care about adversarial faults (attackers with lasers or EM injection capabilities). But DATE 2025 makes clear that this distinction is eroding.

The reason is edge deployment. When a chip is deployed in an edge device (a camera in a public space, a sensor in a vehicle, a smart speaker in a home), it is subject to two kinds of stress: (1) random environmental stress (heat, vibration, radiation), and (2) potential adversarial manipulation. An attacker with physical access to an edge device can perform fault injection. An attacker with control of the input data can perform adversarial perturbation attacks. A designer who is serious about correctness must defend against both.

**Soft error under adversarial fault injection** is the core of this nexus. Consider an ML accelerator running an image classifier. A random soft error might flip one bit in one weight matrix, reducing accuracy by a small amount. But if an attacker uses laser-fault injection to deliberately flip specific bits—say, bits in the output layer bias of the classifier—they can potentially force misclassification on demand. A security-conscious designer needs to defend against this. The defense mechanisms are familiar from both domains: error-correcting codes (traditional reliability), redundant execution and voting (reliability and security), and architectural design that limits the damage from any single fault (security and reliability). DATE 2025 papers on edge-AI processor security take this holistic view: they measure vulnerability under both random faults and directed fault injection, then propose defenses that handle both threat models.

**Adversarial perturbation in image segmentation** is a second manifestation. Consider a safety-critical image segmentation network (used in autonomous vehicles or medical imaging). A random soft error might produce a slightly incorrect segmentation. An adversarial attacker might add imperceptible perturbations to the input image designed to cause segmentation failure. The DATE paper on adversarial noise prediction asks: if you know that your image segmentation algorithm is vulnerable to adversarial perturbations, can you predict where that vulnerability is and defend against it at the hardware level? One approach: add noise to the network's computations in a way that is correlated with the adversarial noise, so that the network learns to be robust. This is a hardware-software co-design problem: you're adding noise to the hardware datapath intentionally, in order to improve robustness to adversarial inputs. This makes sense from a reliability perspective (you're injecting faults intentionally) and a security perspective (you're defending against adversarial inputs).

**AUTOSAR Adaptive for automotive real-time systems** represents the system-level manifestation of this nexus. AUTOSAR (Automotive Open System Architecture) is the standardized middleware for automotive systems. The traditional AUTOSAR was based on fixed-priority scheduling: each task has a priority, and the scheduler always runs the highest-priority ready task. This is deterministic and analyzable, but it is rigid: you can't easily add new functionality or change priorities without re-analyzing the entire system. AUTOSAR Adaptive allows dynamic scheduling: tasks can have variable priority, can spawn new tasks, can request resources dynamically. The challenge is maintaining determinism and timeliness in the presence of this dynamism.

The DATE papers on AUTOSAR Adaptive present the SL-LET (Slot-Level Logical Execution Time) paradigm. The idea is to decompose the system into logical time slots, where within each slot, a task executes with bounded latency (the Logical Execution Time). The hardware and OS are co-designed so that the actual execution time is tightly bounded and predictable. This ensures that even with dynamic scheduling, the system meets hard real-time deadlines. This is a reliability property (meeting deadlines), but it is also increasingly a security property: deterministic timing makes the system less vulnerable to timing-side-channel attacks.

**Novel compute substrates and reliability unknowns** are a third thread. The 2025 corpus includes papers on skyrmion logic-in-memory (TrackScorer), dendritic neuromorphic computation, and cryogenic quantum co-design. These are emerging substrates where the reliability envelope is poorly understood. A skyrmion is a magnetic texture in ferromagnetic materials; logic operations are performed by moving skyrmions around on a substrate. The reliability questions are: how often do skyrmion motions fail? How sensitive is the system to thermal noise? To magnetic fields? The DATE papers on these substrates are trying to characterize reliability while the substrate is still in the research phase, precisely so that designers can factor reliability into their architectural decisions early.

The convergence of security and reliability at DATE reflects a deeper truth: the design-correctness gap is no longer just about random faults. It is about ensuring correctness in the presence of all sources of deviation from the ideal: process variation, environmental stress, random faults, and adversarial attack. A designer who ignores any of these sources is vulnerable.

## Approximate and Adaptive Compute

DATE has a distinct tradition of approximate computing that runs deeper than simple precision reduction. The core insight is that many applications (image processing, machine learning, sensor data analysis) have inherent tolerance for approximation: they can produce slightly wrong results as long as the error is bounded and rare. By exploiting this tolerance at the hardware level, designers can achieve 30-40% energy savings with minimal accuracy loss.

The 2025 corpus shows three distinct directions in approximate computing.

**Exploiting domain-specific structure** is exemplified by WinAcc (Block Floating Point accumulator with Laplace-distribution insight). The problem is that DNN inference requires high-throughput accumulation of many products. The naive approach is to use high-precision (32-bit float) accumulators, which consumes significant area and energy. WinAcc observes that DNN activations have Laplace-distributed values: many activations are close to zero, with exponentially-decreasing tail. This means that the accumulator doesn't need uniform precision across the range of possible values; it can use higher precision near zero (where many values cluster) and lower precision in the tails (where values are rare). By designing the accumulator to exploit this skewed distribution, WinAcc achieves lower precision with the same accuracy as 32-bit float. This is not just "use 16-bit" approximation; it is "understand the statistics of your workload and design hardware to match those statistics."

**Dynamic compression during inference** is exemplified by Dancer (dynamic compression for graph convolutional networks). The observation is that different layers of a GCN have different tolerance for approximation: early layers are robust to significant compression, while later layers need higher precision. Dancer dynamically adjusts compression ratio per layer based on the input graph: if the input graph is highly structured, the early layers can use more aggressive compression; if the input is noisy or unstructured, Dancer backs off. This requires hardware that can switch compression modes on the fly, which is non-trivial, but the energy savings (40%+) justify the complexity.

**Semantic redundancy in training** is exemplified by Slipstream (semantic-based training acceleration for recommendation models). The problem is that training recommendation models (which have to ingest millions of user interactions) is computationally intensive. Slipstream observes that many training samples are semantically redundant: if you've already processed a sample from user A who clicked on similar items as user B, processing the full sample from user B provides little additional learning signal. By intelligently skipping or downsampling redundant samples during training, Slipstream accelerates training 2-3x with minimal convergence degradation. The hardware implications are that you need to detect redundancy (requires understanding the model semantics) and skip computation (requires dynamic control flow in the datapath), but the energy savings make it worthwhile.

The deeper insight is that approximation is not just about precision reduction; it is about co-designing algorithms, hardware, and verification. Once you allow approximation, you must answer: what does "correctness" mean for an approximate circuit? The traditional answer is: produce the same result as an infinite-precision reference. But for an approximate circuit, the answer is: produce a result within some error tolerance (e.g., top-1 accuracy within 1% of reference, or L2 error below a threshold). This changes how you test and verify. You can't use traditional deterministic test vectors (which check for bitwise correctness); you need statistical test frameworks that measure error distribution.

DATE's tradition is to integrate this verification challenge into the design from the start. The papers on approximate computing always include a characterization of error distribution: they run inference on a large test set, measure the accuracy loss, and show that it is acceptable. This is more rigorous than the approximation work at other venues (where researchers often claim approximation works without detailed error characterization). It reflects the design-correctness philosophy: you can't claim your approximate circuit is correct until you have measured the errors.

## Physical Design at the Edge of Convention

ICCAD's physical design papers optimize known structures: algorithms for placement, routing, timing closure, congestion management. The assumption is that the design is RTL-complete and the PDK is mature. DATE's physical design papers often operate at the boundary of these assumptions: they deal with novel substrates (photonic, cryogenic, spintronic, neuromorphic) where the PDK doesn't exist yet, or where the design methodology hasn't been formalized.

**BOSON-1: Photonic inverse design with variation awareness** exemplifies the challenge. Photonic integrated circuits (PICs) are increasingly important for edge computing: they are energy-efficient, have high I/O bandwidth, and can perform optical computation. But PICs are fundamentally different from electronic circuits. Instead of designing transistors and wires, you design waveguides and optical couplers. The design goal is to find structures that maximize a figure of merit (transmission efficiency, bandwidth, etc.) subject to fabrication constraints.

The naive approach is electromagnetic simulation: simulate how light propagates through a proposed structure, measure the efficiency, iteratively improve. But there are infinitely many possible structures, and simulating each one is computationally expensive. BOSON-1 uses inverse design: instead of proposing structures and simulating them, you specify the desired optical behavior and use optimization algorithms (gradient descent, genetic algorithms) to find the structure that achieves it.

The additional wrinkle is process variation. Photonic structures are extremely sensitive to dimensional variation: a 1nm error in waveguide width can shift the resonance frequency by nanometers, destroying the design. Most inverse-design approaches ignore this; they find a structure that works nominally, then hope it survives variation. BOSON-1 is variation-aware: it explicitly incorporates process variation uncertainty into the optimization objective, finding structures that are robust to fabrication error. This requires simulating not just the nominal design but the design under a distribution of process variations—a computationally expensive proposition that makes the inverse design problem much harder.

**Cryogenic quantum co-design** represents a different kind of boundary-pushing. Quantum computers operate at cryogenic temperatures (millikelvin to kelvin), and they require control electronics that must also operate at cryogenic temperatures (to minimize heat leak and power dissipation). A DATE paper on cryogenic quantum co-design proposes designing classical control circuits in cryogenic CMOS: transistors that operate at 4K instead of 300K.

The physics is unfamiliar: at 4K, the bandgap of silicon shifts, the carrier mobility changes, the leakage current floor is dominated by different mechanisms (tunneling rather than thermal diffusion). A 7nm process that is well-characterized at 300K has unknown behavior at 4K. The PDK provided by the foundry is only valid at 300K. The designer must re-characterize the process: fabricate test chips at 4K, measure device parameters, build a new PDK, then design. This is not a software problem; it is a fundamental physics and manufacturing problem.

**Chiplet simulation and cache-coherence verification** (c2c-gem5) shows another boundary: the boundary between "single die" and "system of dice." ICCAD optimizes place and route on a single die, assuming the die is the atomic design unit. But when you connect multiple dice with a cache-coherence protocol, the atomic unit becomes the system of dice. The verification challenge is that cache coherence is hard: there are subtle race conditions involving memory accesses, coherence messages, and clock skew between dice. A simulator that models each die independently is insufficient; you need to model the entire system.

**Memory optimization for emerging storage technologies** like STT-MRAM (Spin-Torque Transfer Magnetic RAM) shows a pattern-repeated at DATE: a new storage technology is introduced, and designers must figure out how to integrate it into the memory hierarchy. STT-MRAM has advantages (non-volatile, high density, low dynamic power) and disadvantages (slow write latency, limited write endurance). The design question is: how do you place and configure STT-MRAM in a cache hierarchy to maximize performance and lifetime? The DATE papers on STT-MRAM don't just optimize—they characterize failure modes (write failures, read disturbance) and design around them.

The common thread is that these physical design papers operate at the frontier where tools don't yet exist. ICCAD's placement and routing algorithms assume the PDK is fixed; DATE's papers are often trying to design while the PDK is still being written. This requires collaboration between circuit designers, device physicists, and tool developers—it is inherently a "design, automation, and test" problem, not just a CAD problem.

## Open Problems

DATE 2025 surfaces three frontier problems that will shape research for years.

**Formal verification for novel physical substrates.** Formal verification tools are mature for digital circuits: SAT solvers, model checkers, and automated theorem provers can prove properties of digital systems. But analog circuits, photonic circuits, and neuromorphic circuits operate in continuous domains where formal verification tools don't apply. The papers on formal verification of analog NNs make a start using reachability analysis and hybrid automata, but the scalability is limited. As more circuits are designed in novel substrates, we need fundamentally new verification approaches. This is not a tool problem (you can't just adapt existing SAT solvers); it is a methodology problem. We need to define what "correctness" means for a photonic circuit under process variation, then develop verification techniques to prove it.

**Testing under adversarial threat models.** Traditional test assumes random faults; adversarial threat models assume an intelligent attacker. How do you test a chip to guarantee it is resistant to fault injection? The naive approach is fault injection testing (deliberately inject faults via simulation or laser, measure whether the circuit fails), but this is incomplete: you can only test a finite set of fault locations and fault types. The DATE papers on this are still exploratory, but the direction is clear: you need formal verification of security properties (e.g., "no single-fault can cause misclassification of an image"), which brings us back to the previous open problem. This is a genuinely hard problem with no clear solution.

**Automating the design transition for emerging substrates.** When a new process technology or novel substrate emerges (neuromorphic, photonic, cryogenic), the first question is: how do designers use it? Initially, design is manual and heroic. Over time, tools are developed. The frontier problem is: can we automate this transition? Can we build tools that, given a new substrate and a design specification, automatically generate a correct, efficient design? The papers on skyrmion logic-in-memory and dendritic computation are taking this on, but it is early. The challenge is that you need to understand both the hardware (what computations are efficient in skyrmions?) and the applications (which algorithms can exploit skyrmion hardware?), then synthesize the bridge. This requires formal models of the substrate (which don't yet exist for emerging technologies) and synthesis algorithms that can target those models.

These three problems are interconnected. To solve the adversarial testing problem, you need formal verification (problem 1). To scale formal verification to real designs, you need better automation (problem 3). To automate design for novel substrates, you need to understand the verification challenges in those substrates (problem 1). DATE is the venue where these problems are being attacked from multiple angles, and the next five years of DATE research will likely focus on making progress on all three.

## Conclusion

DATE 2025 is a conference organized around a single practical reality: when you design a chip and send it to be manufactured, the chip you get back is not the one you designed. It is corrupted by process variation, it is vulnerable to soft errors and adversarial faults, it operates under system-level constraints that weren't visible in RTL simulation. The research at DATE is devoted to closing the gap between design intent and silicon reality.

The tools for this are evolving. Formal verification is being extended to analog and novel substrates. Test methodologies are being rethought to account for adversarial threats. Design automation is being pushed toward new physical substrates where the design methodology is still being written. And approximate computing is being integrated into the design flow, with rigorous characterization of correctness and error behavior.

What makes DATE distinct is that it refuses to pretend these problems are separate. A European automotive engineer designing a safety-critical sensor fusion circuit faces all of these challenges simultaneously: process variation (because the foundry can't guarantee precision), soft error reliability (because the car will be deployed in an environment with radiation), adversarial security (because a disgruntled service technician could attack the circuit), and real-time constraints (because the braking system has a hard deadline). DATE is the venue where these challenges are addressed together, where design and verification and testing are recognized as inseparable.

The 2025 corpus shows a community that is deepening its engagement with the physical frontier. Papers on photonic inverse design, cryogenic CMOS, and skyrmion logic-in-memory are not niche curiosities; they represent the research community pushing into substrates that may dominate computing in 2035. Papers on formal verification of analog circuits and adversarial security are not isolated breakthroughs; they represent a broad reckoning with the fact that correctness is harder to define and verify than traditional EDA assumed. Papers on AUTOSAR Adaptive and system-level determinism show that the design-correctness gap extends from transistors to software.

DATE is where this reckoning is happening, and it is the place to watch if you want to understand the future of hardware design.
