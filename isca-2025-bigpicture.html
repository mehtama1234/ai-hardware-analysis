ISCA 2025 — Big Picture

First-principles overview — the problem, the approaches, where it fits

What ISCA is

ISCA exists to publish hardware designs that worked. Its papers are mostly novel microarchitectures, new memory systems, or circuit techniques—validated on real silicon, credible simulators, or production measurements. Unlike applied systems venues that optimize software for existing hardware, or theoretical venues that prove bounds, ISCA papers describe what a computer is: how transistors move data, what order they execute operations in, how they protect themselves against attacks.

The venue attracts researchers from university labs and company architecture teams who have built something tangible: a new CPU core, a GPU memory controller, an accelerator, a network switch. The implicit contract is "we designed this, we measured it, here are the results." ISCA reviewers police this hard—papers with only simulation results at small scales are regularly rejected. The venue is not a showcase for ideas; it is a record of what has been demonstrated to work at enough scale and fidelity to matter for the field.

ISCA answers a specific question that no other venue does as thoroughly: given the workloads we care about today, how should we architect computers to run them? Decisions made at ISCA filter down through the industry—published designs are cited in architecture textbooks, copied into product datasheets, and influence the next generation of chip designs. It is the site where fundamental trade-offs about performance, energy, area, and correctness get settled through evidence.

The one problem this community is organized around

The core constraint is this: modern workloads (AI training and inference, large-scale analytics, simulation) are data-hungry and sequential in their dependencies, but the laws of physics make moving data expensive and parallel execution cheap. A compute operation takes nanoseconds; moving data from main memory to the compute core takes hundreds of nanoseconds. For large neural networks, GPUs, or databases, data movement—not arithmetic—is the bottleneck.

Simultaneously, the semiconductor industry is hitting a wall. Moore's Law has slowed. Transistors still get smaller, but power density per unit area is not dropping as fast as it used to, so we can no longer count on clock frequency to save us. Every new chip generation must deliver speedups primarily through architectural innovation—cleverer ways to organize memory, smarter scheduling of operations, new forms of parallelism—rather than faster clocks or denser transistors.

These two facts collide at ISCA. The research community is tasked with answering: How do we build computers that move less data, use less power, and still complete the workloads we need in acceptable time? The answer space is finite. You can move the compute closer to the data (in-memory processing, caches, prefetching). You can compress the data (quantization, sparsity). You can exploit redundancy in the computation (result reuse, kernel fusion, approximation). You can parallelize smarter (tensor parallelism, 2D dataflow). You can schedule more carefully (prefetch suppression, traffic shaping, virtualization). ISCA papers describe specific instances of these strategies, applied to real workloads and real constraints.

The stakes are high because the cost of a wrong bet is enormous. A chip is designed years in advance, fabricated at massive expense, and locked in for its product lifetime. An architectural mistake—picking the wrong memory hierarchy, the wrong interconnect topology, the wrong granularity for parallelism—can waste billions in silicon. ISCA is where researchers test whether architectural hunches actually work before they are baked into silicon.

The main approaches in 2025

Exploiting proximity: Moving computation into or near memory

Instead of ferrying data back and forth between memory and processors, some designs push compute into the memory system itself. Papers like "Hybrid SLC-MLC RRAM PIM" embed analog circuits into memory cells so matrix multiplication happens in place; "Hermes" co-locates retrieval indices with datastore partitions to avoid redundant scanning; "In-Storage Processing for RAG" accelerates searches directly inside the storage layer. The idea is ancient—near-data processing—but the 2025 instantiations are increasingly practical: RRAM (resistive memory) can perform analog matrix-vector products with dramatically lower energy than shuttling data through a digital pipeline; processing-in-memory banks can be made reconfigurable to serve different workloads. The tradeoff is precision and flexibility: analog RRAM loses accuracy quickly under quantization stress, so papers expend real effort (gradient redistribution, SVD-based weight selection) to keep only the sensitive parts digital. At 27 papers on near-data processing, this is a dominant theme.

Scheduling and resource partitioning: Doing the right thing at the right time

With limited memory bandwidth and interconnect capacity, the order in which you issue requests matters enormously. Papers like "RAGO" (RAG schema-driven scheduling), "MeshSlice" (2D tensor parallelism with communication-compute overlap), and "NetCrafter" (traffic shaping for non-uniform bandwidth) all optimize when data moves and where it goes. RAGO surfaces that different RAG algorithms have different bottlenecks—some are retrieval-bound, others generation-bound—so a uniform serving system wastes resources; the fix is workload-specific scheduling. MeshSlice overlaps collective communication (all-reduce operations) with matrix multiply by decomposing collectives into smaller chunks that interleave with compute. NetCrafter stitches partial flits together and prioritizes latency-critical packets. These are not new algorithmic ideas; they are architectural optimizations that require precise measurement and careful co-design between the algorithm and the hardware. Scheduling papers account for ~51 papers (most in the data).

Tighter algorithm-architecture co-design: Trading precision for speed

Approximation is increasingly accepted at ISCA. Rather than compute everything exactly, papers exploit tolerance in the workload: "HyFlexPIM" uses a mixed-signal RRAM hierarchy where attention operations stay digital for precision, but most transformer weights land in non-linear analog MLC cells—the accumulated error is <1%. "Temporal-Signal ANN Execution" runs approximate nearest-neighbor search using analog dynamics. Even "Cassandra" trades speculative execution (normally fast but unsafe) for deterministic sequential control flow in cryptographic code—a calculated bet that the security benefit outweighs the performance cost. At 16 papers on approximation and 10 on quantization, the field is learning to manage precision as an architectural knob, not a binary gate.

Interconnect and collective-operation redesign: Topology and routing matter

At scale, the network connecting chips becomes the bottleneck. "Zettafly" redesigns the network topology for zettascale HPC/AI systems to partition large clusters into smaller non-blocking subnetworks; jobs stay confined to regions where all-to-all communication is unblocked, avoiding contention. "NetCrafter" patches the symptom (non-uniform bandwidth) with flit stitching and sequencing. "MeshSlice" adapts 2D tensor parallelism collectives to exploit torus topology on TPUs. These papers recognize that network latency and bandwidth heterogeneity are physical facts that must be incorporated into the architecture, not external constraints to be ignored. The 17 papers on interconnect and 51 on scheduling reflect how critical collective operations are to distributed training and inference.

Specialized dataflow and ISA design: Matching hardware to computation

Rather than general-purpose processors, several papers describe ISAs or dataflow architectures tailored to specific workloads. "Circuit-Switched Streaming ISA for FPGA DNN Overlays" proposes a hardware-friendly instruction set for neural network layers on FPGAs; "Retargetable HDC Programming System with DSL" builds a compiler toolchain for hyperdimensional computing on heterogeneous processors. The insight is that a generic instruction set (like ARM or x86) carries overhead when applied to workloads with known structure; a DSL or domain-specific ISA can eliminate branches, memory indirection, and synchronization overhead. The tradeoff is inflexibility: a custom ISA is only useful for its intended workload class. Papers report 2-10x speedups by accepting this tradeoff for ML workloads. ~39 papers on dataflow and 29 on compilers reflect the industry consensus that software-hardware co-design is now mandatory at performance-critical scales.

Reliability and security: Protecting under resource pressure

As systems grow and operate at lower power margins, faults become more likely. "WarmCache" uses relaxed-retention STT-RAM (non-volatile memory that only needs to survive typical power-outage durations, not indefinitely) to reduce write energy in intermittent computing systems; it pairs this with compiler-level error correction at region boundaries to recover from retention bit-flips. "Cassandra" sacrifices branch-prediction speculation in cryptographic code to prevent Spectre-class transient-execution attacks, using compressed control-flow traces instead. "Hardware-Enforced Transient-Execution Mitigation via Memory Tagging" adds per-cacheline tags to detect unsafe speculative loads. These papers accept performance or area cost to gain provable correctness under adversity (power failures, timing attacks, hardware faults). ~12 papers on reliability and 9 on security show the field acknowledging that correctness is not free.

How it connects to the broader field

ISCA is a sink for ideas from systems, programming languages, and algorithms, and a source for ideas flowing to industry and applied systems venues. Compilers (PLDI, CGO) describe techniques for vectorization and parallelism; ISCA papers embed these into hardware support (SIMD awareness, dataflow scheduling) to close the last 20% of optimization gap. Machine learning (NeurIPS, ICML) publishes algorithms; ISCA researchers then ask "how should hardware change to make this algorithm faster?"—quantization-aware training, gradient redistribution for mixed-precision PIM, approximate matrix multiplication. Distributed systems (OSDI, SOSP) publish serving frameworks; ISCA responds with hardware for collective communication and load balancing.

Conversely, ISCA produces designs that other venues consume. Applied systems papers (VLDB, SIGMOD) optimize database engines assuming a fixed hardware model; when ISCA papers deploy in-storage processing or hierarchical memory, those assumptions change and systems researchers must re-optimize. Networking venues (SIGCOMM) design algorithms for packet routing; ISCA papers on network topology and traffic shaping provide new constraints (non-blocking regions, flit stitching) that networking then optimizes around.

ISCA does NOT answer: How do we write better software? How do we deploy systems at scale? How do we prove algorithms are correct? Those are for systems, networks, and theory venues. ISCA also rarely publishes incremental improvements at small scale—a 10% speedup on a 4-core ARM chip is not competitive; a 2x speedup on a 1000-GPU cluster is. The community cares about designs that matter at the scale workloads now require.

What's open

The most honest gap: ISCA has not yet solved the memory bandwidth crisis for LLM inference at scale. 31 papers tackle LLM inference; nearly all resort to quantization, sparsity, or approximation because the raw bandwidth needed to stream model weights through compute cores remains prohibitive. Papers like "Entropy-Aware Cache Compression" squeeze another 10-20%, but this is optimization-by-a-thousand-cuts. The fundamental issue is that transformer models are compute-light (inference is essentially all matrix-vector products with batch size 1) and data-heavy (the model weights dwarf activation data). Until we either dramatically increase on-chip cache, or find ways to compute on compressed representations natively, or move to radically different model architectures, inference throughput will remain bandwidth-bound. Current approaches (approximate in-memory processing, datastore partitioning for RAG, quantization) are partial solutions, not fundamental breakthroughs. The community is aware this is a hard problem; the proliferation of specialized approaches suggests no single solution has yet won.
