Using smaller numbers

rounding to save memory and time
134 papers · 16 read in full · 118 from the abstract

the problemThe problem, in depth

Start with a single physical fact that almost nothing about modern computer chips makes sense without: a chip does not really think in one place. It is a tiny city. In one district there is a workshop that does arithmetic—adding and multiplying numbers. Somewhere else, often much farther away, there is a warehouse that stores the numbers. And between the workshop and the warehouse there are roads. When people say a chip is fast, they usually imagine the workshop working furiously. But the surprising truth, the one this whole chapter is built on, is that the workshop is almost never the bottleneck. The roads are. The chip spends most of its time and most of its energy not doing arithmetic, but hauling numbers back and forth between the warehouse and the workshop. Doing a multiplication is cheap. Fetching the two numbers you want to multiply, from a warehouse millimeters or centimeters away, is expensive—often ten or a hundred times more expensive in energy than the multiplication itself. This is the root problem of the entire field: computers are limited by moving data around, not by doing arithmetic.

Why is moving data so costly? Because moving a number means pushing electric charge down a wire, and a wire has a kind of electrical stiffness—the longer and thinner the wire, the more energy it takes to change its state from a 0 to a 1 and back. A number sitting in the big off-chip warehouse (the main memory, built from cheap dense memory cells) is far away, down a long wire, behind a narrow doorway that only lets so many numbers through per second. That narrow doorway has a name in the trade—memory bandwidth—and it is the thing everyone is fighting. You can build a workshop that could multiply a trillion numbers a second, but if the doorway can only deliver a hundred billion numbers a second, your magnificent workshop sits idle nine-tenths of the time, waiting for the next delivery. The workshop is not the wall. The doorway is the wall.

Now bring in what these chips are actually being asked to do: run large neural networks, especially the big language models. A language model is, at bottom, an enormous pile of numbers called weights—the settings it learned during training. A modern one has billions of them. To produce even a single word of output, the chip must fetch essentially every one of those billions of weights out of the warehouse, pass each through the workshop for a quick multiply-and-add, and move on. The arithmetic is trivial per weight. The hauling is not. When you multiply billions of weights by the fact that every weight must travel the expensive road, you get a machine whose speed is set almost entirely by how many weights per second you can drag through that narrow doorway. This is why a chip running a language model can look ninety percent idle while still being maxed out: it is starving, waiting on the road.

Here is the lever. Every one of those numbers has to be stored in some number of bits—the little on/off switches that represent it. The traditional choice is to give each number a lot of bits: 16 or 32 switches apiece, enough to represent a value very precisely, with a wide range and many decimal places. That precision is comforting, but it is also the whole cost. A number stored in 16 bits takes up twice the warehouse space, twice the road width, and twice the energy-to-haul of the same number stored in 8 bits—and four times that of a number stored in 4 bits. So the single most powerful thing you can do to a starving chip is not to build a bigger workshop or a wider doorway. It is to make every number smaller: use fewer switches per number. That is the entire idea behind this chapter's title—using smaller numbers. Cut each weight from 16 bits down to 4, and suddenly four times as many of them fit through the doorway every second, and the whole machine speeds up roughly fourfold, while burning far less energy. Nothing else in the field buys you that much for that little.

But smaller numbers are cruder numbers, and this is where the difficulty—and the entire research field—lives. With only 4 switches you can represent just sixteen distinct values. You are being forced to round every one of a billion carefully-learned weights to the nearest of sixteen coarse buckets. Most weights are small and clustered near zero, so a set of buckets tuned for that cluster serves them well. But scattered through every neural network are a few freakishly large values—the field calls them outliers—and a handful of them carry disproportionate importance to the final answer. Round those to the nearest coarse bucket and the model's output degrades, sometimes catastrophically. The central tension of the whole theme is therefore a bargain: fewer bits means faster and cheaper, but too few bits, or the wrong bits in the wrong place, means a model that has quietly gotten stupider. Every technique in this chapter is a different, ingenious way of getting the speed and energy savings of tiny numbers while refusing to pay the accuracy tax—by being clever about which numbers get few bits, which get more, how the buckets are chosen, and how the hardware is rebuilt to consume the smaller numbers without wasting the savings on the machinery of unpacking them.

One more physical wrinkle makes this urgent rather than merely nice. Big models don't only store their fixed weights; while they run, they also build up a growing stack of running notes—intermediate values the model jots down as it reads a long passage so it doesn't have to re-read everything for each new word. The longer the conversation or document, the taller this stack of notes grows, and it lives in the same scarce warehouse and travels the same narrow road as the weights. For long inputs it can dwarf the weights themselves and become the thing that overflows memory or throttles throughput. So the pressure to use smaller numbers falls not just on the model's permanent settings but on these ever-growing notes, and on the streams of intermediate values (the field calls them activations) that flow through the network as it computes. Shrink all three—weights, notes, and activations—and you have shrunk the traffic on every road in the city at once.

the approachesHow the field actually tackles it

1.Round the numbers to a small fixed set of buckets (uniform low-bit quantization)

The most basic move is the most powerful: take numbers that were stored in 16 or 32 switches each and re-express them in far fewer—8, 4, even down toward 2 switches apiece. To do this you pick a small ladder of evenly-spaced values—the buckets—and snap each original number to its nearest rung. Because the rungs are evenly spaced, the hardware that decodes them is dirt cheap: a multiply and an add restores the approximate original value. This is the workhorse of the field, and it is why almost every serving system quantizes weights to 4 bits and often activations to 8. QServe (mlsys-2025-003) is a careful, production-grade example: it stores weights in 4 bits, activations in 8, and the running notes in 4, and its whole cleverness is arranging the numbers so the flexible chip (the graphics processor) can restore them without the unpacking work eating up the savings—it even discovered that the naive way of undoing 4-bit weights wasted 20 to 90 percent of the running time, and reordered the arithmetic to avoid that.

The trouble with evenly-spaced buckets is that real neural-network values are not evenly spread—they pile up near zero with a few wild outliers far out. Force everything onto one evenly-spaced ladder and you either waste most of your rungs on empty territory or crush the important outliers. A large part of this approach is therefore choosing the ladder well: making it asymmetric so it hugs a lopsided distribution (Panacea, hpca-2025-044), or splitting the numbers into small groups and giving each group its own ladder so a group of unusually large values gets a ladder scaled for them. This grouping idea—quantize in little blocks, each with its own scale factor—is the quiet backbone of nearly everything that follows. MEADOW (mlsys-2025-031) shows the same base recipe applied to a tiny low-power edge chip, keeping everything at 8 bits to fit the device's cramped memory.

2.Spend bits where they matter, save them where they don't (mixed precision and outlier handling)

If a few values are precious and most are ordinary, why pay the same price for all of them? Mixed precision is the strategy of giving different numbers different bit-budgets. The ordinary bulk gets crushed to 4 bits; the rare, important outliers are kept at 8 or 16 bits so their information survives. The saving is real because the outliers are rare, so the average across all numbers is still tiny, yet the accuracy is rescued. COMET (asplos-2025-082) is a clean case: it pushes almost all the flowing activations to 4 bits but detects the outlier values and quietly keeps them at 8, which is exactly what makes an otherwise-impractical all-4-bit scheme actually work without the model degrading.

The same idea appears wherever a workload has a natural notion of importance. Oaken (isca-2025-007), attacking the growing stack of running notes, sorts each note's values into three tiers by how extreme they are and quantizes each tier differently—crucially figuring out the tier boundaries ahead of time by profiling, so it doesn't waste live compute sorting values during inference. LightNobel (isca-2025-042) does token-by-token tiering for a protein-folding model, mixing 4-, 8-, and 16-bit values in the same stream. MX+ (micro-2025-063) is an especially elegant trick: in a block-of-numbers format, the single biggest value in each block was being represented crudely, so MX+ steals some now-useless bits from that value's own encoding to give it extra precision—fixing the outlier at essentially zero storage cost. The through-line is: never spend a bit on a number that doesn't need it, and never starve the few that do.

3.Let the shape of the number itself adapt (custom and per-group data types)

So far the buckets have been evenly spaced. But you can also change the very shape of a number—the rule that maps its bits to a value. Some groups of values are best served by a ladder with rungs bunched near zero and spread out far away (a floating-point-like shape); others by evenly-spaced rungs (an integer-like shape); still others by something in between. This approach hands each small group of values the numeric format that fits its particular spread, instead of forcing one format on the whole tensor. BitMoD (hpca-2025-000) picks, for every group of 128 weights, the best data type from a small menu to minimize the rounding error for that group specifically. M-ANT (hpca-2025-036) goes further with a mathematically flexible format assigned per group, and Anda (hpca-2025-029) lets the number of decimal-place bits float up and down per section of the model, spending precision only where the model is sensitive.

The catch is in the hardware. If every group might use a different numeric shape, a naive chip would need a separate specialized workshop for each shape—expensive and wasteful. The shared trick across these designs is bit-serial computation: instead of building a wide multiplier for a fixed number size, the hardware processes numbers one bit-layer at a time, so a single flexible unit can chew through 2-bit, 4-bit, or 8-bit numbers, and any exotic format, by just doing more or fewer passes. FATE (isca-2025-004) pushes the adaptivity to the extreme of giving different dimensions of its vectors different bit-widths. The trade-off throughout: you win accuracy-per-bit by tailoring the format, and you pay for it with more complex encode/decode machinery, which these papers work hard to keep cheap.

4.Replace whole vectors with codebook entries (vector quantization)

Everything above rounds each number on its own. Vector quantization makes a deeper bet: don't compress numbers one at a time—compress little groups of them together. Imagine you have a codebook, a numbered list of, say, 256 common short patterns of values. To store a chunk of the weight matrix you find the closest pattern in the codebook and store just its index number. Instead of keeping four 4-bit numbers, you keep one small index that points at a pattern of four values. Because neighboring weights are correlated, a few hundred patterns can stand in for vast stretches of the model, and the effective cost drops below what any per-number scheme can reach—this is how you get down toward 1 or 2 bits per weight, past the floor of rounding single numbers. LUT-DLA (hpca-2025-055) and NeuVSA (hpca-2025-111) build hardware around this, replacing multiply-and-add with a simple table lookup: you don't multiply anymore, you just look up the precomputed answer for the pattern.

The prize is extreme compression; the peril is that codebooks are awkward for hardware. The codebook must be fetched and consulted constantly, and if you do it naively the table lookups thrash the chip's small fast memory and end up slower than not compressing at all—VQ-LLM (hpca-2025-003) found existing implementations were actually slower than doing no compression, and had to carefully place hot codebook entries in the fastest on-chip storage to win. A second subtlety is that building a good codebook is itself hard: MVQ (asplos-2025-079) noticed that the usual clustering method lets unimportant near-zero weights drag the patterns away from the important ones, and fixed it by clustering only on the weights that matter. eDKM (hpca-2025-022) tackles the memory blowup of learning codebooks during training, shrinking the memory needed per layer by 130x so a 7-billion-weight model can be codebook-compressed at all. The recurring lesson: vector quantization offers the best compression on paper, but only pays off with hardware and algorithms co-designed to make the lookups cheap.

5.Turn multiplication into table lookup (LUT-based compute)

This approach targets a specific awkwardness. When weights are tiny integers but the activations flowing past them are still full floating-point numbers, the chip must multiply a floating-point number by a low-bit integer—a mismatched operation that ordinary workshops handle badly. The lookup-table idea sidesteps the multiply entirely. Since a low-bit weight can only take a handful of values, you can precompute, up front, the products of an incoming activation with every possible weight value, stash them in a small table, and then multiply simply by looking up the answer with the weight's bits as the address. No multiplier fires at all during the main work. FIGLUT (hpca-2025-071) builds exactly this for the floating-point-times-integer case, and the LUT Tensor Core (isca-2025-079) integrates the idea into a graphics-processor-style compute unit, reporting several-fold reductions in area and energy because table lookups are so much cheaper than multipliers.

The trade-off is table overhead: if the tables grow large or must be recomputed too often, the storage and setup cost can swallow the benefit. The engineering across these papers is mostly about keeping tables small and reusing each one many times—the LUT Tensor Core, for instance, reshapes the computation so one table is reused across many outputs, and exploits a symmetry trick (reinterpreting the two weight values as -1 and +1) to halve table size. This family sits close to vector quantization—both replace arithmetic with lookups—but here the compression is per-number and the lookup is about dodging an awkward mixed multiply rather than pointing at shared patterns.

6.Rebuild the machine to actually consume the smaller numbers (hardware and dataflow co-design)

A subtle trap haunts this whole field: you can shrink the numbers and still get no faster, because the ordinary chip pays back all your savings just unpacking the little numbers into a form its rigid workshop understands. If undoing the compression costs as much as the compression saved, you have gained nothing. So a large share of the work is not the compression scheme at all but redesigning the hardware and the choreography of data movement (the dataflow) so the tiny numbers stay tiny as long as possible and the workshop consumes them natively. DECA (micro-2025-035) is the sharpest illustration: on a general-purpose processor, unpacking compressed weights was eating over 95 percent of the instructions—the decompression, not the math or the memory, was the true bottleneck—so they built a dedicated little unit right next to each core just to unpack on the fly, and even added a new instruction so the core can kick off that unpacking without stalling.

This co-design shows up at every scale. On ordinary processors that lack instructions for sub-byte numbers, HiPACK (micro-2025-011) and CAMP (micro-2025-080) invent packing tricks and multiplier structures so a general vector unit can chew several tiny numbers at once instead of wastefully padding each back to full width. On custom chips, MVQ (asplos-2025-079) builds a compute array that skips the pruned-away weights to shrink the hardware by more than half, and Panacea (hpca-2025-044) restructures compute to skip the many all-zero bit-layers that quantization produces. AQB8 (isca-2025-049) makes ray-tracing boxes into 8-bit integers so the chip can test them without ever converting back to floating-point. AIM (isca-2025-126) reaches all the way down to the electrical level, noticing that quantized weights with fewer 1-bits draw less current and cause less voltage droop, and steering the quantization to keep the chip electrically calm. The unifying insight: a compression scheme is only as good as the hardware that consumes it, and the two must be designed together or the savings evaporate.

7.Compress the differences, not the values (exploiting redundancy over time and training)

A more specialized but beautiful idea: sometimes the values themselves aren't the cheapest thing to store—the changes between successive values are. Image-generating models work by refining a picture over many small steps, and consecutive steps produce outputs that are almost identical, differing only slightly. Ditto (hpca-2025-047) exploits this by doing one full-precision step and then, for every step after, computing and quantizing only the tiny differences from the previous step—differences so small they round to just a few bits, and often to exactly zero, in which case the work can be skipped entirely. Storing and moving these narrow differences instead of full fresh values slashes the traffic on the roads.

The same instinct—store only what is small and meaningful, and reconstruct the rest—appears in training-time methods. HyC-LoRA (mlsys-2025-043) attacks the memory a model burns while being fine-tuned, where the buffered activations, not the weights, dominate; it crushes the smooth activations to nearly 2 bits, keeps only the handful of outlier channels at full precision, and cleverly recomputes some values during the backward pass rather than storing them. MiLo (mlsys-2025-021) quantizes a mixture-of-experts model down to 3 bits and then adds a small, cheap correction term that patches back the error the crushing introduced. This is quantization aimed at redundancy and reconstruction rather than at raw magnitude, and it generalizes the theme: the real target is always the number of bits that must travel the expensive road, and if the meaningful information is small, then the number of bits that must move can be small too, regardless of how large the underlying values are.

where it standsWhat's solved, what's open

The broad win is settled and in production: shrinking a model's weights to 4 bits and its activations to 8 is now routine, gives multi-fold speed and energy improvements, and costs almost no accuracy—this is no longer research, it is standard practice, and the running notes of long conversations can now be squeezed to 4 bits as well (QServe, Oaken) without models noticeably suffering. Below that line, the picture is honestly unsettled. Pushing activations all the way down to 4 bits is still delicate and leans on outlier-handling tricks that add complexity (COMET); 3-bit and 2-bit weights remain fragile, salvageable today mainly through vector quantization or added correction terms (MiLo's low-rank patches, MVQ's careful codebooks), and those gains come with real hardware cost that only elaborate co-design recovers.

The deepest open problem is the one this chapter opened with: even after the numbers are tiny, the machinery of decompressing them can itself become the bottleneck (DECA's finding that unpacking devoured 95 percent of instructions), so the frontier is no longer how few bits in isolation but how few bits the hardware can consume without paying the savings back. There is also no single winning format—the field is a sprawl of custom data types, per-group formats, and codebook schemes, each strong on some models and awkward on others, with little standardization and much of the cleverness locked to specific chips. And the hardest values of all—the rare, load-bearing outliers, and the messy activations around normalization steps—still resist compression and force the mixed-precision escape hatches that keep these systems more complicated than anyone would like.

MVQ: Towards Efficient DNN Compression and Acceleration with Masked Vector Quantization

What it does

Neural network weights — the millions of numbers that define what a model has learned — take up a lot of storage and must be loaded from memory every time the chip does a calculation. Vector quantization (VQ) is a compression trick: instead of storing each weight individually, you store a small 'codebook' of representative patterns (say, 512 patterns instead of millions of unique values) and then store only a tiny index per weight group saying which pattern it maps to. The catch is that existing VQ methods treat all weights as equally important during the grouping process, forcing critical high-impact weights to be approximated by the same pattern as irrelevant near-zero weights — which hurts accuracy badly at high compression ratios. MVQ fixes this by first eliminating the unimportant weights entirely (using a structured sparsity technique called N:M pruning) and then running the clustering only on the weights that remain, so the patterns are tuned to fit the weights that actually matter.

The method, in detail

The idea. When you cluster a million weights into 512 patterns, the cluster centers (codewords) get pulled toward the large pile of near-zero weights that dominate the distribution. Important large-magnitude weights end up approximated poorly. The fix: remove the zeros before clustering, so the codewords are forced to approximate only the significant weights.

How it works. 1. N:M pruning. Within every group of M consecutive weights, zero out all but N of them (the smallest-magnitude ones). For example, 2:4 pruning keeps exactly 2 of every 4 weights nonzero. The zeros are stored implicitly as a compact bitmask. The model is then fine-tuned to compensate for the removed weights.

2. Masked k-means. Standard k-means (the clustering algorithm) computes the distance from each weight group to each codeword, then updates codewords as the average of their assigned groups. MVQ's version masks out the zero positions: distances are computed using only the nonzero positions, and codeword updates average only the nonzero values. This prevents the sea of zeros from dragging codewords toward zero and ensures each codeword is a good representative for the surviving weights.

3. Codebook quantization. The codebook entries themselves are stored as 8-bit integers (using rounded, reduced-precision numbers) rather than 32-bit floats, cutting codebook storage cost further.

4. Masked gradient fine-tuning. After compression, the codewords are fine-tuned end-to-end, again applying masks during the backward pass so gradients from zero positions don't corrupt the update.

The key hardware design. A custom chip uses a 'codebook register file' (a small fast memory holding the ~512 codeword patterns on chip). When a weight is needed, the controller looks up the codeword from the register file using the stored index, then applies the sparsity mask to reconstruct the sparse weight — all on chip. The array of multiply-accumulate units (the grid of tiny math circuits) is redesigned to have only N/M active units per weight group instead of the full M, matching the sparsity pattern and cutting both transistor count and energy by 55%.

The result. On 40nm chip silicon, compared to the baseline dense accelerator: 2.3x energy efficiency improvement and 55% smaller compute array, at equivalent or better accuracy versus prior VQ methods at the same compression ratio (e.g., 22x compression on ResNet-18 with 0.6% less accuracy loss than the next-best method).

VQ-LLM: High-performance Code Generation for Vector Quantization Augmented LLM Inference

What it does

Vector quantization (VQ) is a compression technique that groups several numbers together and replaces the whole group with a single index pointing to a lookup table (called a codebook) of pre-trained cluster centres — achieving better accuracy at 2-bit compression than conventional element-wise rounding. In practice though, VQ-compressed LLMs run slower than uncompressed ones because codebook lookups are slow and disorganised. VQ-LLM is a code-generation framework that reorganises how codebooks are stored and accessed across GPU memory levels and redesigns the computation so each thread block handles exactly one codebook, reducing kernel latency by 46-99% over existing implementations.

The method, in detail

The idea. A GPU program (called a kernel) has three levels of memory: small, fast per-thread registers; medium on-chip shared memory shared by a group of threads (called a thread block); and large, slow off-chip global memory. Existing VQ kernels dump the entire codebook into shared memory, which is large enough to cause two problems: (1) fewer thread blocks can run at once because shared memory is a shared resource — hurting GPU occupancy; and (2) there are far more codebook entries than memory banks (e.g., 256 entries vs 32 banks), so random lookups constantly collide — serialising access. VQ-LLM attacks both problems.

Codebook cache — hierarchical placement. VQ-LLM profiles access frequency offline: a handful of entries are accessed hundreds of times more than average (hot), most entries are accessed infrequently (cold). The framework sorts entries by frequency and uses two configurable boundaries: the hottest entries go into per-thread registers (no bank conflicts possible), the medium-frequency ones stay in shared memory, and the cold tail stays in global memory. The boundaries are chosen to fit within whatever memory 'slack' the subsequent compute kernel is not using, so occupancy never drops.

Codebook-centric dataflow — eliminating duplicate loads. The standard GPU parallelisation strategy splits computation by output token, so every thread block processing a different token ends up loading the same codebook from global memory independently — duplicated traffic proportional to the number of tokens. VQ-LLM instead splits and parallelises along the codebook boundary: each thread block owns exactly one codebook and all tokens that use it, then contributes a partial result to a global reduction. An adaptive split factor balances the cost of the added reduction against the cost of eliminated duplicate loads.

Codebook-centric hierarchical fusion — skipping shared memory round-trips. After a thread dequantises a vector (looks up the codebook entry and gets back, say, 8 numbers), it often needs those numbers in a different arrangement for the subsequent matrix-multiply instruction. Normally the thread writes them back to shared memory so other threads can read them in the right order — wasteful. VQ-LLM instead uses GPU intra-warp shuffle instructions (shfl.xor), which let threads directly exchange register values with each other without touching memory. An offline analysis computes the required thread mapping and number of shuffles; if fewer than five shuffles are needed, register-level fusion is used; otherwise shared-memory fusion is kept.

The result. Across VQ-compressed weight and KV-cache kernels for Llama-7B and Llama-65B, VQ-LLM achieves 46% average latency reduction versus the best existing VQ kernels, and up to 99% reduction versus naive open-source implementations. End-to-end, VQ-LLM matches the speed of state-of-the-art element-wise 4-bit quantisation (QoQ/qServe) while offering 2.5% better task accuracy at the same bit-width.

Oaken: Fast and Efficient LLM Serving with Online-Offline Hybrid KV Cache Quantization

What it does

Running large language models at scale requires keeping enormous amounts of intermediate data — called the KV cache, essentially the model's running notes about earlier words in a conversation — in fast memory. High-bandwidth memory chips (HBM) are fast but small; high-capacity chips (LPDDR) are large but slower. Shrinking the KV cache by rounding its numbers to fewer bits (quantization) would help, but existing methods either lose too much accuracy or are too slow because finding which numbers are outliers at runtime is expensive. Oaken is a chip design that solves this by determining outlier boundaries once in advance, then applying a fast hardware-assisted quantization scheme at serving time, achieving both the large capacity of LPDDR and near-HBM throughput.

The method, in detail

The idea. LLM inference batches many user requests together. The bottleneck is the attention step, which requires reading unique key-value data for every request from memory — this cannot be shared, so memory bandwidth and capacity are both strained. Quantization (storing numbers in 4 bits instead of 16) would halve the memory burden, but the problem is outliers: a few values in each layer are much larger than the rest, and if you pick a single scale for the whole vector, the small values get rounded to zero. Previous solutions sorted all values each time (expensive: O(n log n)) or stored outliers in full 16-bit precision (expensive: 23 bits of overhead per entry).

How it works. Oaken runs about 100 warm-up inferences offline to gather statistics on each layer's key-value distribution. From those runs it extracts four threshold values per layer that partition any future vector into three groups: outer outliers (very large magnitude), inner outliers (very small magnitude near zero), and the main body of inliers. At serving time, the hardware decomposes each new vector into its three groups in a single pass — no sorting needed. The outer and inner groups are shifted toward zero by subtracting the offline thresholds, making their range narrow enough for 5-bit quantization. Inliers get standard 4-bit quantization. The outlier entries are stored in a sparse coordinate format (index + value), but their 4-bit value portions are fused into the zero-slots already present in the dense inlier matrix, so each outlier entry costs only 8 bits total instead of 23. A custom memory management unit inside the chip's DMA controller (the data-movement engine) handles burst reads of both dense and sparse pages, keeping memory bandwidth nearly at the physical limit.

The key design choice. Separating the slow part (finding outlier thresholds) from the fast part (applying them) — offline profiling for thresholds, online application at inference — avoids the sorting cost that made earlier approaches impractical.

The result. At a batch size of 256, Oaken achieves up to 1.58x higher throughput than an NVIDIA A100 GPU running the best existing KV-cache quantization method, while incurring an average accuracy loss of only 0.54%.

LightNobel: Improving Sequence Length Limitation in Protein Structure Prediction Model via Adaptive Activation Quantization

What it does

Protein structure prediction models like AlphaFold2 — the AI systems that won the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for figuring out the 3D shape of proteins from their amino acid sequences — run out of GPU memory on proteins longer than about 2000 amino acids because their internal data structures grow quadratically and then cubically with sequence length. LightNobel is a custom chip design that compresses those internal numbers using adaptive precision — different parts of the data get 4-bit, 8-bit, or 16-bit representations depending on their statistical behavior — reducing peak memory by up to 120x and speeding up execution up to 8.4x versus the best current NVIDIA GPUs while keeping prediction accuracy virtually unchanged.

The method, in detail

The idea. Protein folding models maintain a special data structure called the Pair Representation — a table with one entry for every pair of amino acids in the protein, encoding how those two positions relate spatially. If a protein has N amino acids, this table has N×N entries, each of which is a vector of 128 numbers. The attention mechanism (which lets every pair look at every other pair) then operates over N×N×N triples. At N=2000 this already exceeds GPU memory; at N=45000 (a real protein called titin) it would require terabytes. LightNobel addresses this by representing numbers at lower precision to shrink everything.

The key insight about data structure. In most AI models, different channels (the 128 positions in each vector) vary wildly — one might have values around 100, another around 0.01 — so quantization must be done channel by channel. In protein models, the opposite is true: all 128 channels in a given token (a specific amino acid pair) have similar magnitudes, but different tokens vary enormously. This 'distogram pattern' — the protein's pairwise distance map is imprinted on the data — means grouping by token is the right granularity.

Three activation groups. The paper analyzes every tensor in the computation and sorts them into three buckets. Group A (before layer normalization, connected to residual pathways): values are large (average magnitude 82) and have outliers, so they get 8-bit precision for inliers plus 16-bit storage for the few extreme outliers. Group B (after normalization): values are small (magnitude ~4) but outliers persist, so 4-bit for inliers, 16-bit for outliers. Group C (other activations): small values, almost no outliers, so plain 4-bit with no special outlier handling. Outliers are identified at runtime using a parallel sorting algorithm (bitonic sort) that runs directly on the chip's vector units.

The hardware. The method, in detail

The idea. Every weight value stored in a PIM chip's memory has a binary representation (a pattern of 1s and 0s). Metrics first. AIM introduces two measurements. Rtog is the fraction of bits that actually toggle each clock cycle — the real-time sag indicator, but hard to precompute.
