Describing hardware so a machine can build it

compilers and chip generators
52 papers · 9 read in full · 43 from the abstract

the problemThe problem, in depth

Imagine you want to build a house. You have a picture in your head of the finished thing — three bedrooms, a kitchen, a porch. But a picture is not a house. Somewhere between the picture and the actual walls, someone has to decide the order the concrete gets poured, which crew does what, where the plumbing runs so it doesn't collide with the wiring, and how to keep everyone from standing around waiting for materials. The picture is the easy part. The plan that turns the picture into physical work — done in the right order, with nobody idle and nothing wasted — is the hard part. This whole theme is about that plan, but for computation. A person writes down what they want a computer to figure out, in a form that is basically a wish. A translator program has to turn that wish into the exact, ordered, machine-level steps a specific chip can actually perform. That translator is called a compiler, and this chapter is about how modern compilers do their job when the machine is a modern computing chip and the task is running the giant math behind artificial intelligence.

To feel why this is hard, you have to understand the single fact that governs almost everything in modern computing: a chip can do arithmetic far, far faster than it can fetch the numbers to do arithmetic on. We tend to picture a computer as a thing that 'does math fast,' and it does — but the multiplying and adding are almost free now. The expensive part is moving the numbers. Numbers live in memory, which is like a warehouse some distance away from the workbench where the actual arithmetic happens. Every number the chip wants to multiply has to be carried from the warehouse to the bench, and every answer has to be carried back. The bench is blindingly fast; the trip to the warehouse is slow. So the chip mostly sits there, having finished its work, tapping its foot, waiting for the next truckload of numbers to arrive. This is the root problem behind this entire book: computers are limited by moving data around, not by doing arithmetic. Everything in this theme is, at bottom, a trick for hauling fewer numbers, hauling them shorter distances, or keeping the workbench busy while the trucks are still on the road.

Now layer on the reason this got urgent. The math behind modern AI is enormous but also deeply repetitive: the same kind of operation — multiply a big grid of numbers by another big grid of numbers, add them up — repeated billions of times. A grid of numbers is called a 'tensor,' which is just a fancy word for a table, or a table of tables. Running one of today's AI models means shuffling grids with billions of entries through the workbench over and over. If you carry each number from the warehouse fresh every single time you need it, you spend nearly all your time on trucking and almost none on math, and the whole thing crawls and burns enormous amounts of electricity. The difference between a naive plan and a clever plan here is not a few percent — it is often the difference between something finishing in a second versus a minute, or running on a phone versus needing a data center. So the plan matters enormously, and writing a good plan by hand, for every model and every chip, is a soul-crushing amount of specialized labor. Automating that plan-making is the reason this field exists.

What makes a good plan is a set of moves that all reduce to the same idea: touch memory less. The two most important moves show up again and again. The first is reuse: once you've paid to carry a number to the bench, do everything you can with it before sending it back, because carrying it again is the expensive part. The second is fusion: if you were going to compute result A, write it back to the warehouse, then fetch it right back to compute result B, don't — just keep A on the bench and roll straight into B, so it never makes the round trip at all. A huge fraction of the papers in this theme are, at heart, more and more sophisticated versions of 'reuse more' and 'fuse more.' They differ in how cleverly they figure out what can safely be reused or fused, and how they discover those opportunities automatically instead of making a human find them.

But there is a second complication that makes this genuinely hard rather than merely tedious: there is no single 'the chip.' There are flexible general chips (the kind in your laptop), there are flexible massively-parallel chips originally built for video games and now used for AI (often just called by their initials, but think 'a chip that does thousands of small things at once'), there are custom chips built to do exactly one kind of AI math and nothing else, and there are chips reconfigurable like a circuit-board Lego set. Each has a differently shaped workbench, a differently sized close-at-hand shelf of scratch space, and a different set of native moves. A plan that is brilliant on one is terrible on another. And it gets worse: sometimes the thing you're describing isn't even a plan for an existing chip — it's a description of a *new chip you want built*, and the compiler's job is to emit the actual circuit design. The chapter title, 'Describing hardware so a machine can build it,' points at that most ambitious end of the spectrum, where the same discipline of turning a high-level wish into low-level reality is aimed not at running software on a chip but at manufacturing the chip itself.

And there is a final twist that keeps the field from ever being 'solved': the wish is increasingly not fixed. Older programs did the same steps regardless of the input. Modern AI does not — a language model's work depends on how long your sentence is, a model that processes a network or a map does different work depending on the shape of that network, and a program in the field might take one branch on Tuesday's data and another on Wednesday's. The plan can't be baked once and reused blindly; it sometimes has to be chosen, or adjusted, at the last second when the real data shows up. Planning under that uncertainty — committing to an efficient route before you fully know the terrain — is the frontier that several of the most recent and confident papers here are pushing on.

the approachesHow the field actually tackles it

1.Rearranging the math so fewer numbers have to move (fusion and reuse)

This is the most fundamental move, the one everything else builds on. Take the sequence of operations the user asked for and rewrite it into an equivalent sequence that touches the far-away warehouse of memory as little as possible. The two workhorse tricks are fusion — gluing back-to-back operations together so an intermediate result stays on the fast workbench instead of being shipped to memory and immediately shipped back — and reuse — arranging the work so that once a number is on the bench, every operation that needs it happens before it leaves. The result of the math is identical; only the amount of hauling changes, and the hauling was the expensive part all along.

The key difficulty is knowing what is *safe* to fuse or reuse. If operation B secretly depends on operation A, you can't reorder them; if two chunks of work don't actually depend on each other, you can run them at the same time. Compilers figure this out by tracking dependencies — who needs whose output. The subtle insight in this cluster is that the *granularity* at which you track dependencies matters enormously: too fine-grained and the bookkeeping is so tangled you can't see any opportunities; too coarse and you miss real ones. asplos-2025-036 tracks dependencies at the level of tile-sized blocks — between single numbers and whole operations — and that middle grain simultaneously untangles the bookkeeping and exposes hidden chances to run things in parallel, winning up to nearly 3x over production tools. hpca-2025-069 plays the same game for small chips with tiny scratch shelves, working out exactly how many operations deep it can fuse before the scratch space overflows and forces a costly trip to main memory. asplos-2025-020 attacks the layout of the intermediate grids across a whole chain of multiplications at once, rather than optimizing each multiplication in isolation, because a locally good choice can be globally bad.

2.Splitting one giant computation across many chips (partitioning)

Some AI computations are simply too big to fit on one chip — the model has more numbers than any single chip can hold, or the work would take too long alone. So you spread it across dozens, hundreds, or thousands of chips wired together. But now the data-movement problem gets a new, more painful layer: numbers don't just travel from warehouse to workbench, they travel across the *network* from one chip to another, which is slower still. How you cut the work up decides how much of this cross-chip traffic you incur, and a bad cut can drown the whole cluster in communication.

There are a few natural ways to cut. You can give each chip a different slice of the data and have them all run the same steps (split the data). You can give each chip a different piece of the model and pass the data through them like an assembly line (split the model). You can split the bookkeeping that tracks how the model is learning. Each choice trades off differently, and the truly good plans *combine* several at once — which is a combinatorial nightmare, because the number of ways to slice and mix explodes. asplos-2025-013 makes these strategies composable: you apply one cut, then another, then another, each as a clean rewrite of the computation's description, so you can reason about, check, and mix them predictably instead of tangling them into the model's code. asplos-2025-099 is a community contest that lays bare just how hard picking the best cut for a single operation is, and how far current automatic methods still are from optimal — an honest admission that this remains open.

3.Searching for the fastest plan instead of deriving it (auto-tuning and cost models)

For any real computation there are astronomically many valid plans — different orders, different tile sizes, different assignments of work to different parts of the chip. There is usually no formula that hands you the best one, because 'best' depends on the messy physical details of a specific chip. So a large branch of the field simply *searches*: generate many candidate plans, estimate how each would perform, keep the promising ones, refine, repeat. The catch is that actually running each candidate on real hardware to time it is far too slow when there are millions of candidates, so the field builds a 'cost model' — a fast predictor that guesses how well a plan will run without running it.

The cost model is the whole ballgame, and it is where the cleverness lives. A slow, accurate predictor makes the search take days; a fast, sloppy one sends the search chasing bad plans. asplos-2025-110 splits the difference with a draft-then-check scheme: a cheap, rough analysis quickly throws out obviously bad candidates, and only the survivors get the expensive accurate predictor — and it can adapt on the fly to a chip it hasn't seen before. The predictor itself is increasingly a learned model rather than a hand-written formula: micro-2025-021 uses a language model as the predictor and, cleverly, has it predict performance one digit at a time so it can estimate for computations far larger than anything it was trained on, and correct itself from real feedback when a program's behavior shifts with its input. isca-2025-083 blends a hand-written physics-based model with a learned model that patches up the residual errors, getting both the interpretability of the formula and the accuracy of learning. asplos-2025-117 makes a sharp point that most tuners quietly assume a quiet, dedicated machine — and then break in a shared cloud where noisy neighbors distort every measurement — and fixes it by racing candidate plans against each other simultaneously so they all suffer the same interference.

4.Choosing the plan at the last second, when the data's shape is finally known (dynamic and input-adaptive compilation)

Classic compilers do all their thinking ahead of time, once, and produce a fixed plan. That works when the work is the same every run. But modern AI often isn't: a language model's workload depends on how long your prompt is, a model over a network or a 3D scene depends on the shape of that specific input, and you genuinely do not know those shapes until the moment the real data arrives. A plan optimized for one shape can be badly wrong for another. So this cluster pushes decision-making later — sometimes all the way to runtime — while still doing as much preparation as possible in advance.

The tension is real: deciding at the last second lets you fit the plan to the actual data, but you have almost no time to decide, so the decision itself must be nearly free. asplos-2025-075 carries the notion of a not-yet-known shape as a first-class idea all the way through compilation, so that optimizations which normally need concrete sizes can still fire on symbolic ones, and the same program can then be shipped to phones, browsers, and tiny devices. asplos-2025-001 profiles a few cheap statistics about an incoming graph — how connected it is, how sparse — and picks a tailored plan on the spot, because for these graph computations no single fixed plan generalizes across the wildly different shapes real graphs take. asplos-2025-090 turns the problem of finding repeated work to reuse into a matter of reshuffling rows and columns, which unlocks a far larger space of reuse opportunities that only make sense once you can see the actual data. asplos-2025-160 discovers, at runtime, that a stream of tasks is secretly repeating itself and memorizes the analysis so it isn't redone — automatically, with no hints from the programmer.

5.Describing a chip in one high-level language and generating both the hardware and the code (hardware generation and unified abstractions)

This is the most literal reading of the chapter's title. Here the compiler's output is not a plan for running software on an existing chip — it is the design of a *new chip*, or a custom circuit, emitted automatically from a high-level description of what that circuit should compute. Designing chips by hand, in the low-level language of circuits, is extraordinarily slow and error-prone, and a chip built for one AI operation is useless for the next. The dream is to describe the computation once, at a human level, and have a machine derive the actual hardware.

The hard part is that the low-level truth of hardware — exactly which wire connects to which, cycle by cycle — is precisely the tangle that makes hand-design miserable, and a naive automatic translation produces bloated, wasteful circuits. hpca-2025-002 introduces a clean mathematical way to describe how data should flow through a spatial arrangement of arithmetic units, deliberately separating the *control* logic (the traffic signals) from the *data* logic (the roads), and from that description it generates efficient, ready-to-manufacture circuits for any dataflow, cutting the wasteful control overhead by several-fold. A closely related pain is that hardware teams normally maintain two separate descriptions of the same design — a fast fake for experimentation and the real circuit — which drift out of sync; isca-2025-111 and asplos-2025-153 collapse that to a single description that yields both, aligned by construction. isca-2025-014 does the same for an unusual style of computing based on very long patterns of numbers, letting one program run on ordinary chips and on purpose-built accelerators alike. This cluster tends to carry the field's highest-confidence, most concrete results, because 'we generated a working chip' is hard to fake.

6.Classic compiler craft, sharpened for a new era

Underneath the AI-specific work sits the older, deeper discipline of compiler construction, and several papers here advance it directly — because these fundamentals still decide how fast the final code runs. The recurring theme is finding hidden mathematical structure in a problem that looks hopeless and exploiting it. asplos-2025-021 tackles register allocation — the puzzle of which handful of ultra-fast on-chip slots should hold which values at each moment, since there are far fewer slots than values and a bad choice forces slow spills to memory. This puzzle is provably intractable in general, but the paper shows that real programs' control structure has a special sparse shape, and solving the puzzle on that shape is fast. asplos-2025-023 speeds up 'equality saturation,' a technique that packs an enormous number of equivalent ways to write an expression into one compact structure and then picks the best — a discrete, brittle search that the paper makes smooth and gradient-friendly so it can even use learned notions of cost.

Other papers here re-examine the plumbing and the measurement itself. asplos-2025-096 notices that programs declare wide number formats out of habit when the actual values are usually tiny, and speculatively uses narrower, cheaper formats — falling back only on the rare overflow — to save energy on the smallest devices. asplos-2025-034 and asplos-2025-115 apply compiler analysis to security and testing rather than speed, tagging code with metadata or working out the minimal state that must be reset between test runs. asplos-2025-019 is a healthy reminder that even our *measurements* of program performance can quietly drift out of alignment with reality, which matters because every optimization in this whole theme is judged by such measurements. micro-2025-089 attacks the fact that simulating a hardware design accurately is punishingly slow, delivering near-fast-simulation speed at near-real-hardware accuracy for tricky designs.

7.Compiling for genuinely strange machines (quantum and other exotic hardware)

The compiler's mandate — turn a high-level wish into concrete low-level operations a specific machine can perform — extends to machines that work nothing like a normal computer, and here the physics driving the plan is completely different. On these machines the scarce, expensive resource isn't trips to a memory warehouse; it's *fidelity* — the tendency of the machine's fragile state to decay a little with every operation and especially with every physical movement, so that the whole answer degrades if you do too much. The compiler's job becomes minimizing the operations and movements that erode the answer, which is the same discipline of 'do less of the expensive thing' aimed at an entirely different cost.

Quantum machines that trap individual atoms, for instance, have to physically shuttle those atoms between different zones to do different operations, and every shuttle costs fidelity. asplos-2025-118 and hpca-2025-059 both compile with atom movement as a first-class concern — hpca-2025-059 borrows the idea of *reuse* straight from ordinary compilers, keeping an atom in place if it's about to be needed again rather than shuttling it away and back. micro-2025-099 goes further and treats the machine's zones exactly like the memory hierarchy of a normal computer — near-and-fast versus far-and-slow — reusing the decades-old logic of which item to evict from the fast tier. hpca-2025-098 pushes optimization down into the bit-level structure of the multiply-and-add circuit itself. What ties this cluster to the rest of the theme is not the hardware but the discipline: describe the computation abstractly, then find the plan that pays the machine's dominant cost as few times as possible.

where it standsWhat's solved, what's open

The bread-and-butter of this theme — rearranging tensor math to move fewer numbers through fusion, reuse, and smart scheduling — is genuinely mature and delivering real, large speedups in production tools, and the newest work is squeezing out the last opportunities by tracking dependencies at cleverer granularities and by driving several types of arithmetic unit at once. The strongest, most confident results cluster around hardware generation and simulation: automatically emitting efficient, manufacturable circuits from a high-level description, and collapsing the wasteful two-codebase design process into one, are working well enough to beat hand-crafted baselines on real chips. The honest gaps are all on the moving targets. Choosing the best way to split a huge model across many chips remains, by the field's own admission via its contests, far from solved and largely combinatorial guesswork. Compiling for computations whose shape and control flow only reveal themselves at runtime is an active frontier where the fixed-plan assumptions of classical compilers break, and the last-second decisions are still crude. Cost models — the fast predictors the whole search-based half of the field leans on — are getting better with learned methods but still struggle to generalize to unseen hardware and out-of-range problem sizes without online correction. And a meta-worry hangs over everything: as one paper here bluntly warns, even our measurements of what is fast can drift out of alignment with reality, so some reported wins rest on shakier ground than they appear. The field is powerful and improving fast, but it is chasing hardware and workloads that are themselves changing underneath it.

DarwinGame: Playing Tournaments for Tuning Applications in Noisy Cloud Environments

What it does

When you rent a virtual machine on a public cloud service like AWS, other customers' workloads are running on the same physical hardware at the same time, creating unpredictable interference — some runs of your application take 300 seconds, the next run 500 seconds, through no change of your own. Performance tuning tools (which try out many combinations of configuration settings to find the fastest one) assume a quiet, consistent environment; in a noisy cloud, they end up recommending settings that happened to look fast on a lucky low-interference run rather than settings that are genuinely fast. DarwinGame solves this by running multiple competing configurations simultaneously on the same machine, so they all experience the same interference level, and judging them by their relative performance rather than their absolute time.

The method, in detail

The idea. You can't control the noise in a cloud environment, but you can neutralize it by exposing every competing configuration to identical noise at the same moment. If configuration A and configuration B run concurrently on the same VM, whatever interference the cloud inflicts hits both equally — so whichever finishes more work in that window is genuinely faster. DarwinGame calls each such concurrent run a 'game' and organizes many games into a structured tournament.

How it works. The search space of configuration knobs (millions of combinations for Redis, GROMACS, or FFmpeg) is first divided into 10,000 regions. Within each region, a Swiss-style tournament phase runs: in each round, the top-scoring configurations from earlier rounds compete against new untested ones. 'Score' is a work-done fraction — how much of the application's total work (requests processed, frames encoded, simulation steps completed) did this configuration finish relative to the fastest configuration in the same game window? Games in early rounds can terminate early if one configuration pulls ahead by more than 10%, saving time. Regional winners advance to a global double-elimination phase, where losers get a second-chance bracket because a good configuration might have been unlucky with noisy co-location partners. In this phase, configurations are scored on both execution speed and consistency — the average of their reciprocal rank across all games they've played — so that settings which are fast but erratic are filtered out. Top survivors from the global phase play a barrage-style playoff (progressive head-to-head knockouts), and the final two configurations play a deciding game.

The key design choice. Running configurations side-by-side on the same VM is the core mechanism. Prior tuners tested configurations sequentially, so configuration A might see heavy interference at minute 1 and configuration B might see light interference at minute 2 — the comparison is unfair. Co-location makes the comparison fair by construction.

The result. Tested on AWS with Redis, GROMACS, FFmpeg, and LAMMPS, DarwinGame's chosen configuration runs within 4.2% of the optimal (found by exhaustive testing in a quiet, dedicated environment), while the next-best tuner (BLISS, a Bayesian optimization tool) produces configurations that run more than 40% slower than optimal. DarwinGame's winning configuration also shows a coefficient of variation (run-to-run variation) of only 0.46%, versus more than 6% for all competing solutions.

LEGO: Spatial Accelerator Generation and Optimization for Tensor Applications

What it does

Building a custom AI chip accelerator normally requires expert engineers to hand-write hardware description code for every new type of math operation — a slow and limiting process. LEGO is a software framework that takes a mathematical description of a tensor computation (like a matrix multiply or a convolution), automatically figures out the best wiring between processing elements, and outputs synthesizable hardware description code (a chip blueprint) without any hand-written templates. It achieves 3.2x higher throughput and 2.4x better energy efficiency than Gemmini, a leading open-source AI accelerator generator.

The method, in detail

The idea. A spatial accelerator for AI is essentially a grid of small multiply-accumulate units (called functional units, or FUs) connected by wires, with small memory buffers at various levels. The key design question is: which FUs should be directly wired together to pass data, and which should read from a shared buffer? Getting this right determines whether a unit sits idle waiting for data. Prior tools either fill in sizing numbers in a fixed template (rigid and limited to a few patterns) or entangle the data movement with the control flow (loop counters, address generators), creating 2x area overhead in control logic alone.

How LEGO represents the problem. LEGO describes both the math and the hardware mapping as affine transformations — essentially matrix multiplications on index vectors. For a matrix multiply Y[i,j] = X[i,k] * W[k,j], one matrix maps computation indices (i,j,k) to data indices, and another maps computation indices to which FU executes which iteration. Crucially, separating these two matrices decouples the data access pattern (what data a FU needs) from the execution schedule (when each FU runs). This makes it straightforward to ask: 'do two adjacent FUs ever need the same data element at the same time?' If the answer is yes, wire them together directly; no shared memory needed.

Front end: wiring and memory. LEGO solves a linear equation to find all pairs of FUs that reuse the same data. It then runs a minimum spanning tree algorithm (Chu-Liu/Edmonds) to keep only the minimum set of wires that ensures every FU is covered, minimizing expensive delay FIFOs. When multiple operation types (e.g., convolution and attention) must share one chip, a breadth-first search heuristic merges their wiring plans efficiently. Memory bank counts are set just large enough to prevent two FUs from trying to read the same bank simultaneously.

Back end: optimizing the chip blueprint. The front end produces a functional-unit-level graph. The back end expands every node into primitive operations (adders, registers, multiplexers) and then runs three linear-programming passes: insert the minimum number of pipeline registers needed for timing, rewire broadcast trees into forwarding chains to further reduce registers, and merge underused adder-tree input pins across dataflow modes. The result is synthesizable RTL (hardware description code ready for chip fabrication tools).

The result. On a suite of neural networks including LLaMA-7B and Stable Diffusion, the automatically generated chip achieves 3.2x speedup and 2.4x energy savings over Gemmini, and its area and power are comparable to hand-designed chips like Eyeriss.

Choco-Q: Commute Hamiltonian-based QAOA for Constrained Binary Optimization

What it does

Many hard real-world problems — assigning delivery routes, coloring maps, splitting networks into groups — can be framed as 'constrained binary optimization': find the best yes/no assignment to a set of variables while satisfying exact equality rules. Quantum computers offer a way to search many candidate solutions in parallel, but existing quantum algorithms either ignore the rules or encode them so badly that the success rate is near zero (as low as 0.02%). Choco-Q redesigns the quantum search so the rules are guaranteed to be satisfied on every step, pushing the success rate from fractions of a percent up to 67% on medium problems and slashing total run time by 4.69x.

The method, in detail

The idea. Quantum optimization algorithms (called QAOA — Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithms) work by preparing a superposition of all possible answers and then nudging the quantum state toward good solutions. They do this using two ingredients: a 'cost Hamiltonian' that rewards good objective values, and a 'driver Hamiltonian' that steers the search. The constraint problem is: how do you steer without ever leaving the region where the rules are satisfied?

The core insight. In quantum mechanics, there is a principle (from Heisenberg's picture of physics) that says: if a mathematical operator commutes with the driver Hamiltonian — meaning the two operators multiply to the same result regardless of order — then whatever quantity that operator measures stays constant throughout the search. Choco-Q constructs the driver Hamiltonian to commute with the constraint operator, guaranteeing that the constraints remain perfectly satisfied on every step of the search. This is called the commute Hamiltonian approach and it handles any linear equality constraint, not just the narrow 'sum equals constant' format that prior methods required.

Three optimizations for real hardware. Naively, this commute Hamiltonian is exponentially expensive to decompose into actual quantum gates. Choco-Q applies three successive reductions: (1) Serialization — the global commute Hamiltonian is split into smaller independent local pieces; the paper proves this split still preserves the constraint. (2) Equivalent decomposition — each local piece is transformed into simple 'phase gates' and 'flip gates' using a linear-time algorithm, cutting circuit depth from roughly 4,000 down to 24. (3) Variable elimination — the most 'expensive' variable is algebraically removed and its two possible values are tested separately, shrinking the circuit further from 24 to 14 gates at the cost of one extra measurement.

The result. On IBM's real 159-qubit quantum processor (Fez), Choco-Q achieves 100% constraint satisfaction and a 2.65x better success rate versus the prior best, with 4.69x lower end-to-end latency. The decomposition step that used to time out or require 41 GB of memory now finishes in under 0.1 seconds with under 10 MB.

HPVM-HDC: A Heterogeneous Programming System for Accelerating Hyperdimensional Computing

What it does

Hyperdimensional Computing (HDC) is an alternative to deep neural networks that represents data as long random-looking vectors and classifies them through lightweight similarity math — it is fast, energy-efficient, and naturally forgiving of errors. The problem is that writing HDC programs today means rewriting them from scratch in a different language for every hardware target: CUDA for GPUs, C for FPGAs, custom APIs for specialized chips. HPVM-HDC solves this by introducing a single higher-level language (HDC++) and a compiler that automatically translates one program into efficient code for CPUs, GPUs, and two real HDC accelerator chips.

The method, in detail

The idea. HDC encodes data as hypervectors — vectors with thousands of elements (e.g. 2048-dimensional). Classification works by encoding a query, then measuring how similar it is to stored class prototypes using operations like Hamming distance (counting positions where two binary vectors differ) or cosine similarity (the angle between two vectors). These are all simple, massively parallel, and noise-tolerant — a perfect fit for dedicated hardware.

HDC++ language. The authors created a C++ dialect with 24 built-in operations for hypervector math: create, encode, multiply, Hamming-distance, cosine-similarity, argmin, and so on. Crucially, three loop primitives — encoding_loop, training_loop, inference_loop — describe entire HDC phases in one line. These coarse-grained descriptions are what the compiler needs to target specialized chips that expose only high-level commands like 'run inference on this chip.'

HPVM-HDC compiler. The compiler translates HDC++ into an intermediate representation (a tree of parallel tasks with explicit data edges) extended with HDC-specific operations. From there, four backends take over: a CPU backend emits multithreaded C with SIMD vector extensions; a GPU backend maps HDC operations directly to optimized NVIDIA libraries (cuBLAS for matrix multiply, Thrust for reductions, or custom CUDA programs); and two accelerator backends map the coarse encoding/training/inference loops to the chip's native function calls, eliminating unnecessary data shuffles between host and chip.

Two approximation tricks. Because HDC is error-tolerant by design, HPVM-HDC applies two optional speedups. Automatic binarization: the compiler traces which hypervectors pass through a sign() operation (which maps every value to +1 or -1) and automatically shrinks those vectors to 1-bit representations throughout the program — reducing data movement and enabling bitwise-AND math instead of floating-point. Reduction perforation: similarity computations (Hamming distance, cosine, matrix multiply) can skip elements along their inner loop — either every other element (strided) or just the first half (segmented) — because the relative ranking between classes stays correct even with an approximate count.

The result. On CPUs and GPUs the compiler-generated code matches or beats hand-written CUDA baselines (geometric mean 1.17x speedup on GPU). Approximation can add up to 3.4x GPU inference speedup with negligible accuracy loss. Most importantly, HPVM-HDC is the first system to run complete HDC applications on a physical 40nm digital HDC chip and a simulated resistive-RAM HDC accelerator — tasks that had no baseline implementation before this work. Code is 1.6x shorter than equivalent target-specific versions across all platforms.

LLMulator: Generalizable Cost Modeling for Dataflow Accelerators with Input-Adaptive Control Flow

What it does

When chip designers want to know how fast a specialized processor (like a Google TPU or AI accelerator chip) will run a given program, they currently face a frustrating tradeoff: detailed chip simulators are accurate but very slow, while fast prediction models fail badly on programs or hardware they haven't seen before. LLMulator solves this by fine-tuning a pre-trained language model (the same kind used for text generation, specifically LLaMA-3.2-1B) to predict chip performance metrics — chip area, power, and execution time — achieving 12.2% average error across diverse programs and hardware configurations, and adapting in real time to programs whose behavior changes depending on input data.

The method, in detail

The idea. Predicting how long a program takes to run on a custom chip normally requires either running a detailed simulation (accurate but takes minutes per prediction) or training a machine-learning model on past examples (fast but fails on anything outside the training distribution). Three specific failure modes plague existing fast models: (1) programs with extreme loop sizes the model has never seen, (2) programs whose execution path changes depending on the input data (dynamic control flow), and (3) new hardware configurations not in the training set. LLMulator addresses all three.

How it works.

Part 1 — Numeric-sensitive encoding and digit-by-digit prediction. Standard language models treat a number like '1024' as a single opaque token, which breaks when a program has a loop boundary of 10,000 that the model was never trained on. LLMulator splits every number into individual digit characters before tokenizing ('1', '0', '2', '4'), so the model sees numbers the way humans do — digit by digit. Prediction is then done as a sequence of digit classifications (picking each digit 0-9) with beam search to correct errors in early digits. This lets the model extrapolate to numerical ranges well outside training data and also produces a confidence score for each prediction.

Part 2 — Online learning for input-dependent behavior.
