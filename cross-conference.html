One loop, cut into three fields

A machine that does anything in the physical world runs the same tight circle over and over. It takes in the world as it is, works out what to do, moves to do it, and then looks again to see what changed. We have given the pieces of that circle different names and different conferences, but they are not different subjects. Computer vision is the machine opening its eyes. Robotics is the machine closing the circle, adding the decision and the muscle that vision alone never had. Hardware is the floor the whole circle stands on, the silicon that every glance and every move has to run through.

The three research fields line up almost exactly with the three parts of the loop. Vision asks how to turn flat pictures into an understanding of shape, motion, and meaning. Robotics takes that understanding and asks the next two questions vision never had to answer: given what I see, what should I do, and how do I make my body do it. Hardware asks the question underneath both: whatever you decided to compute, what will it actually cost to move the numbers around fast enough to matter. Read the three together and they stop being a reading list and become a single machine described from three angles.

Vision is the eye, and it already leans toward acting

The vision field frames itself as a two-way bridge between pixels and meaning: reading meaning out of pixels, and writing pixels back from meaning. Most of its work is the reading direction. Pulling 3D shape out of a flat photo, naming and locating what is in a scene, following things as they move through a video, joining what is seen to language so the machine can describe and answer and reason about a picture. This is exactly the sense stage of the loop, done as well as anyone knows how to do it.

What is striking is that the vision field, left to its own devices, keeps drifting toward the rest of the loop. One of its own themes is using vision to act in the world, the path from seeing to deciding to moving. That is robotics phrased in a vision accent. The eye does not want to stay an eye. The moment a system can reliably read the world, the obvious next thing to do with that reading is to act on it, and the boundary between the two fields turns out to be a convenience, not a real seam.

Robotics is the same eye, plus a body and a decision

Robotics states the relationship plainly: sense the world, decide what to do, move to do it, where vision is the sense stage and robotics adds the two stages vision skips. And its themes fall neatly into those buckets. Cheap fast reliable seeing and leaner self-driving perception are the sense layer, the same problem vision works on but now bound by a small onboard computer and cruel deadlines. Language models that plan the job and turning describing AI into doing AI are the decide layer. Teaching legs and hands to move, planning motion in real time, and handling things by feel are the act layer.

Two things become clear once the loop is closed. First, closing it is harder than any single stage, because now the machine must be trusted, which is why safety and being sure it will not fail is a theme all its own once learned parts enter the control path. Second, the real bottleneck in robotics is not cleverness but examples: teaching a robot by demonstration needs mountains of hand-collected data, and gathering it on real hardware is slow and expensive. That single fact is what pulls the whole field toward the same rescue that transformed vision, which brings us to the fabric they share.

The shared fabric: one architecture, and models that carry knowledge across the seam

The deepest reason these fields are one machine is that they have converged on the same building material. The transformer, and attention as its core move, sits under vision and under robot planning alike. Once the eye and the planner are made of the same stuff, an idea proven in one place can be poured directly into the other, and that is exactly what is happening. Foundation models, the large pretrained models that learned the shape of the world from oceans of images and text, grew up in vision and are now moving into robot policies. A model that learned to look at a scene and describe it becomes the seed of a model that looks at a scene and decides what to do in it.

The crossing is not free, and the friction is itself instructive. The vision-language models are strong at naming and describing but weak at judging distance and 3D layout, the very things a body needs to act. So robotics is not importing vision wholesale, it is repairing exactly the parts that only matter once you have to move. The transfer of knowledge from the seeing world into the doing world is the current frontier of both fields at once, and it is possible only because they finally speak the same architectural language.

Silicon is the floor, and its one hard limit is moving data

All of it runs on chips, and the hardware field reduces to a single surprising truth: arithmetic is nearly free, and the real cost is moving data. Hauling weights and the running memory of a model out of storage and across wires is what burns the time and the energy. Every hardware theme is one of three moves against that cost: make the data smaller before it travels, carry it a shorter distance, or push the computation over to where the data already sits. Compression, low precision, and skipping the work that does not matter shrink what moves. Smarter memory and near-data processing shorten the trip. Redesigning the wires between chips hides the trip underneath other work.

This floor sets the ceiling for everything above it. A robot has to run its whole sense-decide-act loop on a small computer, fast enough to react before the world moves on, which is a data-movement budget stated in the language of a moving body. A self-driving stack that builds a heavy full picture of everything around the car is flagged as too costly precisely because moving and processing all that data will not fit the budget. When hardware makes data cheaper to move, the loop can run faster, cheaper, and in more places. When it cannot, no amount of cleverness in vision or robotics gets off the lab bench.

The pressure that binds all three: do more with less

There is one force felt in every layer, and it is what finally welds the three fields into a single story. Vision names it directly, learning more from less and not breaking, its push toward models that adapt, shrink, and stay robust, using techniques like distillation to pack a big model's skill into a small one. Robotics feels the same pressure as its data bottleneck and its onboard-compute limits, the demand to learn from fewer examples and run on a smaller brain. Hardware is nothing but this pressure made physical, the relentless drive to move less data for the same result.

So the efficiency squeeze is not three separate problems that happen to rhyme. It is one problem viewed from three heights. The machine has to sense, decide, and act inside a real budget of time, energy, and money, and every field is chipping at that same budget from its own side, using tools that increasingly cross over. Seen this way, computer vision, robotics, and AI hardware are not neighbors who occasionally collaborate. They are the top, the middle, and the foundation of one machine that reads the world, acts on it, and pays for every bit of both in the currency of moved data.

The through-lines that cut across all three

The clearest sign these are one field: the same problems and the same machinery keep showing up in all three at once.

01  The transformer is the shared nervous tissue

▸ every piece weighs every other

The shared problem. All three fields converged on one computational primitive: attention, the operation that lets every piece of data weigh every other piece and decide what matters. Vision, robot policies, and silicon all now organize themselves around this single pattern rather than field-specific machinery. Because the machine is shared, an advance in one field is directly transplantable to the others.

In vision — Transformers and attention replaced hand-built vision pipelines; the same block reads images, video, and language, listed explicitly among CVPR's core engines.
In robotics — Robot planners and 'one flexible tool for both seeing and acting' policies are attention-based sequence models that treat sensing and acting as one token stream.
In hardware — An entire theme (T1: Attention and the KV-Cache) exists only because attention is now THE workload chips are built to feed.

Where they meet. Because all three speak transformer, a trick discovered for one (e.g. cheaper attention) instantly serves the others; hardware optimizes the exact operation vision and robotics depend on.

02  Foundation models flowing from seeing to talking to doing

▸ one base model, specialized many ways

The shared problem. Instead of training a narrow model per task, all three fields now start from one large pre-trained model and specialize it. The root problem is that task-specific systems are brittle and expensive to build; a general model that already 'understands' the world can be pointed at many jobs. The frontier is watching this general competence spread outward from perception into action.

In vision — Vision-language/foundation models are a named engine; one model describes, answers, and reasons across pixels and words.
In robotics — Two themes trace the migration: language models that plan the job, and 'turning seeing-and-talking AI into doing AI' (vision-language-action policies) that make a describing model actually move a robot.
In hardware — Sparsity and Mixture-of-Experts (T5) plus serving schedules (T9) exist to make these giant general models affordable to run at all.

Where they meet. Robotics borrows vision-language models wholesale and bolts on action; vision borrows language; hardware reshapes itself around serving the one big model that all of them now share.

03  Moving less data is the real cost, everywhere

▸ hauling data is the real cost

The shared problem. In every field the expensive part is not the thinking but the hauling: getting data to where computation happens, fast enough and cheaply enough. Arithmetic is nearly free; memory traffic, bandwidth, and power are what actually bind. Each field spends enormous effort making its models smaller and its data movement shorter.

In vision — Distillation is a named engine and a whole theme is 'learning more from less, and shrinking' so models fit real deployment budgets.
In robotics — 'Cheap, fast, reliable seeing' and 'self-driving done leaner' are about running perception on the robot's own small computer without a heavy top-down world model.
In hardware — This IS the field's stated single constraint: compression, quantization, near-data processing, interconnect, and sparsity are all ways to move less data a shorter distance.

Where they meet. Vision's distillation and quantization tricks become hardware's low-precision formats; hardware's memory limits dictate what vision and robotics can deploy, so all three co-design toward the same shrink-and-localize goal.

04  Learning from cheap or imagined data instead of expensive reality

▸ few real examples, many made

The shared problem. Real labeled examples are the true bottleneck: collecting them by hand is slow, costly, and sometimes impossible. So all three fields manufacture their training signal, generate it, simulate it, or synthesize it, rather than paying for real-world collection. The shared risk is the gap between the cheap fake data and the real world it must transfer to.

In vision — Generative diffusion makes pixels from meaning, and self-/weak-supervision lets models 'learn more from less' without exhaustive labels.
In robotics — 'Making training examples cheaper' is explicitly called the field's real bottleneck; skills are learned inside a simulated computer, then transferred to the real robot.
In hardware — Machines now design chips automatically (T6) and honest evaluation (T0) exists to check whether cheaply-obtained gains are real, not artifacts of the shortcut.

Where they meet. Vision's generative models become robotics' simulators and data factories; the sim-to-real transfer problem in robotics is the same distribution-gap problem vision fights in synthetic-to-real.

05  Generative and world models: imagining the future to act on it

▸ imagine the future to act on it

The shared problem. To decide well you must predict what happens next, so all three fields build models that generate plausible futures, whole images, video rollouts, or world states, rather than only classifying the present. The common root is that a system that can imagine consequences can plan, fill gaps, and reconstruct what it hasn't directly seen.

In vision — Diffusion generates images and video (seeing and making things that move); Gaussian splatting reconstructs and re-renders full 3D scenes.
In robotics — Robots rebuild and store the 3D world in one shared representation, and planners simulate rollouts to pick actions before committing to a move.
In hardware — The heavy generative rollout is precisely the compute-bound, memory-bound workload that quantization, sparsity, and serving schedules are built to sustain.

Where they meet. Robotics adopts vision's Gaussian-splatting and video-generation as its world model and 3D memory; both then hand hardware the same iterative generative workload to accelerate.

06  Closing the loop from pixels to decision to motion

▸ pixels → decision → motion → repeat

The shared problem. None of the fields wants a passive observer; the goal is a full loop, sense the world, decide what to do, act, and feel the result. The hard part is that these stages were historically built by separate communities with separate representations, and stitching them into one fast, coherent loop is where systems break.

In vision — 'Using vision to act in the world' is a named theme, vision explicitly framed as the sense stage that feeds decision.
In robotics — Robotics IS the loop by definition (sense to decide to move), adding force feedback, real-time motion planning, and safety guarantees on top of vision.
In hardware — Security and reliability (T7) plus serving schedules keep the loop trustworthy and responsive under real load, since a control loop cannot pause.

Where they meet. Robotics consumes vision's perception as its front end and pushes back real-time and closed-loop demands; hardware co-designs to keep the whole sense-decide-act loop within its latency and power budget.

07  Real-time and on-device: the tyranny of the latency budget

▸ shrink to fit the latency budget

The shared problem. A model that is accurate but too slow or too power-hungry is useless in the situations these fields actually care about. All three are pinned by a hard clock and a small power envelope: the answer must arrive within a fixed budget, on the device at hand, or it does not count. This constraint, not raw accuracy, increasingly decides what ships.

In vision — Distillation and shrinking exist so models run fast enough on real, limited devices rather than only in the datacenter.
In robotics — 'Fast enough to run on its own small computer' and real-time motion planning make the deadline explicit; a jittery late plan is a failed plan.
In hardware — Energy accounting and fair serving under millions of users (T9), plus near-data processing, are all in service of meeting latency and power limits.

Where they meet. Robotics' hard deadlines and vision's on-device needs set the targets hardware designs to; hardware's efficiency wins in turn expand what vision and robotics can attempt within the same budget.
