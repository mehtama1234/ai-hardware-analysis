When chips must talk to chips

the wiring and messages between them
43 papers · 5 read in full · 38 from the abstract

the problemThe problem, in depth

Start with a single fact that almost nobody outside the field appreciates: a modern computer chip can do arithmetic almost unimaginably fast. A good chip can add or multiply billions of pairs of numbers every second, and a room full of them can do that trillions of times over. If the only thing that mattered were doing sums, we would have run out of interesting problems long ago. But that is not where the time goes. In practice, the machine spends most of its life not calculating, but waiting for numbers to arrive. The arithmetic is cheap; getting the right numbers to the right place at the right moment is the expensive part. This chapter is about that one root problem, and everything in it is a variation on the same theme: computers today are limited by moving data around, not by doing math on it.

To feel why this is true, picture what a number actually is inside a machine. It is a pattern of tiny electrical charges sitting in one specific physical spot: a spot on a chip, or in a memory stick, or in another machine across the room. To use that number, the chip has to physically pull the charge pattern out of where it lives and carry it, as electrical signals down metal wires, to the little circuit that does the addition. Charges do not teleport. They travel down a wire at a real, finite speed, and every wire has a limited width, meaning only so many numbers can flow through it per second. That width-per-second is called 'bandwidth', and the travel time is called 'latency'. Both are set by physics and by how far apart things are. The closer two things sit, the fatter and shorter the wire between them can be, so the faster they can talk. The farther apart, the thinner and longer the pipe, and the longer the wait. Distance, on a chip, is destiny.

Now scale this up, because the workloads that dominate today, the giant pattern-learning programs behind modern AI, are far too big to fit on any single chip. A single one of these programs holds hundreds of billions of internal numbers (its learned 'settings', the knobs it tuned during training). No one chip can hold them all, nor could one chip crunch through them in a reasonable time. So the work is spread across many chips, often thousands of them, each holding a slice of the problem. And here is the trap: the moment you split the work across chips, those chips must constantly tell each other what they found. Chip 3 computes a partial result that Chip 4 needs before it can take its next step. Multiply that by thousands of chips and billions of numbers, repeated over and over, and you get a torrent of cross-chip conversation. The chips that talk to chips.

The cruel arithmetic is that compute scales beautifully and communication does not. If you double the number of chips, you get roughly double the arithmetic power, that part is nearly free. But the amount of talking between them tends to grow faster, and the pipes connecting them do not magically get fatter. So as you add chips to go faster, a larger and larger fraction of every second is spent with expensive chips sitting idle, twiddling their thumbs, waiting for a message from a neighbor to show up. You can buy all the raw calculating power in the world and still see it stalled most of the time, blocked on data in transit. That is the bottleneck this whole theme attacks.

It gets worse at every scale, and the same problem wears different costumes. Inside a single chip, dozens of little cores share wires (a 'network-on-chip', literally a tiny road network etched into the silicon) and fight over them. Between chips packaged inches apart, the connecting wires are precious and few, because you can only fit so many physical links across the gap where two chips meet. Across a room, machines talk over networking cables and switches, the same basic technology as the internet, which adds its own delays as messages get wrapped, addressed, queued, and routed. At each level, the story repeats: the calculators are starving, and the supply lines are the constraint. When people say a system is 'communication-bound', they mean exactly this: make the wires faster and the whole machine speeds up; make the math faster and often nothing happens at all, because the math was never the thing holding it back.

There is one more twist that makes this genuinely hard rather than merely annoying. Communication and computation are not naturally cooperative. In the simplest design, a chip computes, then stops and sends its results, then waits to receive, then computes again, a stop-and-go rhythm where the chip is either calculating or talking but never both. Every second spent talking is a second of wasted calculating power. The holy grail of this entire field is to make the talking happen in the background, hidden underneath the calculating, so the chip never has to stop. That sounds simple and is fiendishly difficult, because you have to break the work into pieces that can be sent while other pieces are still being computed, without the pieces getting out of order or a chip acting on data that has not arrived yet. Much of the cleverness in this chapter is about winning that hidden battle: sending sooner, sending less, sending smarter, or building better roads so the sending costs less in the first place.

the approachesHow the field actually tackles it

1.Hide the talking underneath the calculating

The single most important idea in this field is deceptively simple: never let a chip sit idle waiting to talk. If a chip must send its results to a neighbor, arrange things so that while that message is traveling, the chip is already busy computing the next piece of work. The communication happens in the background, tucked underneath the arithmetic, so its cost mostly disappears. This is called 'overlap', and it is the closest thing this whole theme has to a universal goal.

The catch is that overlap does not happen by itself. In the naive way of doing things, a chip finishes a whole big chunk of work, then hands off the entire result at once, and only then starts the next chunk, an all-or-nothing handoff with nothing to do during the wait. To create overlap you have to slice the work into smaller pieces so that some pieces can be sent while other pieces are still cooking. Several papers automate exactly this slicing so a human does not have to hand-tune it: Concerto (asplos-2025-028) treats the whole scheduling puzzle, what to compute when, what to send when, as a planning problem and lets a solver find a near-optimal order automatically, regardless of how the work was split across chips. MeshSlice (isca-2025-082) and Chimera (isca-2025-090) chop the cross-chip messages into partial sends that can be interleaved with ongoing math, so most of the waiting vanishes behind useful work.

Making this easy for programmers is its own contribution. Writing overlapped code by hand is notoriously error-prone, because you are essentially juggling calculation and messaging in the same breath and must never let a chip act on data that has not landed yet. TileLink (mlsys-2025-002) gives programmers simple building blocks, 'this piece of data is ready, you may now use it', so they can express overlapping compute-and-send code in a couple hundred lines instead of a couple thousand lines of expert-level low-level code, while matching what the experts achieve by hand. The trade-off across all of these is complexity: overlap makes the machine much faster but the software much harder to get right, which is why so much effort goes into automating it rather than leaving it to humans.

2.Send less in the first place

The cheapest message is the one you never send. If communication is the bottleneck, an obvious lever is to reduce the sheer volume of data crossing the wires, either by cutting out redundant sends, by squeezing the numbers smaller, or by only sending the parts that actually matter.

One flavor is eliminating redundancy. When you combine several ways of splitting a job across chips, you often end up with the same information being shipped around more than once; Chimera (isca-2025-090) detects these duplicate conversations and fuses them into a single send that does the job of many. Another flavor is compression and selective sending: Radius (mlsys-2025-057) noticed that during training, the important updates a chip needs to share are mostly the same few from one step to the next, so instead of shipping every update, it ships only the handful that matter and reuses the pattern for a stretch of steps, cutting the data on the wire by nearly ninety percent. Frugal (asplos-2025-150) similarly shrinks and bundles the updates for recommendation-style workloads so they fit through the thin pipes of cheap, commodity hardware.

A subtler version is avoiding the redundant fetch. In systems built from many separate chip modules, several modules often want the very same number. NearFetch (hpca-2025-118) notices when a requested piece of data is already sitting on a module that a message happens to be passing through, and grabs it from there instead of traveling all the way to the distant source, more than halving the effective travel for high-sharing workloads. Push Multicast (hpca-2025-001) goes proactive: when one core asks for a shared piece of data, the memory guesses which other cores will want it too and pushes it to all of them at once, so they need not each ask separately. Delegato (micro-2025-081) tackles the small but painful case of chips fighting to update the same counter, by sending the tiny operation to wherever the data lives rather than dragging the data back and forth. The shared trade-off: sending less usually means spending extra cleverness figuring out what is safe to skip, and getting that judgment wrong can corrupt results, so these schemes lean heavily on being provably correct.

3.Match the message pattern to the shape of the wiring

Chips are physically wired together in some fixed layout, a grid, a ring, a tree, a mesh of short links between neighbors. The pattern of who-talks-to-whom in a given job may or may not fit that physical layout. When the two mismatch, messages take long detours, collide, and waste the pipes. A whole line of work redesigns the communication pattern so it flows naturally along the actual wires, like routing traffic to follow the streets that exist instead of wishing for streets that do not.

The most common heavy operation is one where every chip must combine its partial answer with every other chip's, then everyone ends up with the total. Done carelessly this floods the network. TidalMesh (hpca-2025-058) designs this operation from scratch for chips laid out in a flat grid, sending data in wave-like flows that ride the grid's rows and columns and use every link fully instead of forcing a ring-shaped conversation onto grid-shaped hardware. PIMnet (hpca-2025-080) builds a dedicated talking network for a special kind of hardware where the computing happens right inside the memory, matching the network's shape to how the memory is physically packaged, so these chips can combine results directly instead of routing everything through a central bottleneck, an up-to-eighty-five-fold speedup on the combining step.

The reverse move also appears: instead of reshaping the messages to fit the wires, reshape the wires to fit the messages. Ruche Networks (isca-2025-054) add a few long-range express links across an otherwise neighbor-only grid so distant chips can reach each other in fewer hops. EIGEN (hpca-2025-041) and FRED (isca-2025-093) build reconfigurable fabrics whose connections can be rearranged to suit whatever conversation the current job needs. Zettafly (isca-2025-027) designs a room-scale network layout that can be carved into isolated, non-colliding zones sized to match typical job sizes. The universal trade-off here is generality versus fit: a layout tuned perfectly for one pattern of talking is often mediocre for another, so these designs fight to stay flexible while still exploiting structure.

4.Split the work so the chips talk less

Before you optimize any message, you first chose how to carve the giant job into chip-sized pieces, and that choice largely determines how much talking is needed. Cut the job one way and neighboring chips must exchange mountains of data; cut it another way and they barely need to speak. This is the art of parallelism strategy, and it lives one level above the wires: it is about deciding what goes where so that expensive conversation is minimized before it ever starts.

There are several natural ways to divide the work, each with a different communication fingerprint. You can hand each chip a different stage of the pipeline (like an assembly line), a different slice of every calculation, or a different batch of the input. The papers here refine these cuts to shrink the talking. Mithril (hpca-2025-017) rethinks how to split a graph-learning job so that cross-chip exchange shrinks by a factor equal to the number of stages, turning a ten-fold communication blowup into a manageable one. GraphPipe (asplos-2025-063) notices that real programs are not simple straight lines but branching structures, and splits them along their true branches so independent branches run on different chips at once. MeshSlice (isca-2025-082) picks a two-dimensional way of splitting each calculation so the talking grows far more slowly as you add chips.

The subtle members of this family target one specific imbalance. In these learning systems, one particular layer, the part that maps words to numbers, is unusually lopsided, and if it lands on a single chip it stalls everyone. Vocabulary Parallelism (mlsys-2025-052) spreads that lopsided layer evenly across all chips and, cleverly, cuts the required synchronization from three coordination points down to one. DiTile-DGNN (isca-2025-119) splits a job over evolving graph snapshots while stripping out the parts that repeat between snapshots. The trade-off throughout is that the best way to split depends on the exact job and the exact machine, so a huge amount of work goes into automatically searching for the right cut rather than fixing one in advance.

5.Do work inside the network instead of only at the endpoints

Normally the network is just a dumb courier: it carries data from A to B and does nothing to it along the way. But if many chips are all sending numbers that need to be added together, you can add them up as they pass through the switches in the middle, so that only the final sum comes out the other end instead of every chip's contribution arriving separately. Making the network itself do a little arithmetic, 'in-network computation', can slash the data that reaches any single destination.

The payoff is largest for the combine-everything operations that dominate these workloads. Instead of every chip shipping its full partial answer to one place and that place doing all the adding, the switches along the path add up contributions as they flow together, so traffic shrinks at every merge point. FRED's wafer-scale fabric (isca-2025-093) builds this reduction directly into its tiny in-fabric switches. TRACI (isca-2025-048) tackles a harder case where the pattern of who-sends-what changes with every input: it tags messages so the switch hardware can spot, on the fly, when two chips are asking for or sending the same thing and combine them right there, rather than blindly forwarding everything.

A close cousin is putting logic in the network to coordinate and route smarter rather than to compute. EDM (asplos-2025-129) places a scheduler inside the network switch that hands out dedicated lanes to talkers so their messages never queue up behind each other. AccDirect (isca-2025-075) lets the network card hand work directly to a chip's load balancer without bothering the main processor, cutting the middleman out of the hot path entirely. The trade-off for all of this is that switches and network cards are simple, cheap, high-speed devices by design, and teaching them to do real work adds complexity and cost to the one part of the system that was supposed to stay lean, so these designs work hard to keep the added logic tiny.

6.Build faster physical roads between the chips

Underneath all the cleverness sits the raw physical link, the actual wires, connectors, and switches carrying the charges from one chip to another. If you can make those links physically fatter, shorter, or lower-overhead, everything above them speeds up for free. This approach attacks the bottleneck at its most fundamental layer: the road itself, not the traffic on it.

One front is the packaging, how chips are physically mounted and connected close together. As single chips hit their size limits, designers stitch many smaller chip-lets side by side on a shared base, or even spread them across a whole silicon wafer, so the links between them can be far shorter and fatter than cables across a room. EIGEN (hpca-2025-041) builds smart interconnect into the base layer that carries chiplets; FRED (isca-2025-093), the wafer-scale cabinet work (isca-2025-122), and Titan's co-design (isca-2025-122) push toward wiring an entire wafer's worth of chips into one dense fabric. Plaid (asplos-2025-077) attacks waste inside a single chip, where the little routers that shuttle data between compute units were eating nearly half the chip's power and area; it shares one lightweight router among several compute units, cutting that overhead dramatically.

Another front is the room-scale network stack, the layers of wrapping, addressing, and queuing that a message passes through when machines talk over standard networking gear. Each layer adds delay. EDM (asplos-2025-129) strips the remote-memory protocol down to the lowest electrical layer of standard networking hardware, skipping the usual processing layers to reach a few-hundred-nanosecond round trip, far faster than the normal path. Vela (asplos-2025-100) shows you can run demanding training on ordinary, shared cloud machines with standard networking and still hit around eighty percent of the speed of dedicated specialized gear, by letting chips talk directly to each other without routing through the host processor. The trade-off across this whole approach is cost and manufacturability: fatter, shorter, custom links are expensive and hard to build, which is exactly why several of these papers aim instead to squeeze the most out of cheap, commodity hardware.

7.Keep the machine healthy and fairly shared while it talks

A network that runs perfectly on paper still has to survive the real world: hardware fails, traffic collides, and multiple different jobs must share the same wires without trampling each other. This last group is about the operational reality of communication at scale, keeping the conversation flowing smoothly when thousands of chips are involved and something is always going slightly wrong.

At large scale, failure is constant, not exceptional. When a run spans thousands of chips, at any given moment something is probably broken, and a slow-to-detect fault can stall the entire fleet. C4 (hpca-2025-024) turns the regular, repeating rhythm of the chips' cross-talk into a health monitor: because every round of communication should look the same, any deviation is a fingerprint of a failing part, letting the system spot and isolate the bad component fast instead of waiting for a timeout. It also pre-plans the predictable heavy traffic so those big flows do not collide, recovering a solid chunk of lost efficiency.

The sharing problem is about carving one physical machine among many tenants. vNPU (isca-2025-033) lets several jobs share one AI chip while preserving each job's internal communication structure, so a shared chip does not force jobs to trip over each other's wiring. NetCrafter (isca-2025-032) shapes the traffic where fast internal links meet slower external ones, coalescing half-empty messages, dropping guesses that turned out unnecessary, and letting urgent messages jump the queue. Underpinning all of this is the unglamorous but essential ability to model these systems before building them: LEGOSim (micro-2025-072) simulates heterogeneous multi-chip systems fast enough to be usable, by letting each chip's simulation run independently and only synchronizing when they actually need to exchange a message, a direct echo of the same overlap principle that runs through this entire theme. The shared trade-off here is overhead versus robustness: monitoring, isolating, and fairly sharing all cost something, and the art is keeping that cost small enough that the cure is cheaper than the disease.

where it standsWhat's solved, what's open

The field has clearly won the first battle: on the well-behaved, predictable workloads, the giant regular training runs, the community now knows how to hide most of the communication underneath computation, how to split the work sensibly, and how to shape the heavy repeating traffic so it does not collide. Real systems now reach a respectable fraction of their theoretical peak even on ordinary commodity hardware and standard networking, which was not true a few years ago. The core insight, that you must design the talking and the calculating together rather than separately, is settled and widely applied, and much of what used to require hand-tuned expert code is now automated by compilers and solvers.

The honest gaps are all on the irregular and the unpredictable. When the pattern of who-talks-to-whom depends on the input and changes every step, as in recommendation and graph workloads, the clean overlap tricks break down and progress is still piecemeal. Doing real work inside the network is powerful but remains bespoke, tied to specific hardware and specific operations rather than being a general, portable capability. The physical layer, packaging many chips densely and wiring whole wafers, is advancing fast but is expensive, hard to manufacture, and hard to keep cool and reliable, so most of it is still closer to research than to commodity. And the whole field is chasing a moving target: every jump in the size of these AI programs shifts the balance and reopens questions that looked closed, because the arithmetic keeps getting cheaper while the wires stubbornly do not. The root problem, that computers are limited by moving data rather than by doing math, is not going away; it is being managed, workload by workload, one clever overlap at a time.

Enhancing CGRA Efficiency Through Aligned Compute and Communication Provisioning

What it does

A Coarse-Grained Reconfigurable Array (CGRA) is a type of programmable chip that can be rewired in software to accelerate different algorithms — think of it as a grid of small programmable math units connected by a software-configurable network, used in power-constrained edge devices like smartphones and wearables. The problem is that today's CGRAs waste roughly 44% of their power and area on communication routers that are oversized for the vast majority of what programs actually demand. Plaid redesigns the basic building block of a CGRA so that three math units share a single lightweight local router, and uses a compiler that automatically recognizes recurring three-node patterns (called 'motifs') in program data-flow graphs to map programs efficiently onto this structure — achieving 43% lower power and 46% smaller area than a standard high-performance CGRA while matching its speed.

The method, in detail

The idea. In a standard CGRA, every processing element (PE) — a small math unit — comes paired with a full crossbar router capable of sending data in any direction to any neighbor at peak congestion. In practice, most data communication is simple: one result feeds directly into the next operation, or two results merge into a third. The complex multi-hop routing capability is rarely needed yet consumes power constantly. Plaid's insight: if you look at a program's data-flow graph (a diagram where each node is a computation and each edge is a data dependency), nearly all connections fall into just three patterns among groups of three adjacent nodes: fan-out (one result feeds two others), fan-in (two results feed one), and unicast (a chain of three in sequence). These three shapes are called 'motifs,' borrowed from network theory.

The hardware. Plaid replaces the individual PE+router pairs with a Plaid Collective Unit (PCU). Each PCU contains three arithmetic units (16-bit ALUs supporting add, multiply, shift, and bitwise operations) connected to a single small local router. This local router handles all communication among the three ALUs inside the PCU — covering all possible three-node motif patterns — without needing the full crossbar. A second, global router connects each PCU to its neighbors across the chip, handling longer-distance communication between different PCUs. A 2x2 array of PCUs gives 16 functional units total, the same as a conventional 4x4 grid of single-unit PEs.

The compiler. The Plaid compiler takes annotated C loop code, converts it to a data-flow graph, and automatically identifies motif subgraphs using an iterative algorithm: it first greedily extracts motifs, then randomly breaks and re-assembles them to find a better-covering set. It then maps motifs to PCUs using a simulated-annealing scheduler (a search technique that sometimes accepts slightly worse placements to avoid getting stuck) with flexible schedule templates — allowing the three ALUs within a PCU to execute in any order, not just left-to-right, which prevents under-utilization.

The key design choice. Using three math units per router (rather than two or four) is mathematically justified: a two-node subgraph has only one possible internal connection pattern, offering no routing flexibility to exploit; larger groups appear less frequently in real programs and require more complex routing. Three is the smallest size where reconfigurable internal routing pays off.

The result. Implemented in Verilog (a hardware description language, essentially a blueprint for physical circuits) at 22nm and 100MHz, Plaid achieves 43% power reduction and 46% area savings versus a standard spatio-temporal CGRA, while maintaining the same average performance. Against an energy-efficient spatial CGRA, Plaid delivers 1.4x better performance with 48% less area.

micro-2025-081full textMICRO

A. Delegato: Locality-Aware Atomic Memory Operations on Chiplets

What it does

Modern processors are built from multiple chiplets — separate silicon dies glued together — which means memory and data can sit physically far from the core that needs them, adding expensive latency penalties. Atomic memory operations (read-modify-write instructions used for locks and shared counters, abbreviated AMOs) are particularly sensitive to this because every update requires exclusive ownership of a data block, causing blocks to bounce between chiplets. Delegato introduces two new ways to execute these atomic updates — forwarding the operation to whoever already holds the data, or moving ownership to the requester — and adds a lightweight tracking mechanism that lets the memory controller predict which strategy is best, achieving a 1.13x speedup over the state-of-the-art predictor and 1.07x over always centralising updates.

The method, in detail

The idea. When a processor core wants to atomically update shared memory (say, increment a counter), existing systems do one of two things: fetch the data block all the way to the requesting core (near AMO), or send the update to a fixed central directory controller (far/centralised AMO). On a multi-chiplet system, either approach can force data to cross the slow inter-chiplet link unnecessarily. The key insight is that a third option often works better: if core C9 already has the data block in its private cache (level-2 cache), just ask C9 to perform the update locally and report the result — no data needs to move across chiplets at all.

Two new transaction types. Delegated AMOs introduce a new snoop message called SnpAMO. The memory directory (which tracks who owns each block) sends this message to the current owner; the owner performs the arithmetic, sends back just the old value and a completion acknowledgement, and the block never leaves the owning core. Migrating AMOs behave like existing near AMOs — they transfer ownership to the requester — but use a distinct request type (AtomicLoad rather than ReadUnique) so the directory can distinguish an AMO from a plain store and track the requester's identity for future predictions.

Five static policies. The directory can be configured with different rules for when to use each type: always centralise; always migrate; centralise only when the block is already in the directory's own cache; delegate when exactly one private cache owns the block; or a chiplet-aware rule that checks whether the requester and owner are on the same chiplet to decide whether crossing the inter-chiplet link can be avoided.

Delegato — the dynamic predictor. No single static policy wins everywhere, so Delegato adds adaptive hardware. Each level-2 cache keeps a small Reuse Table tracking whether it has used a delegated block since the last delegation. When the directory sends an SnpAMO, the cache piggybacks a one-bit reuse flag on the reply. The directory's Predictor Table uses this feedback to move between three policies: Pinned Owner (keep migrating data to the same requester that keeps asking), Present Central (centralise in the directory), and the Chiplet-Aware policy. If a requester asks twice in a row, the block is migrated to it; if the owner later reports no reuse, the directory switches to centralisation to recover ownership.

The result. Evaluated on a simulated dual-chiplet 32-core system using 22 parallel benchmarks including graph analytics, lock-free data structures, and scientific kernels, Delegato achieves a geometric mean speedup of 1.80x over near AMOs — the common default — and 1.13x over DynAMO, the previous state-of-the-art predictor, while also reducing inter-chiplet data transfers.

TileLink: Generating Efficient Compute-Communication Overlapping Kernels using Tile-Centric Primitives

What it does

Training and running large language models on many GPUs requires each GPU to constantly send partial results to the others — a process that can consume 10–50% of total run time just sitting idle waiting for network transfers to finish. The standard fix is to run computation and communication simultaneously, but writing the fused GPU programs that do this requires thousands of lines of expert-level code. TileLink is a compiler that lets programmers express the same overlapped computation in about 200 lines of Python and then automatically generates the efficient low-level code.

The method, in detail

The problem. When a model is split across GPUs, operations like all-reduce (combining partial sums from every GPU), all-gather (assembling a complete tensor from shards on each GPU), and reduce-scatter (distributing partial results back out) must finish before the next computation can begin — unless the programmer manually interleaves the two, which is extraordinarily difficult and error-prone.

The idea. TileLink breaks the problem into tiles — small rectangular chunks of data — and lets computation tiles and communication tiles operate on different tiles at the same time, synchronized by lightweight signals rather than full barriers.

Frontend primitives. The programmer writes Python using a small set of named operations: producer_tile_notify/consumer_tile_wait (tell a downstream tile its input is ready, and wait for that signal), peer_tile_notify/peer_tile_wait (synchronize the same tile across two GPUs), and tile_push_data/tile_pull_data (actually move data between GPUs). These replace thousands of lines of hand-written assembly.

Backend compilation. The compiler translates these primitives into machine code in three steps: shape mapping (figure out which chunk of data each tile ID corresponds to), rank mapping (figure out which GPU holds or needs that chunk — computed either at compile time via simple formulas for regular patterns like tensor-parallel layers, or at run time via lookup tables for irregular patterns like mixture-of-expert routing where each token goes to a different specialist), and channel mapping (assign each data transfer to an NVLink or InfiniBand communication lane). The result is a single GPU program where compute threads and communication threads run inside the same kernel, sharing data through on-chip barriers with no CPU involvement.

Key design choice. Decoupling the tile size and ordering for communication from those for computation lets each side be tuned independently — for instance, communication tiles can be small (utilizing more GPU cores for sending) while compute tiles are large (better cache efficiency), something tightly coupled fusion cannot do.

The result. On 8 NVIDIA H800 GPUs, TileLink achieves 1.17x–20.76x speedup over the baseline of running computation and communication sequentially, and matches or exceeds the performance of hand-tuned state-of-the-art libraries like FLUX while requiring roughly 10x less code.

Balancing Pipeline Parallelism with Vocabulary Parallelism

What it does

When you train a large language model across many GPUs using a pipeline — where each GPU handles a different slice of the model's layers — the word-embedding layers (which convert words to numbers and back) end up crowded onto just one GPU, causing it to fall far behind the others and wasting time. This paper proposes Vocabulary Parallelism (VP), which spreads those embedding layers evenly across all pipeline GPUs so no single machine is overloaded, improving training speed by up to 51%.

The method, in detail

The idea. In pipeline parallelism, you split a model's layers across multiple GPUs like an assembly line: GPU 1 handles the first few layers, GPU 2 the next, and so on. The problem is that the word-embedding tables (which can be enormous for models with vocabularies of 64k–256k words) all land on the first and last GPUs. Those GPUs end up carrying much more work and memory than the middle GPUs, creating a traffic jam that stalls the whole pipeline.

How it works. VP partitions the vocabulary tables across all GPUs in the pipeline by slicing along the vocabulary dimension — each GPU owns a fraction of the word list. Now every GPU participates in embedding lookups and output-projection computations. The challenge is that converting raw scores into probabilities (the softmax step, where each score is exponentiated and divided by their sum) normally requires every GPU to know the global maximum and sum — meaning you need messages between all GPUs (called all-reduce operations) before you can finish.

The key design choice. The paper reorders and precomputes parts of the calculation so that the number of these cross-GPU synchronization points drops from 3 down to 1. Algorithm 1 collapses two of the three barriers using a trick called online softmax (computing the exponents locally with a local maximum, then correcting later). Algorithm 2 goes further: it moves the expensive matrix multiply for computing input gradients before the single remaining barrier, so the barrier only has to do lightweight arithmetic. This keeps the pipeline schedule almost unchanged — each GPU just gets one small extra 'vocabulary pass' inserted between its regular forward and backward passes.

The result. On up to 32 NVIDIA A100 GPUs, VP achieves 5–51% higher training throughput compared to the naive baseline. Unlike the competing 'interlaced' approach (which requires 1.5x more memory per stage and adds synchronization bubbles), VP adds only a small constant activation memory overhead. When combined with the V-Half schedule (a memory-balanced pipeline schedule), VP achieves perfectly balanced memory across all devices.

Radius: Range-based Gradient Sparsity for Large Foundation Model Pre-training

What it does

Training a large language model across dozens of GPUs requires each GPU to send its computed gradients (the update signals used to improve the model) to all other GPUs after every step — a communication step that can consume 40% of total training time at scale. Existing tricks to shrink that traffic either break modern optimizers or become slower than no compression at all as the number of GPUs grows. Radius fixes this by exploiting the observation that the positions of the largest, most important gradients barely change from step to step, allowing workers to skip 60% of gradient traffic with only a dense averaging operation — no index exchange needed — yielding a 19% end-to-end speedup with no loss in final model quality.

The method, in detail

The problem with existing sparsification. A natural idea is to keep only the top-k largest gradients and discard the rest. But two things go wrong. First, each GPU picks a different set of top-k positions, so you need to broadcast both values and their positions to everyone (an 'allgather' operation), and that broadcast grows linearly with the number of GPUs — eventually costing more than sending everything. Second, modern optimizers like AdamW (the standard for large model training) apply non-linear transformations to raw gradients before updating weights; dropping small raw gradients and accumulating them as error feedback does not undo the damage, so the model's training curve diverges.

The key observation. Radius plots which gradient positions rank in the top 1% over many consecutive training steps and finds that — after roughly 15–20% of training is complete — the same positions keep appearing in almost every step. The landscape of 'important gradient positions' is stable.

How it works. After the warm-up phase, Radius reuses the same position list for T=200 steps at a time. Because all workers ran the same dense averaging step at the last resample point, every worker has identical position lists. This means that in the 199 steps between resamples, each worker only needs to send the actual gradient values at those positions — no positions attached — and a standard 'allreduce' (a ring-based sum-and-average across all workers) suffices, with communication volume proportional to the compression ratio, not to the number of workers. At resample time, a dense allreduce is performed to refresh the position list.

Two correctness patches. Because AdamW's non-linear behavior means small raw gradients can produce large weight changes, Radius runs a 'gradient correction' pass before picking the top positions: it simulates exactly what AdamW would compute and picks positions based on that corrected signal. Non-selected gradients go into a local residual buffer and are added back in at the next resample step.

The result. At 40% compression (sending only 40% of gradient values) on 64 NVIDIA A100 GPUs training a 2-billion-parameter GPT model, Radius cuts per-step communication time by 90% and delivers a 19% end-to-end speedup with downstream task scores matching the full-gradient baseline. Scaling from 8 to 128 GPUs, Radius sustains 42.5% efficiency where the uncompressed baseline drops to 32.8%.

What it does

MeshSlice: Efficient 2D Tensor Parallelism for Distributed DNN Training

What it does

Training very large AI models requires splitting the model's enormous weight matrices across dozens or hundreds of chips and doing a massive collective operation — called an 'all-reduce' — where every chip adds up contributions from every other chip after computing its local portion. When only one dimension of splitting is used (one-dimensional parallelism), the all-reduce traffic grows linearly with the number of chips, becoming a severe bottleneck at scale. MeshSlice introduces a new algorithm for performing large matrix multiplications (the core arithmetic operation in AI training) split across a two-dimensional grid of chips, where instead of one big all-reduce at the end, smaller partial communication steps are interleaved with ongoing arithmetic, so the chips are computing and communicating simultaneously rather than taking turns. An automatic tuner searches over how to shape the chip grid, how many slices to split the work into, and how to arrange the dataflow, to find the fastest configuration for each layer of the model and each cluster size.
