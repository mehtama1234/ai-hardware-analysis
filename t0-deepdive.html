Everything else that keeps it running

scheduling, data, and infrastructure

the problemThe problem, in depth

Start with the one fact that governs everything in this book: a computer is fast at doing arithmetic and slow at fetching the numbers it needs to do arithmetic on. The part that does the math sits in one place. The numbers it works on live somewhere else — in memory chips, on disk, or on another machine across a network cable. Every calculation is really two steps: go get the numbers, then combine them. The 'combine them' step is nearly free. The 'go get them' step is where the time, the electricity, and the waiting all pile up. If you remember nothing else, remember this: modern machines are not limited by how much thinking they can do; they are limited by how much moving-around-of-data they have to do to keep the thinking fed.

Most chapters of this book are about a single, sharp problem: how to make one chip multiply numbers faster, or how to store a model's memory more cheaply. This chapter is the opposite. It is the drawer of important things that don't fit in the other drawers — and what is striking is that when you lay them all out, they turn out to be different faces of that same one root problem. A tool that squeezes scientific data down to a third of its size is fighting data movement: less to move means less waiting. A system that trains a giant model across a dozen buildings on a slow internet link is fighting data movement: it can't afford to shout every number to every building on every step. A trick that renders a video-game frame at full detail only where your eye happens to be looking is fighting data movement: why compute and shuttle around pixels no one will see? A method that stops a training run from keeping three private copies of every number is fighting data movement: fewer copies means they fit in the fast local memory instead of spilling out to the slow distant memory.

To feel why this is hard, picture the machine physically. The tiny part that does the arithmetic — people often call it the 'processor' or, when it's the flexible workhorse chip built for graphics and now used for everything, the GPU — can do a stunning number of multiplications per second. But the numbers it multiplies have to arrive from memory over wires. Those wires have a fixed width: only so many numbers can flow down them per second, no matter how fast the arithmetic unit is. It's like having a chef who can chop vegetables at superhuman speed, standing at the end of a single narrow hallway down which every vegetable must be carried by hand. The chef's knife-speed stopped mattering long ago. The hallway is the bottleneck. Making the knife faster — which is what most of hardware research has historically done — does nothing if the hallway is already jammed.

So the recurring move in this chapter is: don't widen the hallway, carry less through it. Every clever idea here is, at bottom, a way to send fewer numbers, store fewer copies, compute only the numbers that matter, or agree on the numbers less often. Compression carries less by packing numbers tightly. Foveated rendering computes less by only working where you're looking. Federated training communicates less by letting each site work alone for a long stretch before comparing notes. Memory-frugal training stores less by refusing to keep redundant copies. They look unrelated — a compression library, a video-game trick, a training system, an optimizer — but they are all the same insight applied in different rooms of the house.

There is a second, quieter thread running through this drawer, and it is just as important: how do we even know if any of this is working? When you build a faster machine and test it on ten different jobs, you get ten different speedup numbers. To brag about 'the' speedup, you have to average them — and it turns out the average that nearly everyone in the field has used for decades is the wrong average, in the plain mathematical sense that it can rank a worse machine above a better one. Alongside that is a deeper accounting question: a chip's true cost isn't just the electricity it burns while running, but the pollution baked into manufacturing it in the first place — the mining, the fabrication, the shipping. If you optimize only for the running cost, you can 'win' by building a bigger, dirtier chip that was expensive to make. So part of this chapter is about honest measuring: getting the arithmetic of comparison and the arithmetic of environmental cost right, so that all the cleverness above actually points in the direction we think it does.

What ties the whole drawer together, then, is a shift in what counts as progress. For a long time 'better' meant 'the knife is faster.' This chapter is a collection of arguments that better usually means 'less has to travel down the hallway' — and a couple of hard reminders that you can only claim you've made things better if you're measuring the right thing in the first place.

the approachesHow the field actually tackles it

1.Squeeze the data so there's less to carry (compression)

The most direct way to move less data is to make the data smaller before it travels. Scientific instruments and simulations produce staggering rivers of numbers — more than storage disks and network cables can keep up with. So before you write a number to disk or send it across a machine, you compress it: you find the redundancy and repetition inside it and encode the same information in fewer bits. When it arrives, you unpack it back to the exact original. This is 'lossless' compression — nothing is thrown away, unlike the 'lossy' shrinking that a JPEG photo uses.

The catch is a genuine tension. You can compress hard — spend a lot of effort and shrink the data a lot — or you can compress fast, but historically not both. A tool that shrinks beautifully but slowly is useless if the data is flying by faster than it can pack. asplos-2025-017 attacks exactly this by designing compressors built specifically for the way the machine stores fractional numbers (the 'floating-point' format, which splits a number into a scale part and a precision part). Because these two parts have very different statistical patterns, a compressor that understands their internal structure can find redundancy that a generic one misses.

The mechanism worth naming is that the same compressed bundle can be produced and unpacked on either kind of chip — the flexible graphics-style chip (GPU) or the ordinary general-purpose chip (CPU) — and it runs at hundreds of gigabytes per second, roughly the pace at which the data is generated. The trade-off it accepts is complexity: these are specialized algorithms tuned to the number format and to the parallel, do-many-things-at-once nature of the hardware, not a one-size-fits-all zip tool. But the payoff is squarely in the root problem: a number that's been packed to a third of its size occupies a third of the hallway.

2.Only compute the numbers that actually matter (do-less accelerators)

A different way to move less data is to never create most of it in the first place. If you can prove that a chunk of work will not affect the final answer a human cares about, you can simply skip it — no computing it, no storing it, no shuttling it around. The art is spotting which work is safe to skip, and building hardware that skips it cleanly.

The clearest example is how your eye works. Your vision is sharp only in a tiny central spot; everything else is a blur you never consciously notice. So in a virtual-reality headset, rendering every pixel at full crispness is enormous waste — the person can only see detail in the small patch where their eye is aimed. isca-2025-108 tracks where the eye is pointed and renders full detail only there, dropping the periphery to low quality. Crucially, it pairs a fast eye-tracking method with a small dedicated helper chip (an accelerator built for exactly this one job) so that figuring out where you're looking doesn't itself become the new bottleneck. The result is the same-looking image for a fraction of the pixels computed and moved.

The same 'skip the pointless work' logic shows up in a totally different setting: searching huge streams of text for patterns (the workhorse behind log analysis and databases). micro-2025-033 compiles pattern-searches into a form where much of the intermediate bookkeeping turns out to be all-zeros — nothing there. Instead of dutifully processing those empty stretches, it detects and skips them, and it also reorganizes the work so each block of data is read from memory once and fully chewed on before the next block is loaded, rather than being re-fetched over and over. Both papers embody the same trade-off: they give up generality (each is tuned to one kind of task) to win big on the thing that costs the most — fetching and processing data you didn't need.

3.Agree less often when training across slow links (federated and pipelined training)

Training a large model normally happens inside a single building packed with chips wired together by very fat, very short cables. The reason it has to be one building is subtle: the standard method forces every chip to share its latest updates with every other chip after every single step. That constant chatter is fine over the fat in-building cables, but it collapses the moment the chips are far apart — in different cities, or across the ordinary internet — because those links are hundreds of times narrower. The training grinds to a halt not because the math is hard but because the machines spend all their time talking instead of computing. Root problem again: the hallway between buildings is too narrow for constant chatter.

The fix is to talk far less often. mlsys-2025-036 lets each site go off and work on its own for many steps before it compares notes with the others — and then, instead of exchanging every detail, the sites just average their progress. This 'work alone, then average' rhythm cuts the amount of communication by hundreds of times. The surprising bonus is that the averaging doesn't just tolerate the slow link — it can actually make the final model slightly better, because the mild disagreement between sites acts like healthy noise that keeps the model from over-fixating. That's the trade it accepts: each site's copy of the model drifts a little between check-ins, and the method leans into that drift rather than fighting it.

mlsys-2025-045 tackles a related version of the same squeeze. When a model is too big to fit on one chip, you cut it into stages, like an assembly line, with each stage on a different chip. The naive assembly line leaves chips standing idle waiting for the previous stage, and the popular software for this could only express the wasteful schedules. This work lets engineers describe smarter schedules — ones that keep more chips busy and use less memory — with a light annotation in their code, and it automatically figures out who has to send what to whom without deadlocking. Both papers share one belief: the arithmetic units are not the scarce resource; the wires between them are, so the whole design should revolve around using those wires sparingly.

4.Stop keeping redundant copies of numbers (memory-frugal training)

Here the data movement is hiding in plain sight. Training a model isn't just storing the model's numbers; the training method keeps several private extra copies of housekeeping numbers alongside every single one of the model's numbers — running averages it uses to decide how big each adjustment should be. The most popular method keeps two such extras per number, which roughly triples the memory the training needs. That extra memory is not a mere inconvenience: when the numbers don't fit in a chip's fast local memory, they spill out to slower, more distant memory, and now you're back to paying the hallway tax on every step.

mlsys-2025-059 asks whether all that bookkeeping is really necessary. Its insight is that the fine-grained, per-number tuning the standard method does is overkill — you can get almost the same benefit by tuning at a coarser grain, one knob per group of numbers instead of one per number. And you can estimate those coarse knobs cheaply by projecting the numbers down into a much smaller scratch space, doing the bookkeeping there, and reading the answer back out. The result is roughly the memory of the bare-bones method that keeps no extras, with the quality of the fancy method — shrinking the housekeeping from tens of gigabytes down to nearly nothing, which lets the whole job fit in fast memory and run with bigger batches.

The trade-off is a small approximation: the coarse knobs aren't literally identical to the fine ones, and the projection introduces a bit of randomness. The paper's real contribution is showing, with a mathematical guarantee, that this random shrinking preserves the important information closely enough that the training doesn't suffer. It's the same theme as compression — find the redundancy and refuse to pay for it — applied to the invisible scratch numbers of training rather than to the data itself.

5.Throw away the code you never run (software debloat)

Not all the wasted data is numbers you're computing on — some of it is the program itself. The software libraries that everyone uses to train and run models are enormous, and a huge fraction of the code inside them is machinery for hardware you don't have and features you never touch. That dead weight isn't harmless: it takes up memory, slows down loading, and clutters the chip's caches, which are the small fast holding-areas nearest the arithmetic unit. Clutter there means the useful code and data get pushed out to slower memory more often — once again, the hallway tax.

mlsys-2025-054 measures how bad this is and then removes it. The clever part is figuring out which code is actually dead. It watches a real run and notes exactly which pieces of chip-code get called, the way you might tag every tool a mechanic actually reaches for during a job. Everything never reached is unused for that workload and gets cut out of the library. Across the popular frameworks it finds that most of the bloat lives in the graphics-chip code — the code destined for the flexible workhorse chip — because those libraries ship versions for many chip generations at once, and any given machine uses only one.

The trade-off is that a debloated library is specialized to the workloads you measured: it's leaner but less universal, so you'd re-trim it if your job changed. But the payoff is concrete — large cuts in code size and in peak memory use, and meaningfully faster execution — all from carrying less, this time less program rather than less data. It's the same root instinct as everything above, aimed at a target most people never think to look at.

6.Measure the right thing before you claim you improved it (honest evaluation)

None of the cleverness above means anything if you can't tell whether it worked — and this is where the drawer holds a genuinely uncomfortable finding. When you test a new machine on many different jobs, each job gives its own speedup number, and to summarize you must average them. For decades the field has averaged these speedups using a particular formula (the 'geometric mean') that feels reasonable but is, in a precise mathematical sense, the wrong tool for ratios like speedups. hpca-2025-025 shows it can literally rank a slower machine above a faster one, and derives from scratch the average you should use instead — one that correctly answers 'across this whole mix of jobs, how much faster did we really get?' This isn't nitpicking: real architectural conclusions have been drawn from the wrong average.

The second measurement problem is about cost, honestly counted. It's easy to judge a chip by the electricity it burns while running. But a chip also carries a hidden environmental debt from being built at all — the mining, the manufacturing, the shipping — spent before it computes a single thing. If you optimize only for running-cost, you can appear to win by building a bigger, dirtier chip whose manufacturing pollution you conveniently ignored. hpca-2025-026 rolls both costs — the pollution from running and the pollution from making — together with performance into a single score, so that a design search can't cheat by hiding one kind of cost. In one case it finds that stacking the memory directly on top of the logic (so data travels a shorter distance) improves this combined score several-fold, which is fitting: even the environmental accounting rewards shortening the hallway.

Both papers trade excitement for rigor. They don't make anything faster or greener themselves; they fix the ruler. But a bent ruler is worse than no ruler, because it makes you confident in the wrong direction — so getting the measurement right is what lets every other idea in this chapter be trusted.

where it standsWhat's solved, what's open

The healthy news is that the field has largely internalized the core lesson: across compression, do-less accelerators, communication-light training, memory-frugal optimizers, and debloating, the winning moves are all about carrying less down the narrow hallway between arithmetic and memory, and each of these papers shows a solid, measured win in its own corner. The methodology work is also a real step forward — there's now a mathematically correct way to average speedups and a way to price in a chip's manufacturing pollution, not just its running cost.

What's still open is that these wins are mostly one-off and specialized. The compressor is tuned to one number format, the rendering trick to eyeballs, the debloater to the specific jobs you measured; change the workload and you re-do the work. There is no general theory that tells you, for a new task, how much data movement is truly avoidable or which of these tactics to reach for. The honest gaps are that federated and pipelined training still lean on assumptions about network shape and model size that don't always hold, memory-frugal training rests on approximations whose limits at the very largest scales aren't fully mapped, and the carbon accounting depends on pollution estimates that are themselves uncertain. And the deepest gap is cultural: the better measuring tools exist, but the field has not yet fully switched to using them — which means some of the speedup claims we currently trust may not survive being re-measured with the right ruler.

Photon: Federated LLM Pre-Training

What it does

Training a large language model (LLM) normally requires all GPUs to be in the same data center, connected by extremely fast links, because every training step requires every GPU to share its gradient updates with every other GPU — a process that saturates slower internet connections. Photon is a system that trains LLMs across GPUs in different cities and organizations over ordinary internet links by having each participant do many training steps locally before synchronizing, reducing communication by 64 to 512 times. Counterintuitively, models trained this way reach lower prediction error (better perplexity) than models trained the conventional centralized way, while finishing in 35% less wall-clock time.

The method, in detail

The idea. Standard distributed training uses a technique called data-parallel training: every GPU trains on a different slice of the data and then, after every single gradient step, broadcasts its updates to all other GPUs so everyone stays perfectly in sync. This requires network links running at 100-400 Gbps. Cross-datacenter or home internet links run at 1-40 Gbps, 10-100x slower, making this approach impractical at scale.

Photon's core algorithm. Photon uses federated learning with LocalSGD: each participant (called a client) receives the current global model, trains locally for many steps — typically 64 to 512 steps — using its own private data and its own optimizer (AdamW), and only then sends its updated model weights back to a central aggregator. The aggregator averages all the received updates and applies them to the global model. This reduces total communication from one sync per step to one sync per round of local training, cutting the data transmitted by 64-512x.

The key design choice: small batches plus high learning rates. Normally, small local batch sizes combined with high learning rates cause training to diverge (the model oscillates and never settles). But federated averaging acts as an implicit regularizer — it averages out the noise from many clients' independent trajectories, landing the model in a wide, flat region of the loss landscape that generalizes better to new data. Photon exploits this property deliberately: it sets local batch sizes to whatever fits the client's GPU memory (as small as 32 samples) and uses a high learning rate with a long cosine decay schedule. This combination produces models that converge twice as fast as the prior state-of-the-art federated method (DiLoCo) and reach lower final error than centralized training.

Adaptive local parallelism. Within a client that has multiple GPUs or multiple servers, Photon automatically selects whether to run standard distributed data-parallel training (if the intra-client network is fast enough) or apply another level of federated local training (if it is not). The aggregator never sees the internal topology of any client.

The result. Photon trained decoder-only LLMs from scratch up to 7 billion parameters using 4-16 clients spread across five global regions (England, Utah, Texas, Quebec, Maharashtra). The 7B model reached a perplexity 16.9% lower than its centralized baseline and finished in 95.6 hours versus 147.9 hours for the centralized approach, despite communication overhead being reduced to under 0.1% of total training time.

mlsys-2025-045full textMLSYS

Scaling Deep Learning Training with MPMD Pipeline Parallelism

What it does

Training a very large AI model requires splitting the work across thousands of GPUs, including a technique called pipeline parallelism where different groups of GPUs process different slices of the model in overlapping shifts — like an assembly line. The standard Google JAX framework could only do a crude version of this; JaxPP extends it to support flexible assembly-line schedules that cut wasted idle time, using 51% less time per training step than JAX's built-in approach and matching specialist hand-tuned systems like NeMo within 5%.

The method, in detail

The idea. Pipeline parallelism divides a neural network into sections (stages) and assigns each section to a group of GPUs (an actor). When one actor finishes processing a small chunk of data (a microbatch) it hands the intermediate results to the next actor while it starts processing the next chunk, keeping all actors busy like workers on an assembly line. The order in which forward and backward passes are interleaved — the schedule — determines how much memory is needed and how much time is wasted in 'bubbles' where actors sit idle. Better schedules like '1F1B' (one forward pass, one backward pass, alternate) dramatically reduce both. JAX's existing system could only express the simple GPipe schedule, which stores activations for every microbatch and needs the most memory.

How it works. Users mark stage boundaries in ordinary Python/JAX code with a single function call (pipeline_yield). JaxPP traces the program into a dependency graph, splits it into tasks, and assigns tasks to actors according to whichever schedule the user requests (1F1B, Interleaved 1F1B, etc.). A single driver process coordinates everything: it figures out which actors need to send data to which other actors, inserts the necessary send/receive operations in the correct order (using topological sort to avoid deadlocks), and manages when intermediate buffers can be deleted to free memory. All task dispatches for an entire training step are batched into one remote call per actor, eliminating round-trip overhead.

The key design choice. Running send and receive operations asynchronously, scheduled immediately after their producing task completes, lets communication overlap with computation — so actors do not stall waiting for data.

A subtle fix. When model weights are shared across stages (as in tied word embeddings), a naive implementation would send large gradient updates across the network many times. JaxPP reorders the accumulation math so each actor independently sums its own partial gradients and combines at the end, cutting cross-network traffic to a single transfer.

The result. Training GPT-3 175B on 128 H100 GPUs: 478 TFLOPS per device with JaxPP versus 316 for JAX's SPMD pipeline (51% faster) and 412 for JAX's FSDP (data-parallel sharding). Weak scaling efficiency to 1024 GPUs: 98.3%.

mlsys-2025-054full textMLSYS

The Hidden Bloat in Machine Learning Systems

What it does

AI frameworks like PyTorch and TensorFlow ship as giant software packages — shared libraries — that contain far more code than any single workload ever uses. This wasted code (called bloat) consumes extra memory, slows startup, and increases security risk. Negativa-ML is a tool that identifies which pieces of code are actually used for a given workload, then surgically removes everything else, shrinking the libraries by up to 55% without changing what the program does.

The method, in detail

The idea. A shared library is a file of compiled code that many programs can load and reuse. ML frameworks pack an enormous amount of code into these files — both ordinary CPU code and GPU code (programs that run on the GPU's many parallel processors, called kernels). Prior tools only knew how to trim CPU code; no one had a method to find and remove unused GPU code, which turns out to make up 68-92% of the biggest library files.

How it works. Negativa-ML has two main components.

Kernel Detector: Every time a GPU program (kernel) is about to be launched, the CPU must first call a specific system function called cuModuleGetFunction, passing the kernel's name. Negativa-ML hooks into this function — it intercepts the call and records the name. Crucially, this function is only called once per kernel regardless of how many times that kernel runs, so the overhead is low (41% overhead vs. 126% for standard profiling tools like Nsight). The result is a list of every GPU kernel the workload actually used.

Kernel Locator: GPU code inside a shared library is organized in a nested structure: regions contain elements, and each element contains a self-contained binary blob called a cubin (CUDA binary) that holds one or more kernels. The tool uses Nvidia's cuobjdump utility to extract and index every cubin, matching them to the used kernel names. It also notes which GPU architecture (e.g., Ampere, Turing) each element targets — elements compiled for the wrong architecture can never run and are always removable. Unused elements are zeroed out in the file while memory addresses are remapped so the remaining code still loads correctly.

The key design choice. Rather than trying to locate individual kernel functions byte-by-byte inside the binary (fragile and version-specific), Negativa-ML works at the coarser cubin granularity. If any used kernel is in a cubin, the whole cubin is kept — this also automatically preserves kernels that are called from other GPU kernels and would otherwise be invisible to CPU-side monitoring.

The result. Across 4 frameworks, 10 workloads, and 300+ libraries: up to 75% of GPU code and 72% of CPU code removed; total library file size shrinks up to 55%; peak CPU memory drops up to 74.6%, GPU memory up to 69.6%, and execution time up to 44.6%. The biggest single cause of GPU bloat: libraries compiled to support 6 different GPU chip generations at once, even though the deployed machine uses only one.

APOLLO: SGD-like Memory, AdamW-level Performance

What it does

Training a large language model requires an optimizer — software that adjusts the model's weights after each batch of examples. The most popular optimizer (AdamW) must remember two running statistics for every single weight, which roughly triples the memory needed compared to just storing the weights themselves: for a 7-billion-parameter model that means 28 GB of extra memory just for these statistics. APOLLO is a replacement optimizer that achieves the same quality of training while keeping its statistics in a tiny compressed space, cutting that overhead to nearly zero and freeing enough memory to use bigger batches and train 3x faster.

The method, in detail

The idea. AdamW works by dividing each weight's gradient by a personalized scale factor — one factor per number in the model. APOLLO's key observation is that you don't need a unique scale factor for every number: one factor per column of a weight matrix (called 'channel-wise' scaling) works just as well or better, because the fine-grained per-number adjustments are mostly noise for large models.

How it works. Instead of tracking full statistics for all weights, APOLLO projects each gradient matrix down into a much smaller 'low-rank' space using a random mixing matrix (think of it as squishing a large spreadsheet down to just a few rows). It then runs AdamW's update math on that tiny projection, extracts a scale factor per column, and applies that scale back to the full-size gradient to update the actual weights. The random mixing matrix costs no memory to store — you just save a random seed and regenerate it every 200 steps.

The key design choice. Using random projection instead of the expensive SVD decomposition (which finds the mathematically best low-rank approximation) that competing methods like GaLore require. The paper proves mathematically that random projection preserves the relative sizes of the scale factors up to a known ratio, which can be corrected with a simple scalar. This makes APOLLO both faster and cheaper than GaLore.

APOLLO-Mini pushes further: it uses a single scale factor for the entire weight matrix (rank-1 space), bringing optimizer memory to essentially zero — the same cost as simple gradient-descent with no statistics at all.

The result. On LLaMA-7B training across 8 GPUs, APOLLO uses 1.6 GB of optimizer state versus 28 GB for AdamW, enables 4x larger batches, and achieves 3x higher throughput — while producing a lower perplexity (a measure of model quality; lower is better). APOLLO-Mini fits LLaMA-13B pretraining on a single 80 GB GPU without any special distributed setup, something AdamW cannot do.
