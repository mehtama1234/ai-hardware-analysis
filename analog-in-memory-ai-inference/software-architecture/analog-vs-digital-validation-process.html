Analog IMC Vs Established Digital Validation Process

This page compares the analog in-memory computing proof process against the way mature digital semiconductor companies usually validate chips, software stacks, and customer workloads.

Main question: where is our analog proof flow the same as an established digital process, and where does analog need extra evidence?

Plain rule:

Digital validation asks whether the hardware executes the workload correctly and efficiently. Analog IMC validation asks a harder question: can the workload survive imperfect physical math and still beat the digital baseline on the metric that matters?

Current Validation Boundary

The current project has a digital-style proof slice: generated cases feed RTL checks, Yosys synthesis lowers controller logic, and OpenLane readiness checks show the selected controller blocks can enter an educational physical-design flow. This is the right kind of early digital control evidence.

The analog side still needs extra validation. It must prove that physical approximation does not break the task, that converters and calibration are counted, that board and meter evidence exist for measured claims, and that analog macro integration is physically verified. That is why C2 and C3 remain needs review and production readiness remains blocked.

The Shared Shape

Both digital and analog hardware companies are trying to avoid the same failure: building silicon that looks impressive in a demo but does not help a real customer workload. The process starts with customer requirements, moves through modeling and compiler work, reaches board measurements, and ends with a controlled claim.

Same Starting Point

A customer has a workload, a model, a task, power limits, latency limits, accuracy limits, and safety or reliability needs.

Same Middle Work

The engineering team profiles the model, maps it to hardware, tests software support, simulates performance, and prepares a board or lab run.

Same Final Need

The company must show evidence: what ran, where it ran, how fast, how much energy, what accuracy, what failed, and what claim is allowed.

The difference is the kind of uncertainty. A mature digital accelerator is mostly limited by throughput, memory movement, compiler support, power, and software maturity. An analog IMC chip has all of those problems plus physical math uncertainty: drift, noise, limited precision, imperfect writes, tile mismatch, voltage variation, temperature variation, and calibration.

The Core Comparison

QuestionEstablished Digital ProcessAnalog IMC Process
What is the main proof target? Prove that the chip executes the workload correctly, quickly, and efficiently using its digital compute units and memory system. Prove that the workload still works after analog physical imperfections are included, and that the full hybrid system beats a digital baseline.
What does the compiler mostly decide? Which operators run on CPU, GPU, NPU, DSP, or fixed-function units; how tensors move through memory; which kernels are used. Which model parts can run on analog arrays, which must stay digital, how weights are tiled, how bit slicing works, how ADC/DAC sharing is scheduled, and which calibration profile is required.
What does simulation mean? Performance, power, memory, RTL, system timing, and sometimes quantization accuracy. All of that, plus analog device behavior: write error, read noise, drift, IR drop, converter precision, tile variation, voltage, temperature, and calibration.
What can erase the advantage? Memory bandwidth, software overhead, bad kernels, poor utilization, thermal throttling, or weak customer fit. All digital risks, plus converter overhead, calibration cost, write cost, drift correction, limited precision, analog/digital handoff cost, and task accuracy loss.
What is the safest claim style? "This workload runs on this chip under these measured settings." "These selected parts of this workload run on analog under these assumptions or measurements, the rest runs digitally, and this is the proof level."

Side-By-Side Step Map

This is the detailed connection between our analog proof ladder and an established digital company process.

Analog Step 1

Customer Workload Intake

Collect the model, task, sensor path, latency target, power target, update behavior, environment, and safety boundary.

Why analog asks more: analog fit depends heavily on whether weights are stable, the task tolerates small numerical error, and the system benefits from compute near sensors.

Digital Equivalent

Product Requirements And Workload Capture

A digital company collects customer workloads, target benchmarks, accuracy needs, latency targets, memory footprint, software stack, and deployment environment.

Common artifacts: product requirements document, benchmark list, customer constraints, software support matrix.

Main Difference

Analog Must Classify Update And Sensor Risk Early

For digital chips, frequent model updates are usually a software/storage problem. For analog memory, updates are a physical write problem involving write energy, write time, endurance, retention, and recalibration.

extra analog gate
Analog Step 2

Model And Operator Analysis

Break the model into parts: matrix-heavy layers, dynamic attention, normalization, nonlinear functions, action heads, preprocessing, and safety/control logic.

What we are looking for: repeated matrix work with stable weights. That is where analog arrays may help.

Digital Equivalent

Model Profiling And Operator Coverage

A digital accelerator team checks which operations are supported by kernels, which fall back to CPU, which tensors dominate runtime, and where memory bandwidth is spent.

Common artifacts: profiler report, operator support list, unsupported-op report, kernel coverage table.

Main Difference

Analog Must Separate Static From Dynamic Work

A digital accelerator can often run many operator types if kernels exist. Analog arrays are best at stable vector-matrix work. Dynamic attention, Softmax, LayerNorm, routing, and safety usually need digital support.

hybrid split required
Analog Step 3

Analog Non-Ideality Simulation

Simulate lower precision, noise, drift, imperfect weight writing, ADC/DAC limits, voltage and temperature variation, tile differences, and accumulation error.

Goal: find out whether the task result survives realistic analog behavior before a board run exists.

Digital Equivalent

Quantization And Numerical Accuracy Testing

Digital teams test INT8, FP16, BF16, sparsity, kernel approximations, and numerical differences between training and deployment.

Common artifacts: quantization report, accuracy delta, calibration dataset, precision tradeoff report.

Main Difference

Analog Simulation Has To Include Physics

Digital quantization error is usually stable and repeatable. Analog error can change with time, temperature, voltage, device variation, tile location, and write history.

physics model needed
Analog Step 4

Physical Mapping, Tiling, And Bit Slicing

Split large matrices across physical arrays. If one logical weight needs more precision than one analog cell can store, map it across multiple cells and combine partial results.

Goal: prove that the model can fit the actual chip shape, not an imaginary unlimited array.

Digital Equivalent

Kernel Selection, Tiling, And Memory Planning

Digital teams tile tensors into cache, SRAM, tensor cores, vector units, or systolic arrays. They schedule memory movement and choose kernels.

Common artifacts: compiler schedule, memory plan, kernel trace, utilization report.

Main Difference

Analog Tiling Changes Accuracy, Not Just Speed

In digital systems, tiling mostly affects performance. In analog systems, tile size, wire resistance, bit slicing, ADC sharing, and partial accumulation can also change the numerical result.

fit plus accuracy
Analog Step 5

Compiler Target And Runtime Package

Turn model sections into chip commands: placement, weight-write plan, converter schedule, digital fallback, calibration metadata, and runtime instructions.

Goal: move from "this could map to analog" to "this package could actually run on our chip."

Digital Equivalent

Compiler Lowering To ISA, Drivers, And Runtime

Digital companies lower model graphs to device-specific kernels, instruction streams, driver calls, memory commands, and runtime graphs.

Common artifacts: compiled binary, runtime graph, driver trace, device command log.

Main Difference

Analog Compiler Must Carry Calibration And Write Rules

The analog compiler cannot only schedule math. It must also know how weights are physically written, corrected, checked, and recovered when cells or tiles behave badly.

chip-specific lowering
Analog Step 6

System Timing Simulation

Measure or estimate the full path: sensor capture, preprocessing, host transfer, DAC, analog compute, ADC, digital coprocessor, memory movement, safety check, and output.

Goal: prevent a fast analog core from being described as a fast product if the full loop is slow.

Digital Equivalent

SoC Performance Modeling

Digital teams model CPU/NPU dispatch, DMA, cache behavior, DRAM bandwidth, interconnect traffic, interrupts, OS overhead, and accelerator utilization.

Common artifacts: cycle model, system trace, bandwidth report, latency histogram.

Main Difference

Analog Adds Converter And Boundary Costs

The analog/digital boundary is expensive if crossed too often. ADCs, DACs, buffering, and digital accumulation can dominate the workload even when the array itself is efficient.

full-loop timing
Analog Step 7

Hardware-Aware Training Or Adaptation

Train or fine-tune the model while simulating analog errors so the model learns weights that are less fragile when deployed on imperfect hardware.

Goal: avoid training a perfect digital model that collapses when physical noise is introduced.

Digital Equivalent

Quantization-Aware Training And Deployment Tuning

Digital teams use quantization-aware training, distillation, pruning, sparsity, and kernel-specific tuning to preserve accuracy on deployment hardware.

Common artifacts: QAT run, tuned checkpoint, distillation report, deployment accuracy report.

Main Difference

Analog Training Must Match Measured Device Behavior

Hardware-aware training is useful only if the simulated noise resembles the real device. Otherwise the model becomes robust to the wrong problem.

calibrated HAT
Analog Step 8

Board Bring-Up And Runtime Proof

Load the package on a board or prototype setup, program weights, start inference, collect runtime logs, and record failures.

Goal: prove the workload moved beyond a simulator into a physical setup.

Digital Equivalent

Silicon Bring-Up And Driver Validation

Digital teams boot the chip, validate firmware, load drivers, run diagnostics, execute known workloads, and collect traces.

Common artifacts: bring-up log, firmware version, driver log, benchmark run, board configuration.

Main Difference

Analog Bring-Up Includes Programming And Calibration

The board must show not only that code ran, but that weights were written, verified, calibrated, corrected, and used under known voltage and temperature conditions.

measured board gate
Analog Step 9

Power And Thermal Measurement

Measure energy per inference, peak power, duty cycle, temperature, cooling assumptions, and whether heat changes accuracy over time.

Goal: prove the full system saves energy or heat, not only that the analog array is efficient in isolation.

Digital Equivalent

Power Lab And Thermal Characterization

Digital companies measure power rails, thermal throttling, peak workloads, sustained workloads, package temperature, and cooling needs.

Common artifacts: power trace, thermal trace, workload configuration, measurement setup, sustained-run report.

Main Difference

Analog Heat Can Change The Computation

For digital chips, heat mostly affects speed, leakage, and reliability. For analog IMC, heat can also change stored conductance, drift, noise, and calibration validity.

power plus accuracy
Analog Step 10

Task Accuracy And Real Metric

Run the workload against the actual task metric: slip detection, obstacle detection per watt, fault detection false alarms, missed faults, action success, or another customer-defined score.

Goal: prove that lower power or faster math still matters because the task result remains useful.

Digital Equivalent

Application Benchmark And Customer Acceptance

Digital teams run application benchmarks, customer datasets, MLPerf-style workloads, regression suites, and acceptance tests.

Common artifacts: benchmark report, accuracy score, latency score, baseline comparison, customer acceptance result.

Main Difference

Analog Must Tie Accuracy To Physical Conditions

The task score should record calibration, temperature, voltage, chip state, update history, and simulator profile. Otherwise the result may not repeat.

metric under conditions
Analog Step 11

Weight Update, Retention, And Rollback

Measure whether weights can be rewritten, how long writing takes, how much energy writing uses, whether cells wear out, whether values stay stable, and whether rollback works.

Goal: decide whether the product is fixed-weight, rare-update, adapter-update, or not ready for frequent learning.

Digital Equivalent

Firmware, Model Update, And OTA Validation

Digital teams test software updates, model replacement, rollback, secure boot, version control, failure recovery, and update timing.

Common artifacts: update log, rollback log, version manifest, security check, post-update regression report.

Main Difference

Analog Update Is A Physical Operation

Changing a digital model file is mostly data movement. Changing analog weights means physically programming cells, verifying them, possibly recalibrating, and consuming device endurance.

update class decides product fit
Analog Step 12

Safety And Fallback Proof

Show who owns unsafe-action blocking, watchdogs, shutdown, digital override, host fallback, and safe behavior when analog confidence is low.

Goal: prevent learned analog inference from being the only safety layer in a physical system.

Digital Equivalent

Functional Safety And Fault Handling

Digital teams use watchdogs, safety islands, secure firmware, fault detection, reset handling, redundant checks, and safety standards depending on the market.

Common artifacts: safety case, fault injection result, watchdog trace, reset behavior, fallback mode report.

Main Difference

Analog Output Needs Trust Boundaries

If analog values drift or confidence drops, the system needs a digital lane or companion controller that can block, scale down, or override actions.

independent safety lane
Analog Step 13

Evidence Matrix And Claim Control

Map every claim to proof: simulator output, compiler map, board trace, power trace, thermal trace, task result, update result, calibration result, and failed runs.

Goal: stop weak evidence from unlocking strong sales, investor, or customer claims.

Digital Equivalent

Validation Report And Release Criteria

Mature companies use validation matrices, sign-off checklists, quality gates, benchmark reports, errata, and release notes.

Common artifacts: validation report, release checklist, known-issues list, benchmark table, customer qualification package.

Main Difference

Analog Needs More Proof-Level Separation

A simulator result, calibrated simulator result, board runtime result, and long-run reliability result must not be treated as the same kind of evidence.

claim boundaries
Analog Step 14

Final Fit Answer And Next Work

Return a direct answer: fit, partial fit, not fit, or unknown. Explain what proof exists, what breaks, what is safe to say, what is blocked, and what must be built or measured next.

Goal: make the next engineering, customer, partner, or investor decision concrete.

Digital Equivalent

Go/No-Go, Customer Pilot, Or Product Release Decision

Digital companies decide whether to continue development, ship a software update, enter a customer pilot, fix blockers, respin silicon, or move to production qualification.

Common artifacts: go/no-go memo, pilot readiness report, product release note, errata plan, next milestone plan.

Main Difference

Analog Fit Is Usually Narrower At First

The first safe answer is often "this selected workload may fit under these conditions," not "the chip is generally ready for Physical AI."

narrow proof first

How Mature Digital Companies Usually Think About Sign-Off

Established digital companies already have strong sign-off habits. They usually separate product requirements, architecture modeling, RTL verification, emulation, firmware, drivers, compiler support, board validation, power, thermal, reliability, and customer acceptance.

That discipline is useful for us. We should copy the structure: clear requirements, owned artifacts, test gates, traceable results, release criteria, known limitations, and customer-facing proof packages.

What We Should Copy Directly
  • Requirement traceability from customer need to test result.
  • Compiler/runtime validation before customer demos.
  • Board bring-up logs tied to firmware and hardware versions.
  • Power and thermal measurements from controlled setups.
  • Known-issues and errata style documentation.
  • Go/no-go gates before expanding claims.
What We Must Add For Analog
  • Measured write error and write endurance.
  • Retention and drift over time.
  • Temperature and voltage impact on task accuracy.
  • ADC/DAC precision and scheduling impact.
  • Tile-to-tile mismatch and weak-tile fallback.
  • Calibration profile, recalibration frequency, and failure behavior.

Where Analog Can Be Stronger Than Digital

Analog IMC should not compete everywhere. The best comparison is against digital systems on selected workloads where data movement, sensor distance, power, heat, or latency dominate the problem.

Workload ConditionWhy Digital Can StruggleWhy Analog Might WinProof Needed
Fixed or rarely updated matrix-heavy inference Moving weights and activations through memory can cost a lot of energy. Weights can stay in analog memory and repeated matrix math can happen where the weights live. Energy per inference, task accuracy, retention, drift, calibration, and digital baseline comparison.
Sensor-near processing Sending raw high-rate sensor data to a central processor can waste power and add latency. The chip may reduce or classify data close to the sensor before the main processor is involved. Full sensor-to-answer timing, sensor-path energy, preprocessing cost, and task metric.
Tight power or heat limits Even efficient digital edge processors may be too power-hungry for tiny, sealed, or battery systems. Analog arrays may reduce energy for the repeated matrix section if converters and host transfers are controlled. Same-run power, thermal, latency, and task result under the real duty cycle.
Clear task metric Digital baselines are already strong, so a vague "AI acceleration" claim is not persuasive. Analog can win if it preserves the score that matters while reducing power, heat, or latency. A customer metric such as slip detection, obstacle detection per watt, false alarms, missed faults, or action success.

Where Established Digital Still Has The Advantage

The roadmap should be honest about where digital remains better. This makes the analog claim more credible because it narrows the target instead of pretending analog is universal.

Highly Dynamic Workloads

If the model changes constantly or attention patterns dominate runtime, a digital accelerator may be a better fit unless analog only handles stable subparts.

High-Precision General Compute

Digital hardware is better for exact logic, branching, control flow, safety rules, cryptography, and many software-heavy workloads.

Mature Software Ecosystems

Digital GPUs, NPUs, CPUs, and DSPs already have strong compilers, libraries, drivers, debuggers, and deployment paths.

Production Qualification

Established digital companies often have mature reliability, packaging, supply-chain, security, and customer-support systems.

Safe conclusion: analog should be positioned as a hybrid accelerator for selected workloads, not as a replacement for the whole digital stack.

What This Means For Our Workbench

The workbench should feel like a digital semiconductor validation flow, but with extra analog proof gates. A customer or investor should be able to see the familiar structure and then understand why analog needs additional evidence.

  1. Start like a digital company: collect the workload, profile the model, map operators, estimate timing, build a runtime package, and compare against a baseline.
  2. Add analog proof: simulate physical imperfections, calibrate the simulator, track weight writes, measure drift, include converters, and separate analog from digital sections.
  3. Validate like a product company: run on a board, measure power and heat, score the task, test updates, test fallback, and export a claim-controlled evidence package.

The product should not merely say "analog is efficient." It should say, "Here is the same kind of evidence a mature digital company would require, plus the extra evidence needed because the compute is analog."

Final Answer

Our analog IMC process is not separate from established digital process. It is the digital validation process extended downward into device physics and upward into claim control.

That is the correct shape for the company. It lets us speak the language of mature semiconductor validation while showing the extra work required to make analog useful: calibrated simulation, hybrid compiler mapping, board evidence, task evidence, update evidence, safety evidence, and careful claim boundaries.

Blocked claim: analog IMC is broadly better than digital.

Safe claim: analog IMC can win selected physical-edge workloads when the full evidence package shows task success, power or latency improvement, calibration stability, update fit, and a clear digital baseline comparison.
