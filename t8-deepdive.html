Being sure it's actually correct

silent errors, faults, and proofs
169 papers · 9 read in full · 160 from the abstract

the problemThe problem, in depth

Start with a fact that sounds too simple to matter: a computer chip is a slab of silicon with billions of microscopic switches carved into it, and thin metal wires connecting them. When you ask it to add two numbers, electricity flows through some of those switches and a fresh answer comes out the other side. We have spent seventy years making those switches smaller, faster, and more numerous, and along the way we quietly convinced ourselves of something that is not actually true: that the machine always gives the right answer. It usually does. But 'usually' is a terrifying word once you run millions of these chips day and night for years, because at that scale even a one-in-a-billion mistake happens constantly, and nobody rings a bell when it does.

Here is the deeper reason such a mistake is so hard to catch. The chip does not know it is wrong. When one of those microscopic switches has a tiny manufacturing flaw, or wears out a little, or gets nudged by heat or a stray particle, the arithmetic can come out subtly wrong while every light on the machine stays green. The chip reports success. It hands you a number that is off by a bit, and then your program keeps going as if nothing happened. Engineers call this a silent data corruption, silent because there is no crash, no error message, no smoke, just a wrong answer that flows downstream into everything you compute next. One paper here (hpca-2025-016) actually modeled the guts of modern processors and measured how often the arithmetic units, the parts that literally do the adding and multiplying, quietly produce garbage, and combined this with real-world data showing that at fleet scale roughly one chip in a thousand carries a defect that can corrupt data. Another (asplos-2025-041) spent six years watching real data centers and learned to spot a bad chip not by testing it directly, but by noticing the strange pattern of crashes and log anomalies it leaves in the software running on top of it.

Now connect this to the one root problem that runs underneath this entire book: modern computers are limited not by how fast they can do arithmetic, but by how much data they have to shovel around to feed that arithmetic. A chip today can do a stunning amount of math in the time it takes to fetch a single number from distant memory. So to keep the math units busy, we build enormous, elaborate machinery whose only job is moving data to the right place at the right moment: layers of fast local scratch memory (caches), multiple chips passing data to each other, thousands of these things wired into one giant machine. And here is the cruel twist: correctness is mostly a property of that data-moving machinery, not of the arithmetic. The add itself is simple. What is fiendishly hard is guaranteeing that when two chips both want the same piece of data, they agree on what its current value is; that a number written just before a power failure actually survived; that one part of the machine's work does not leak secretly into another's. The more we spread data out to keep the math fed, the more places there are for the data to get stale, lost, reordered, or corrupted, and the harder it becomes to be sure the whole thing is actually correct.

Make it concrete. Suppose two chips share a scratchpad of recently-used numbers, each keeping its own private nearby copy for speed. Chip A changes a number; Chip B is still looking at the old copy. If nobody enforces a rule that says when one writes, the others must be told, in the right order, at the right moment, the two chips now disagree about reality, and every calculation downstream is built on sand. The rulebook that prevents this is called a coherence protocol, and it is one of the most intricate things humans design. One paper here (asplos-2025-134) took the official industry rulebook for how chips from different vendors keep their shared data in sync, translated it into precise mathematics, and discovered it contained genuine holes, cases where two correct-looking implementations would silently disagree, leading to five official corrections to the standard. The rulebook the whole industry trusted was, in five places, wrong.

There is a second, subtler reason correctness is hard, and it lives inside a single chip even when nothing is broken. To go fast, modern chips cheat: they guess. When a chip reaches a fork in your program, rather than wait to learn which way to go, it picks a direction and starts running down it speculatively, ready to throw the work away if it guessed wrong. It also does many things at once and lets parts of a calculation finish out of order, as long as it can paper over the mess so that from the outside it looks orderly. This guessing and reordering is where most of a chip's speed comes from, but it makes the chip's true behavior fantastically more complicated than the simple story of do this, then do that. The rules for what a fast, guessing, reordering chip is even allowed to do are so subtle that, remarkably, they were never fully written down for real commercial chips until recently. Papers isca-2025-097 and asplos-2025-137 wrestle with exactly this: pinning down, precisely and provably, what such a chip is permitted to do, so we can even state what correct means before trying to check it.

So the problem this theme is about is not make the chip faster. It is the quieter, more unsettling question underneath everything: when a machine hands you an answer, how do you actually know it is right? And know is a strong word here. Not we tested it a lot and it seemed fine, testing shows the presence of bugs, never their absence, you can run a billion examples and the billion-and-first is the one that breaks. What we reach for in the hardest parts of this field is proof: a mathematical argument that a certain kind of mistake simply cannot happen, ever, on any input. That is a wildly ambitious goal for something as big and messy as a real processor, and much of the work here is the long grind of making proof scale up to real machines instead of toy ones. The stakes grew sharply with the way we now compute: training a single large AI model can mean running tens of thousands of chips flat-out for months, where hardware failure is not a rare accident but the weather (hpca-2025-049), and a single silently-wrong number injected early can quietly poison the entire months-long result with no crash to warn you.

the approachesHow the field actually tackles it

1.Catch the errors after the fact by watching for symptoms

The most practical approach, because it works on the messy real world we already have, is to stop trying to prevent every silent error and instead become very good at noticing when one has happened. The insight is that even a silent wrong answer usually leaves ripples. A program fed a corrupted number tends, sooner or later, to crash, to write a nonsensical entry into its logs, to dump its memory, or to fail in some visible way. On its own, any one such failure looks like an ordinary software bug. But if you watch millions of machines over years, and you notice that one particular physical chip sits behind a suspiciously large share of these failures, you can point a finger at the silicon itself.

This is the strategy of Hardware Sentinel (asplos-2025-041), which correlates software-level symptoms, crashes, core dumps, log anomalies, with the identity of the specific chip they ran on, statistically fishing out the defective ones across years of fleet data and dozens of data centers, without needing any special test circuitry built into the chip. A companion effort, Veritas (hpca-2025-016), goes deeper into the chip's arithmetic units to build a model of where these corruptions are born, and crucially estimates how often they really strike across many chip designs, turning a vague fear into a measured rate.

The trade-off is honesty about limits. This approach cannot catch a corruption that never causes any visible symptom, the one that quietly poisons a result and slips away. It is a statistical dragnet, not a guarantee. But it has the enormous advantage of working on the actual chips deployed today, and it turns an invisible enemy into something you can count and locate.

2.Prove correctness with mathematics before trusting the hardware

The opposite philosophy refuses to rely on catching errors later, and instead proves, with the full rigor of mathematics, that a design cannot misbehave in the first place. The idea is to translate a piece of hardware or software into precise logical statements, then have a machine grind through every possible case to show that some desired property, say that two chips never disagree about a stored value, always holds. Not usually, not in the cases we tested. Always, for every input, forever. This is called formal verification, and when it works it is the strongest assurance there is.

The headline example is the formal proof of a real chip-to-chip coordination rulebook (asplos-2025-134). The team modeled the protocol inside a proof-checking program and proved a bedrock property: that at any moment, either one chip is writing to a location or several are reading it, but never a writer and readers at cross purposes. Getting there required a single logical statement with 796 interlocking clauses and over fifty thousand supporting lemmas, and the effort directly exposed five real errors in the official industry standard. Related work proves an operating system's core secure all the way down to the actual compiled machine code (asplos-2025-055), verifies that a chip's speculative guessing cannot leak secrets (asplos-2025-137), and nails down the precise rules for interruptions on reordering chips (isca-2025-097).

The cost is brutal effort and limited reach. The coordination-protocol proof took twelve person-months. Proofs historically could only handle small, tidy designs, because the number of cases to check explodes as a design grows, which is exactly the wall that H-Houdini (asplos-2025-037) attacks by learning the key facts about a design in pieces rather than swallowing it whole. Formal proof gives certainty, but you pay for that certainty in human years, and it does not yet stretch to the largest systems.

3.Guarantee that transformations preserve meaning

A gentler, more targeted flavor of proof appears when we want to change a design to make it faster or smaller, and need to be certain we did not accidentally change what it does. Compilers and hardware tools constantly rewrite things, replacing one circuit with a cleverer equivalent. The danger is that the cleverer version might behave differently in some corner case. The approach here is to verify each rewrite rule once, proving the before and after are truly equivalent, so it can then be applied freely with confidence.

ElasticMiter (asplos-2025-043) does this for a tricky kind of circuit where signals arrive whenever they happen to arrive rather than on a strict clock, which breaks the usual tools for checking equivalence. It builds a construction that compares two circuits under every possible timing of arriving signals and flags any difference, then uses that to certify simplification rules as safe. In a similar spirit, Cross-Level Debugging (asplos-2025-162) checks the trustworthiness of debugging tools by inspecting the same program two ways and demanding the two views agree, catching bugs in the tools themselves, and QECC-Synth (asplos-2025-164) treats laying out an error-correcting circuit onto real hardware as a solvable optimization puzzle rather than hand craft.

The trade-off is scope: you are not proving the whole system correct, only that a specific transformation is faithful. But that is often exactly what you need, and it is far cheaper than a full system proof. It lets tools optimize aggressively without gambling on correctness.

4.Design to survive failures rather than prevent them

At large scale, some component failing is not a possibility to guard against but a certainty to plan around. This approach accepts that parts will die and focuses on keeping the overall system correct and available anyway. The core mechanism is redundancy and graceful retreat: keep enough of a spare copy, or enough slack, that when something fails the system carries on or steps down gently instead of collapsing.

Several papers embody different versions of this. Phoenix (asplos-2025-012) practices graceful degradation: when a data center runs short on capacity, rather than crashing everything, it switches off the services marked non-essential to keep the critical ones alive, doing this across a hundred thousand machines within seconds. Fault Escaping (asplos-2025-167) handles the modern arrangement where a virtual machine's state is split across two chips: it keeps a consistent copy reachable from both, so if either chip dies the virtual machine hops to the survivor instead of dying with it. PCcheck (asplos-2025-128) tackles the pain of long model-training runs, saving snapshots to permanent storage continuously in the background, overlapped with the ongoing computation, so a crash costs minutes of lost work rather than days. And the reliability study of real training clusters (hpca-2025-049) measures how often these giant jobs actually fail and introduces a clean way to score how much of your expensive machine time is truly productive after accounting for failures and restarts.

The trade-off is overhead and complexity: redundancy costs resources, snapshots cost bandwidth, and coordinating a graceful failover is itself something that can go wrong. But at the scale of modern systems, where failure is constant, this is not optional. The measured picture, spanning millions of jobs and hundreds of millions of chip-hours, shows just how central it has become.

5.Get the ordering right when many parts must agree

A whole family of correctness problems comes down to timing and order: when several parts of a system, or several chips, act on shared data, the result is only correct if things happen in an acceptable order. Because moving and coordinating data is the real bottleneck, there is constant tension between doing this ordering carefully (correct but slow) and doing it loosely (fast but risky). The approach is to find mechanisms that preserve the necessary order while paying as little coordination cost as possible.

This shows up at every level. Inside a single chip, Rush or Wait (hpca-2025-010) deals with special instructions that must lock a piece of shared data to update it safely; it learns to predict whether that data is contended and chooses, on the fly, whether to grab it eagerly or wait, cutting the time other threads are blocked, all with a tiny sixty-four bytes of tracking state. Across chips, CORD (isca-2025-123) notices that the usual way of enforcing write-ordering forces wasteful round-trips back through the originating chip, and moves that ordering responsibility to the shared directory instead, cutting both delay and network traffic. And in the software world, GOLF (asplos-2025-121) catches a specific ordering disease where concurrent tasks wait forever on each other, a deadlock, by cleverly reusing the memory-cleanup machinery already running to spot and break the stuck tasks.

The trade-off is that loosening ordering for speed always risks correctness, so these designs live on a knife-edge, and each must prove or carefully argue that its shortcut never actually breaks the required order. The payoff, when done right, is speed with the ordering guarantees intact, which is exactly what shared-memory AI and scientific workloads need.

6.Verify correctness across the crash and the unexpected boundary

Ordinary reasoning about a program assumes it runs to completion. But correctness often must hold across ugly discontinuities: a sudden power loss, or an interruption that yanks the chip away from what it was doing. The approach here is to reason explicitly about every bad moment where things could be cut off, and prove the system lands in a recoverable state no matter when the interruption strikes.

The crash-consistency work (asplos-2025-052) confronts a new kind of memory that keeps its contents after power loss, which sounds like a gift but is a trap: data can be caught half-written when the power dies, in an impossible in-between state, because programmers routinely forget to force pending writes out to the durable medium in the right order. The paper reduces the question could this crash leave us broken to a form an automatic logical solver can answer, and hunts down these bugs without a human having to guess the failing scenarios. The precise-exceptions work (isca-2025-097) tackles the sibling problem of interruptions on a fast reordering chip, giving the first exact definition of what state the chip must present when execution is snatched away mid-flight, so the interrupted work can be resumed or unwound coherently.

The trade-off is that these are deep, narrow investigations, immense effort spent to make one boundary provably safe. But these boundaries are precisely where silent, catastrophic corruption hides, the corruption that only appears once in a thousand power failures and is nearly impossible to reproduce. Making them rigorous is worth the cost.

7.Correct errors continuously in machines that are always failing

The final approach lives in quantum machines, where reliability is not a rare concern but the entire game. A quantum bit is so fragile that it forgets its value almost as fast as you set it, and errors arrive constantly, like static on a radio. The only way to compute at all is to spread the information of one reliable logical bit across many physical bits and run a nonstop repair process that detects and fixes errors faster than they accumulate. This is quantum error correction, and it is less a feature than the main activity of the machine.

Because the error correction itself dominates the cost, the papers here are about making that repair loop faster and cheaper. Synchronization for fault-tolerant machines (isca-2025-077) notices that different patches of the machine can drift out of step, forcing some to sit idle and accumulate errors while waiting for others, and spreads that idle time into small harmless slivers rather than one damaging gap, cutting the error rate several-fold. SWIPER (isca-2025-106) borrows the guessing trick from classical chips: rather than wait for one repair step to finish before starting the next, it predicts what the answer will be and forges ahead, rolling back on the rare wrong guess. The transversal-architecture work (isca-2025-132) and the early-fault-tolerance work (isca-2025-026) redesign how repairs are structured to slash the overhead, in one case cutting the projected runtime for a landmark code-breaking calculation roughly fiftyfold, in another correcting only the cheap operations fully and handling the expensive ones more sparingly. Interleaved logical qubits (hpca-2025-088) even removes the need to physically shuttle atoms around, a major source of delay and error, by exploiting the physics of how atoms interact at different distances.

The trade-off is that all of this is overhead in the deepest sense: millions of physical bits and days of runtime devoted entirely to making a modest number of trustworthy logical bits. But without it, a quantum machine computes nothing correct at all, so here reliability is not a tax on the computation, it is the computation.

where it standsWhat's solved, what's open

The honest picture is a field that has gotten very good at the extremes and is still struggling in the middle. Catching errors after the fact now works at real fleet scale, and formal proof has, for the first time, reached genuine commercial artifacts, a real coordination protocol, a real operating-system core, real speculation rules, exposing actual bugs in shipping industry standards. Surviving failures at scale is a mature engineering practice, with measured data spanning millions of jobs. And in the quantum world, error correction has moved from theory to concrete, quantified architectures with hard resource numbers.

What remains open is the gap between those poles. The strongest guarantees, the mathematical proofs, still cost person-years each and do not yet stretch to the largest and most complex designs, while the most scalable methods, watching for symptoms in the fleet, remain statistical dragnets that by definition cannot promise they caught the truly silent corruption that left no trace. Nobody yet has a way to get proof-grade certainty at fleet-grade scale and cost. The silent corruptions themselves are still only partially characterized, we are learning how often they strike but not fully why, or how to stop them cheaply in the chips we already own. And the quantum repair machinery, however clever, remains staggeringly expensive, spending the overwhelming bulk of the machine on catching errors rather than doing useful work. The root difficulty has not changed: it is not the arithmetic we cannot trust, it is everything that happens to a number while it is in motion, shared, guessed at, and saved, and that is precisely the part that gets harder, not easier, as systems grow.

Formalising CXL Cache Coherence

What it does

CXL (Compute Express Link) is an emerging industry standard that lets CPUs, GPUs, and accelerators share each other's memory caches seamlessly over a fast link — like giving every chip in a server a shared whiteboard. The part of the standard that keeps those caches consistent (CXL.cache) was written in English prose, which turned out to contain ambiguities, contradictions, and at least one rule that, if relaxed, produces silently wrong answers. This paper translates CXL.cache into precise mathematical notation and then machine-proves that the protocol actually guarantees cache safety.

The method, in detail

The idea. Cache coherence means that if chip A writes a value, chip B must never read a stale old version. The 'single writer, multiple reader' (SWMR) property captures this: at any moment, at most one chip may hold write access to a given memory location, and if someone holds write access no one else may hold even read access. Proving SWMR for a real industrial protocol is hard because the protocol has dozens of message types and hundreds of legal sequences.

How it works. The authors encoded the entire CXL.cache protocol in Isabelle/HOL — a proof assistant, a program that checks mathematical arguments with machine precision. They modeled the system as a state-transition machine with 20 components (two device caches, a host cache, multiple message channels, buffers, and a transaction counter) and wrote 68 rules, each capturing one legal message exchange. They then ran litmus tests — small concrete scenarios taken from the CXL specification — to confirm the model behaves as expected, and showed that relaxing one key rule ('Snoop-pushes-GO', which prevents a new invalidation message from overtaking an in-flight permission grant) immediately causes both devices to believe they hold exclusive write access simultaneously — a coherence violation.

The key design choice. SWMR alone is not an inductive property (the system can pass through unreachable bad-looking states), so the proof required constructing a stronger invariant — a 796-conjunct logical statement that implies SWMR and is preserved by every one of the 68 rules. That requires 53,332 individual sub-lemmas. The authors built a custom automation tool called super_sketch that invokes Isabelle's automated theorem prover on each sub-lemma unsupervised, filling in found proofs automatically. Without it the proof could not have been completed in any reasonable time.

The catch. The model covers only two devices and one memory location; the SWMR proof does not yet guarantee freedom from deadlock or liveness.

The result. Four of five proposed fixes to the CXL specification have been accepted by the CXL Consortium for inclusion in the next version of the standard.

No Rush in Executing Atomic Instructions

What it does

Modern processors now execute atomic read-modify-write instructions—operations that lock a memory location, modify it, and release it without interference from other cores—without the old mandatory 'fence' barriers that serialized everything. The problem is that launching these lock operations the moment their inputs are ready can hold the memory location locked for a long time, blocking other cores that need it. This paper presents Rush or Wait (RoW), a tiny hardware predictor that decides, per instruction, whether to launch atomics immediately (good when no one else is fighting for that location) or delay until the last moment (good when other cores are competing).

The method, in detail

The idea. An atomic instruction like fetch-and-add on x86 processors now runs without mandatory serializing fences. Two policies are possible: 'eager' (launch as soon as the operands are ready, hide latency by overlapping with older instructions) and 'lazy' (hold off until this instruction is the oldest pending memory operation and the store buffer is drained, minimizing the time the cache line is locked). Neither is universally best: canneal and freqmine run 42–26% faster with eager; pc and sps run 43–44% faster with lazy.

How it works. RoW uses a 64-entry table of 4-bit saturating counters—essentially the same hardware trick used by branch predictors—indexed by the 12 least-significant bits of the instruction's program counter (XOR-folded). When a counter is high the instruction executes lazy; when low, eager. The key challenge is detecting contention accurately to train the counters.

Three contention detectors, applied in layers. First, the 'execution window' check watches whether a coherence invalidation arrives while the cache line is locked—simple but misses cases where the lock is released before the message arrives. Second, the 'ready window' check starts monitoring as soon as the address is computed, even if the instruction is still waiting to execute lazy. Third, a 'directory detection' check flags a cache line as contended when it arrives from another core's private cache with latency above 400 cycles, catching cases fully resolved before any invalidation reaches this core.

Atomic locality override. When a lazy-predicted atomic finds a matching older store in the store buffer it flips to eager, keeping the cache line warm.

The result. RoW with all three detectors reduces average execution time by 9.2% and up to 43% over always-eager execution, using only 64 bytes of extra storage.

isca-2025-077full textISCA

Synchronization for Fault-Tolerant Quantum Computers

What it does

Quantum computers need multiple logical qubits to stay in lockstep before they can perform joint operations, but in practice different qubits drift out of phase — a problem called desynchronization — due to mixing different error-correction codes, fabrication defects, and inter-chip communication delays. This paper defines three policies for bringing qubits back into sync, and shows that spreading the required waiting time across many small pauses rather than one big one dramatically reduces the extra errors that creep in while a qubit sits idle.

The method, in detail

The idea. Every logical qubit — a reliable qubit constructed from many fragile physical qubits using an error-correcting code — must run through a fixed cycle of gates and measurements called a syndrome generation cycle. When two qubits need to be 'fused' (a Lattice Surgery merge operation, the main way quantum gates are performed at scale), both cycles must start at exactly the same moment. If one qubit is ahead of the other, the leading qubit must pause and wait — but any pause lets errors accumulate through a process called decoherence, where quantum states decay due to noise.

How it works. The paper defines three sync policies. Passive sync (the baseline) stops the leading qubit right before the merge for the full waiting time in one go — the simplest approach but the most damaging, since a long uninterrupted idle window lets errors pile up. Active sync breaks that same total waiting time into tiny fragments and inserts one fragment at the end of each error-correction round, so no single pause is long enough to cause serious decoherence. Hybrid sync goes further: when the two qubits have different cycle times, it first runs a few extra correction rounds to shrink the remaining mismatch, then applies the Active approach to handle whatever slack is left.

The key design choice. The insight is that many short pauses are far less harmful than one long pause of equal total duration. A short idle window gives decoherence less time to corrupt each individual qubit, so fewer errors reach the decoder — even though the overall delay is the same.

The result. A lightweight hardware unit (a table of counters plus a small calculator) determines the current phase of each qubit pair at runtime and injects the appropriate pause schedule into the controller without slowing down the critical computation path. Compared to Passive sync, Active reduces the logical error rate by up to 2.4x for a single synchronization; Hybrid achieves up to 3.4x improvement. Across full quantum programs, using Passive instead of Active can inflate the final error rate by up to 23x, and the lower error rates enable a 2.2x speedup in the error-correction decoder.

Resource Analysis of Low-Overhead Transversal Architectures for Reconfigurable Atom Arrays

What it does

Quantum computers make errors constantly, so they must constantly check and correct themselves, which burns enormous amounts of time. This paper designs a complete architecture for fault-tolerant quantum computing on neutral-atom hardware that replaces the slow standard error-checking scheme with a faster one called transversal gates, cutting the estimated run time for cracking 2048-bit RSA encryption from years down to 5.6 days using 19 million physical qubits — roughly a 50x speed-up with no increase in the number of qubits needed.

The method, in detail

The idea. Quantum computers encode each 'logical qubit' (the reliable logical unit of computation) across many physical qubits (the noisy atoms) using a quantum error-correcting code. Every logical gate operation then requires several rounds of 'syndrome extraction' — measuring certain combinations of neighboring qubits to detect errors without disturbing the stored data. Standard schemes based on lattice surgery (joining and splitting code patches along their edges) need roughly d rounds of syndrome extraction per logical gate, where d is the code distance, a number typically around 27–30 for large algorithms. That time overhead compounds to years for algorithms like Shor's factoring. Transversal gates are a different approach: instead of patching code blocks together at their edges, you physically interleave two code patches side by side and apply the same physical gate to every matching pair of atoms simultaneously. Because errors never spread from one code block to the other, this is inherently fault-tolerant and requires only about 1 syndrome extraction round per gate — a factor-of-d speedup in time.

How it works. The authors decompose Shor's 2048-bit factoring algorithm into three reusable building blocks, each implemented with transversal gates and laid out to keep atom movement distances small. The magic state factory converts 8 noisy 'T-type' resource states into one high-quality three-qubit gate resource using the [[8,3,2]] quantum error-correcting code. The quantum adder uses a ripple-carry circuit made of MAJ (majority-vote) and UMA (undo-majority-and-add) blocks, each consuming one of those factory outputs. The quantum lookup table loads classically precomputed values into a quantum register by fanning out a control signal using GHZ states (maximally entangled states of many qubits), prepared with only local atom moves.

The key design choice. Every sub-circuit is laid out so atom movements stay local — no qubit ever needs to travel more than a few code-patch widths — which keeps movement time short and the decoding problem tractable. A heuristic formula calibrated against simulation captures how transversal gates elevate the effective noise rate (characterized by a factor alpha ≈ 1/6), letting the team choose the right code distance to hit the required error budget without over-engineering.

The result. For 2048-bit RSA factoring with 1 millisecond quantum error correction cycles and 19 million physical qubits, the estimated run time is 5.6 days — compared to years in prior estimates under similar hardware assumptions — representing close to a 50x wall-clock speedup with no increase in qubit count.

mlsys-2025-011full textMLSYS

Uncertainty Disentanglement for Multimodal Foundation Models in Robotic Systems

What it does

When a robot uses a large AI model to look at a camera image and decide what to do next, two entirely different things can go wrong: the camera view might be blurry or occluded (a perception problem), or the model might simply not know the right rule for the situation (a planning problem). Today's systems lump these together as generic "uncertainty," so when something fails it's impossible to know which fix to apply. This paper presents a framework that separates the two kinds of uncertainty, measures each one independently, and triggers a targeted fix — re-observe the scene if vision is poor; automatically retrain the model if planning is poor.

The method, in detail

The idea. Think of a robot's AI pipeline as two stages in a factory: first a vision scanner (the visual encoder, based on CLIP) that looks at the camera image and recognizes objects, then a text generator (a language model, based on LLaVA) that reads the list of recognized objects and writes a plan. Each stage can fail independently.

Measuring perception uncertainty. After the vision scanner scores its confidence for each object it recognizes (a raw number between 0 and 1), that raw score is unreliable — high confidence does not guarantee correct recognition. The paper uses conformal prediction, a statistical technique that calibrates these raw scores against a set of known-correct reference examples, and converts each score into a provable lower bound on the probability of correct identification. If this calibrated perception uncertainty score falls below a threshold (for example, below a 70% probability of correct identification), the robot is instructed to look again — rotate the camera, switch to another view, or wait briefly and retake the image.

Measuring decision uncertainty. For the planning step, the system takes the text plan the model generated and converts it into a formal state machine (called a Kripke structure) — a compact graph where each node is a step in the plan and edges represent transitions. It then runs a model checker (a formal verification tool) against a set of logical rules written in temporal logic, such as "always wait for pedestrians" or "never turn right at a red light." The checker returns a binary yes/no for whether the plan satisfies every rule. By gathering many such yes/no examples offline on calibration data and again applying conformal prediction to the model's confidence scores, the system constructs a decision uncertainty score — a probabilistic guarantee that the plan is safe — without needing any human-labeled ground truth.

Intervention. If perception uncertainty is low (bad image), the robot re-observes. If decision uncertainty is low even with a good image, the system automatically filters out low-confidence training examples and fine-tunes the model on only the high-certainty, rule-passing data — no human annotation required.

The result. On real-world driving tests, the combined approach raises the probability of generating specification-compliant plans from about 32% (raw model with active sensing only) to 95.9%, and reduces plan-to-plan variability (standard deviation) by 40% compared to a baseline fine-tuning approach that does not separate the two uncertainty types.

mlsys-2025-020full textMLSYS

Interference-Aware Edge Runtime Prediction with Conformal Matrix Completion

What it does

When you run software on edge devices — small computers close to where data is produced, like a smart factory sensor or a phone — you often need to predict how long a program will take to run, including when other programs are running alongside it and slowing things down. This paper introduces Pitot, a system that predicts those runtimes accurately even when you have only seen a handful of measurements, on a variety of very different hardware types, and even when multiple programs are competing for resources at the same time. It also provides mathematically guaranteed upper-bound estimates — not just a best guess, but a provable promise that the true runtime will fall below the predicted ceiling with a stated probability.

The method, in detail

The idea. Runtime on diverse hardware is wildly variable: a microcontroller might take 1000x longer than an x86 server. And when two programs share a device, one can slow the other down by 20x. Predicting this from sparse measurements is the core challenge.

1. Log-residual objective. Runtimes span many orders of magnitude, so Pitot works in log space. 3. Interference term. An activation function handles the case where little interference happens until a threshold is crossed.

4. Provably bounded uncertainty. Using conformalized quantile regression — a technique that wraps any predictor with a statistically valid confidence interval — Pitot produces an upper-bound prediction guaranteed to cover the true runtime at least (1-epsilon) percent of the time. A smart trick: train on multiple quantile targets and pick the one that produces the tightest bound at calibration time, without retraining.
