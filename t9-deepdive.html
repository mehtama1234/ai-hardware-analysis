Beyond the GPU

new kinds of chips, and new jobs for them
184 papers · 12 read in full · 172 from the abstract

the problemThe problem, in depth

Start with a fact that sounds too simple to matter: a modern chip can do arithmetic almost for free. Adding or multiplying two numbers takes a tiny sliver of energy and happens in a fraction of a billionth of a second. What is expensive is not the thinking, it is the fetching. The numbers the chip wants to work on live in memory, which sits some distance away on the board, and hauling a number from memory to the arithmetic unit costs far more time and energy than the arithmetic itself, often by a factor of a hundred or more. Picture a brilliant accountant who can add any column of figures instantly, but whose ledgers are stored in a warehouse across town. The accountant is never the bottleneck. The trips to the warehouse are. This one fact, that moving data dominates doing math, is the root problem underneath almost everything in this chapter.

For ordinary tasks we hide this cost with a flexible, mass-produced chip, the kind inside a graphics card, which we will just call a flexible chip. A flexible chip is a marvel of compromise. It can run almost any program, and to make that possible it keeps thousands of small arithmetic units fed by an elaborate system of nearby scratch storage, wide pipes to memory, and clever scheduling that tries to keep the warehouse trips overlapping with useful work. That compromise is exactly why it is a compromise: because it must be ready for anything, it cannot be perfectly shaped for any one thing. For the mainstream job of training and running neural networks it is close enough, and the rest of this book is largely about squeezing that mainstream case. This chapter is about the opposite: the workloads where the flexible chip's shape is simply wrong, and where the only way forward is to change the shape of the hardware itself, or to invent a different kind of machine entirely.

Why would a workload's needs be so different? Because the ratio of fetching to math, and the pattern of the fetching, varies wildly from one kind of problem to another, and the flexible chip is tuned for only one region of that space. Consider computing on secret data without ever unlocking it, a technique that lets a server run a calculation on numbers it cannot read. To make a number unreadable this way, you bloat it: a single secret number becomes a giant bundle of thousands of ordinary numbers, so every operation touches an enormous amount of data. Now the warehouse trips utterly swamp the math, and worse, the math itself keeps degrading the secret, adding a kind of static that must periodically be scrubbed out with an expensive cleanup step. A flexible chip drowns here, not because it cannot do the arithmetic, but because it cannot move that much data fast enough. Several papers in this theme measure the raw scale of the pain: an encrypted version of a language model that takes seventeen hours on an ordinary processor, and the years-long effort to drag that down to seconds.

Now swing to the far opposite corner and consider a quantum computer. Here the problem is not too much data movement but that the fundamental unit of storage, the qubit, is desperately fragile. A qubit forgets what it holds in a fraction of a second and is corrupted by the faintest disturbance, so a quantum machine spends most of its effort not computing but constantly checking itself for errors and correcting them before the errors spread. This checking has to finish within a hair's-breadth deadline, under a millionth of a second, or the errors pile up faster than they can be fixed. So an entire supporting cast of ordinary, non-quantum electronics has to sit next to the quantum chip, reading out its state, deciding what went wrong, and reacting, all under a merciless clock. That supporting electronics is a hardware design problem exactly like the ones in the rest of this book, just in service of a strange and delicate new kind of computer.

Between these two poles lie a dozen more domains, each with its own peculiar shape. Drawing lifelike 3D scenes for virtual reality out of millions of soft translucent blobs, where the hard part is that every blob touches many pixels and the work per pixel is wildly uneven. Aligning strands of DNA, where the data is irregular and sequential and refuses to line up neatly into the tidy grids a flexible chip loves. Robots that must, within a single control loop, both run a neural network and solve a mathematical planning problem, two workloads with opposite appetites. Computers small and thin enough to print onto a sticker or weave into clothing, where the entire budget is a few thousandths of a watt. Even datacenters flying on satellites in orbit, where every gram and every watt is rationed. What unites them is not the application. It is the recognition that the general-purpose machine is the wrong shape, and that real gains, ten times, a hundred times, sometimes a thousand times faster or more efficient, come only from building the machine around the true structure of the problem, and above all around its data movement.

That is the deep reason this whole theme exists. The flexible chip was a bargain we struck to avoid designing new hardware for every problem. This chapter is a catalog of the problems for which that bargain no longer pays, where the cost of moving data, or the fragility of a new kind of memory, or a power budget measured in crumbs, forces us back to first principles and asks: if this were the only thing your machine ever had to do, how would you build it?

the approachesHow the field actually tackles it

1.Move the computing to where the data already sits

If the root problem is the cost of hauling data from memory to the arithmetic unit, the most direct fix is to stop hauling it: put small arithmetic units right inside the memory, or right next to it, and do the work there. This is often called processing-in-memory or near-memory processing, but the plain idea is simply to bring the calculator to the filing cabinet instead of carrying every file to the calculator. It works because memory chips are physically organized into many small banks that can all be busy at once; if each bank has a tiny bit of its own math capability, you get an enormous amount of arithmetic happening in parallel without any of it needing to cross the expensive gap to a central processor.

This shows up most sharply in encrypted computing, where the data is so bloated that movement is everything. One paper (hpca-2025-006) makes a telling discovery: on a flexible chip, the bottleneck for encrypted math was not the fancy transform everyone assumed, but the dull, simple element-by-element arithmetic, because that simple arithmetic touches so much data that it saturates the pipe to memory. Their answer is to do exactly that simple arithmetic inside the memory itself. Others push the same idea further out the hierarchy: one design (asplos-2025-067) does encrypted text search inside the flash storage chips, so the data never even leaves the drive, and another (hpca-2025-070) weaves the computation through the internal bank structure of memory so results never have to cross between banks.

The tradeoff is that memory is a hostile place to compute. There is very little room for logic, the operations you can build are simple, and you must carefully arrange your data so that each little in-memory calculator finds its operands in its own local patch, never needing to reach across to a neighbor. So this approach shines exactly when the workload is drowning in simple, regular, data-heavy operations, and it is far less useful when the computation is intricate and irregular.

2.Build one machine perfectly shaped for one problem

The oldest and most fundamental move in this whole field is to give up on flexibility entirely and carve a chip whose circuits are laid out to match one specific computation. This is a fixed-function chip, sometimes called an application-specific chip. Because every wire and every arithmetic unit exists only to serve the target task, nothing is wasted on being ready for other tasks: the data flows through the silicon along a path that mirrors the calculation, and the expensive warehouse trips are designed away by keeping intermediate results moving directly from one unit to the next. The reward is the largest speedups in the theme, often hundreds to thousands of times over a general-purpose processor.

You see this whenever a workload is important enough and stable enough to justify the enormous cost of designing a chip. There are fixed-function chips for generating cryptographic proofs (isca-2025-025, which reports roughly eight hundred times a server processor), for the whole encrypted-computing pipeline (hpca-2025-037), and for a robot's tightly coupled learning-and-planning control loop (isca-2025-104). The design work is largely the art of finding what the different phases of the workload have in common and building shared circuitry for it: one proof-generation chip (isca-2025-025) saves nearly half its area by noticing that three different checking phases can share the same multiplier hardware.

The cost is exactly the flexibility you threw away. A fixed-function chip is expensive and slow to design, and if the problem changes, for instance if the cryptography community invents a better algorithm, your beautiful custom silicon may be obsolete before it ships. This tension, that the very rigidity which makes these chips fast also makes them brittle, is what motivates the next two approaches.

3.Make the custom machine bendable, so it fits a whole family of problems

A fixed-function chip is fast but frozen; a flexible chip is bendable but slow. A large part of this theme lives in the middle, building hardware that is specialized enough to be fast yet reconfigurable enough to serve a family of related tasks rather than a single one. The general trick is to break the work down into a small set of common building-block operations, build efficient circuitry for those blocks, and then let the machine rewire the connections between the blocks at runtime to match whichever specific task is in front of it. You are not designing one machine; you are designing a kit of parts and the ability to snap them together differently on demand.

This pattern recurs across very different domains. For drawing 3D scenes, one accelerator (hpca-2025-054) notices that all the competing rendering methods share a handful of underlying operations and builds a chip that reroutes its dataflow to match whichever method it is asked to run. For cryptographic proofs, another (hpca-2025-107) uses uniform arithmetic units linked by a switchable on-chip network, reconfiguring them for each stage of the proof instead of building dedicated hardware per stage. A third family targets reprogrammable chips called field-programmable gate arrays, a kind of hardware whose wiring can be redefined after manufacture; papers here build shared abstractions (asplos-2025-078) and streaming networks (isca-2025-003) that let one physical board serve many different accelerator jobs and even pipeline the layers of a neural network together without re-wiring between them.

The tradeoff is a bet about how much variety you actually need. Reconfigurability costs area and complexity: the switches and the general-purpose building blocks are less efficient than perfectly dedicated circuits. If your family of tasks is genuinely diverse and still evolving, that overhead buys you longevity and is worth paying. If in the end you only ever run one task, you would have been better off with the frozen fixed-function chip. The whole craft is judging where on that spectrum a given domain sits.

4.Rewrite the problem so there is less work to move

Not every advance is a new chip. A surprisingly large fraction of this theme is a compiler, a program that translates a high-level description of a computation into the exact low-level steps a machine runs, and does so cleverly enough to slash how much work there is in the first place. This matters because the real bottleneck, data movement and expensive cleanup steps, can often be attacked before any hardware is involved, simply by being smarter about what operations to do and in what order. The best software optimization is the operation you managed to avoid entirely.

In encrypted computing this is vivid. Recall that the secret-preserving math slowly accumulates static that must be scrubbed out by an expensive cleanup step. Several papers are compilers that figure out the minimum number of cleanups and the best places to insert them: one (asplos-2025-088) treats it as a classic problem of finding the narrowest bottleneck in a flow network to prove it is using the fewest possible cleanups, and another (asplos-2025-029) extends this reasoning to programs with loops, where the static builds up across repeated iterations. The quantum world has a rich parallel: compilers there shrink the number of fragile operations a quantum program needs, by mathematically canceling whole blocks of the circuit and computing their effect on paper instead of on the hardware (hpca-2025-007), by reusing qubits so fewer are needed (isca-2025-057), or by reusing intermediate results across the thousands of near-identical simulation runs an experiment requires (isca-2025-012).

The strength of this approach is that it is comparatively cheap and it composes with everything else: a better compiler makes both flexible and custom hardware faster. Its limit is that it can only remove work that is genuinely redundant. Once a compiler has wrung out the waste, you are left facing the irreducible core of the computation, and only then do you truly need new silicon. Software and hardware here are partners, not rivals.

5.Co-design the algorithm and the hardware together

The most powerful results in this theme almost never come from optimizing hardware alone or software alone. They come from redesigning both at once, deliberately reshaping the algorithm so that it becomes easy for hardware to run, and shaping the hardware around the reshaped algorithm. This is co-design. The core insight is that the boundary between what the algorithm asks for and what the machine provides is not fixed; if you are allowed to move both, you can find solutions neither side could reach alone. Often the winning move is to change the algorithm into a form with regular, predictable, local data access, because regularity is precisely what lets hardware avoid the expensive scattered warehouse trips.

The real-time 3D rendering work is a clean example. Drawing a scene from millions of soft translucent blobs is punishing because the blobs touch pixels in a scattered, uneven way, and adding up their contributions causes many little pieces of the machine to fight over the same memory locations. One co-design (micro-2025-051) simultaneously prunes away unimportant blobs in the algorithm and builds special hardware that stores intermediate results so they need not be recomputed, and that merges the scattered updates in an orderly pipeline instead of a chaotic scramble. Another (asplos-2025-032) reorganizes how a chip traces rays of light so that nearby rays reuse the same fetched data, and reclaims idle processing units to keep more rays in flight at once. In encrypted computing, one string-matching design (asplos-2025-067) rewrites the algorithm to eliminate its most expensive operation entirely, replacing costly multiplications with cheap additions, precisely so the hardware has less to do.

The tradeoff is that co-design demands rare, hard-won expertise spanning both the mathematics of the domain and the physics of the chip, and the result is tightly wedded to one problem. But when a workload matters enough to justify that effort, co-design routinely delivers the theme's headline numbers, tens of times faster and tens of times more energy-efficient, because it attacks the mismatch between problem and machine from both sides at once.

6.Compute with a different physical medium: light, analog signals, or time

The boldest branch of this theme abandons the ordinary digital transistor as the thing that does the arithmetic, and reaches for a different piece of physics that performs the desired math naturally, almost for free, as a side effect of how the medium behaves. The appeal is that certain physical processes just are a computation: shine light through the right arrangement of components and the interference of the light waves performs a matrix multiplication; let electric charge accumulate on wires and the charges add themselves up with no logic gates at all. If the physics does your math for you, you may sidestep both the arithmetic cost and much of the data-movement cost in one stroke.

Several papers explore this. Photonic designs (isca-2025-071) compute with beams of light in a crossbar arrangement, reporting hundreds of trillions of operations per second at a few watts, an efficiency digital chips struggle to touch. Analog designs let physical quantities like current stand in for numbers: one (isca-2025-114) builds a chip whose natural settling into a low-energy state solves a graph problem, and even uses feedback current directly as the training signal, so the machine learns at the speed of electrons rather than software. A third idea (isca-2025-040) encodes a number not as a voltage but as the moment in time a pulse arrives, so that adding numbers becomes a matter of adding delays, reporting far better energy than the biologically inspired alternative it competes with.

The deep tradeoff is that physics is analog, continuous, and noisy, whereas digital computing earned its dominance precisely by being crisp and error-free. Light and charge and timing all drift, blur, and accumulate imprecision, so these machines fight a constant battle for accuracy and are usually specialists, brilliant at one operation like matrix multiplication and helpless at general logic. This is the least mature and most speculative corner of the theme, a genuine bet that for the right workloads the raw efficiency of computing-as-physics will be worth the loss of digital certainty.

7.The special demands of quantum machines

Quantum computing earns its own place in this theme not because it is one more application, but because it inverts the usual problem. Everywhere else the enemy is moving data; here the enemy is that the memory itself, the qubit, is almost impossibly fragile. A qubit loses its contents in a flicker and is corrupted by the slightest noise, so a quantum machine must spend the overwhelming majority of its effort not on the calculation you care about but on continuously detecting and repairing errors before they spread and ruin everything. This is error correction, and it imposes a brutal real-time deadline: the repair has to complete within under a millionth of a second, cycle after cycle, or the errors outrun the fixing.

That deadline turns quantum computing into a hardware-design problem of exactly the kind this book is about, just aimed at supporting a strange new machine. A cluster of papers builds ordinary fast electronics whose only job is to keep the fragile quantum part alive: an error-decoding engine built on reprogrammable hardware that meets the sub-microsecond deadline for the first time (asplos-2025-148), a scheme that speculatively guesses the outcome of a quantum measurement using branch prediction borrowed straight from ordinary processors (isca-2025-103), a low-cost detector for when a qubit slips into a forbidden state (micro-2025-078), and control architectures that coordinate many controllers with almost no synchronization overhead (micro-2025-007). Others reach further out, designing quantum memory that can serve many requests at once (asplos-2025-154), architectures that combine two families of error-correcting codes to get the best of both (asplos-2025-126), and the plumbing to link separate quantum processors together reliably (isca-2025-088, isca-2025-128).

The honest tradeoff is maturity. This is engineering for a computer that, at useful scale, does not yet exist. The work is a bet that classical hardware ingenuity, decoders, predictors, controllers, memory architectures, can tame quantum fragility enough to make the whole enterprise practical. It belongs in this chapter because it is the purest illustration of the theme's thesis: when a computation has a shape utterly unlike anything a general-purpose chip was built for, you have no choice but to design the machine from first principles around what the problem actually needs.

where it standsWhat's solved, what's open

The mature, load-bearing part of this theme is the classic toolkit for reshaping hardware around a workload: fixed-function chips, reconfigurable accelerators, in-memory and near-memory computing, and above all the algorithm-and-hardware co-design that ties them together. In the domains where the workload is stable and valuable enough to justify the effort, encrypted computing, cryptographic proofs, real-time 3D rendering and the visual perception loops inside robots and headsets, these techniques reliably deliver the theme's dramatic wins, tens to thousands of times faster or more energy-efficient than a general-purpose chip. What is genuinely solved is the method: given a well-understood workload dominated by data movement, the field knows how to find its regular structure, keep its data local, and build a machine that fits.

What remains open is larger and more uncertain. The exotic physical media, light, analog charge, and timing-based computing, promise extraordinary efficiency but are still fighting the fundamental battle against noise and imprecision, and remain narrow specialists rather than general machines; whether they graduate from promising demonstrations to dependable products is an open bet. Quantum computing is the most speculative frontier of all: the surrounding classical hardware is advancing impressively, meeting once-impossible real-time deadlines, but it is scaffolding for a machine that does not yet exist at useful size, and no one can honestly promise when it will. Even in the mature areas, the persistent gap is durability versus specialization, the more perfectly a design fits today's problem, the faster it is orphaned when the algorithm or the application shifts, and the field has no clean answer to how much flexibility is worth its cost. The theme's deepest unsolved question is therefore not how to build a fast machine for a known problem, but how to keep pace when the problems themselves keep changing underneath the silicon.

Micro Blossom: Accelerated Minimum-Weight Perfect Matching Decoding for Quantum Error Correction

What it does

Quantum computers make errors constantly, so they rely on a mathematical referee called a decoder that watches for telltale error signatures and figures out what went wrong — but this referee must respond in under one millionth of a second or the quantum computation degrades. The blossom algorithm is the most accurate known decoder but was considered too complicated to run that fast in hardware; this paper presents Micro Blossom, the first hardware-accelerated blossom decoder to break the one-microsecond barrier, reaching 0.8 microseconds average latency.

The method, in detail

The problem. A surface code (a standard arrangement of physical qubits — the quantum analogue of bits) needs its error-decoding referee to finish before the next batch of error checks arrives, roughly once per microsecond. Every extra microsecond of delay adds idle errors that make the quantum computer less reliable. The blossom algorithm finds the best error explanation but, on a standard CPU, takes several microseconds for useful code sizes.

The idea. The blossom algorithm works in two alternating phases: a "dual" phase that adjusts abstract budgets to find when two error clusters are about to collide, and a "primal" phase that resolves the collision and updates bookkeeping. The dual phase is the bottleneck — it dominates 80%+ of runtime at practical code sizes. Micro Blossom moves the dual phase into parallel hardware while keeping the primal phase on a CPU.

How it works. The hardware (implemented on an FPGA — a reconfigurable chip) instantiates one tiny processing unit per vertex and one per edge of the error-detection graph. For a distance-13 surface code this means about 2,197 vertex units and roughly 10,000 edge units all running simultaneously. Each unit holds only a handful of local numbers and talks only to its direct neighbors — no global memory, no central coordinator. When the CPU broadcasts a simple instruction ("grow by length L"), all units update in one clock tick. Conflict detection — checking whether two growing error clusters have touched — is done by edge units in parallel and reported back via a tree of comparators in logarithmic time.

The key design choice. Isolated errors (single-qubit mistakes far from other errors, the overwhelmingly common case at low noise rates) are detected and fully resolved inside the hardware without any CPU round-trip, cutting the average number of CPU interactions from O(p × N) to O(p² × N), where p is the physical error rate and N is the number of qubits. When a new round of measurements arrives, the hardware fuses it into the ongoing solution immediately rather than waiting for all rounds.

The result. Average decoding latency of 0.8 microseconds at code distance 13 and 0.1% error rate — 8× faster than the best previously published software decoder (Sparse Blossom on Apple M1 Max), with exact accuracy preserved.

Fat-Tree QRAM: A High-Bandwidth Shared Quantum Random Access Memory for Parallel Queries

What it does

A quantum computer needs a special kind of memory — Quantum Random Access Memory (QRAM) — to look up data stored in a superposition of many addresses simultaneously, a trick that gives quantum algorithms their speedup. The best existing QRAM design (Bucket-Brigade) can only serve one query at a time because a single bottleneck node monopolizes the hardware for the entire duration of each query, so multiple quantum processors sharing the same memory must wait in line. Fat-Tree QRAM redesigns the internal routing so that log(N) independent queries run in parallel in the same log(N) time that a single query used to take, using only twice as many quantum bits (qubits), without sacrificing error resilience.

The method, in detail

The idea. In Bucket-Brigade (BB) QRAM, data is accessed by routing quantum bits through a binary tree of quantum router nodes. The root node of the tree is a bottleneck: every query must enter and exit through it, so the root is occupied for the full duration of each query. This forces all parallel users to queue up.

How it works. Fat-Tree QRAM replaces each single router at level i in the binary tree with a bundle of (log(N) - i) routers. The root level gets the most copies and the leaves get one (same as BB). The total qubit count only doubles — from N to roughly 2N — but now up to log(N) queries can be in-flight simultaneously, each using a different copy of the routers at the upper levels. Each query is processed in ten-circuit-layer intervals: four layers of gate steps (controlled-SWAP operations that route address qubits deeper into the tree) followed by one layer of 'swap steps' (fast local swaps within a node that shift each in-flight query to the next larger or smaller sub-QRAM). Two alternating swap types (SWAP-I for even-indexed router pairs, SWAP-II for odd) cycle all concurrent queries through the hardware without any inter-node communication, keeping the constant-depth property intact.

The key design choice. The connectivity required for these intra-node swaps is only nearest-neighbor — adjacent qubits within a node — which is feasible in superconducting hardware. The paper shows the full connectivity graph decomposes into just two planar layers, enabling either a modular chip design linked by coaxial cables or a compact two-layer on-chip design using through-silicon vertical connections.

The result. A FIFO scheduling protocol (proven optimal by exchange argument) maximizes utilization. For parallel quantum algorithms like Grover's search, k-sum, Hamiltonian simulation, and quantum signal processing, Fat-Tree QRAM reduces overall circuit depth by up to a factor of 10 versus BB QRAM, while maintaining the same O(log²N·ε) infidelity scaling.

Reconfigurable Stream Network Architecture

What it does

FPGA-based AI accelerators today run one neural-network layer at a time because their control systems treat each layer as one indivisible command, leaving hardware idle between layers. RSN (Reconfigurable Stream Network) replaces that command style with a network of connected processing stations that stream data continuously, letting multiple layers run in an overlapping pipeline. The prototype chip cuts latency by 6x and triples throughput compared to the prior best FPGA design, while using only 18% of the memory bandwidth of a comparable GPU.

The method, in detail

The idea. A modern FPGA AI chip (AMD Versal VCK190) contains two very different kinds of hardware: a traditional reconfigurable logic fabric (like programmable circuit boards) and an array of 400 small AI-specific processors. Coordinating them is hard, and existing software treats each neural-network layer as one big atomic command — nothing else can happen until that command is completely done, draining all intermediate results to external memory before the next layer starts.

How it works. RSN reimagines the chip as a circuit-switched network — think of a telephone switchboard where you connect wires between stations. Each 'functional unit' (FU) is a station: memory loaders, matrix multipliers, routing switches, output buffers. A computation is programmed by triggering a path through this network. Data flows along streams from one FU to the next without any central memory involved. Multiple non-conflicting paths can be active simultaneously, so independent layers run in parallel (spatial parallelism), and dependent layers pipeline — layer 2 can start consuming the first outputs of layer 1 before layer 1 is even half done (pipeline parallelism).

The key design choice. Instructions control streams, not individual data values. One 1-byte RSN instruction can direct up to 1.6 billion floating-point operations. A three-level instruction decoder fuses all FU command streams into one compact sequence and supports a 'reuse' field that repeats a small instruction packet many times, exploiting the highly repetitive nature of neural-network loops. This slashes instruction overhead while still giving fine-grained control: the software explicitly schedules when memory loads and stores happen at the byte level, interleaving them with compute to avoid any idle gaps.

The result. On BERT-Large (a large language model encoder), RSN-XNN cuts latency 6.1x versus the prior FPGA state of the art, improves throughput 2.4–3.2x across four models, and achieves 2.1x better energy efficiency than an A100 GPU at the same manufacturing process.

Need for zkSpeed: Accelerating HyperPlonk for Zero-Knowledge Proofs

What it does

Generating cryptographic proofs that verify a computation was done correctly — without revealing the inputs — takes minutes to hours on a CPU using the HyperPlonk protocol, because the math is enormous (numbers up to 381 bits wide, polynomials with millions of terms). zkSpeed is a custom chip that offloads every major step of this proof-generation process to dedicated hardware engines, cutting proof time by about 800 times versus a CPU.

The method, in detail

The idea. HyperPlonk works in four sequential phases, each dominated by a different type of expensive math: SumCheck (proving a huge sum is correct), MSM (Multi-Scalar Multiplication — combining millions of points on an elliptic curve to 'lock in' a value cryptographically), tree-shaped computations for building polynomial lookup tables, and fractional polynomial construction. zkSpeed builds a separate silicon engine for each phase and connects them with a shared 4-channel data bus.

How it works. The SumCheck engine (the most memory-hungry part) streams polynomial lookup tables from high-bandwidth chip memory (HBM — a fast, stacked memory chip) rather than storing them all on chip, because the data balloons 100x between rounds. The three varieties of SumCheck (ZeroCheck, PermCheck, OpenCheck) share one unified circuit with 94 multipliers instead of 184, halving the silicon area. The MSM engine uses Pippenger's algorithm — a clever shortcut that replaces expensive per-number multiplications with cheaper additions grouped into 'buckets' — and a faster bucket-aggregation step that cuts that sub-step's time by 92%. The Multifunction Tree Unit handles tree-shaped builds and lookups using a hybrid depth-first/breadth-first traversal that avoids storing entire tree levels at once, saving memory. The FracMLE unit inverts millions of 255-bit numbers using a technique called Montgomery batching: it computes partial products, does one shared inversion, then recovers all individual inverses — processing 64 numbers per inversion to balance pipeline latency and chip area. Sparse polynomial tables stored on chip are compressed 10-11x by packing binary values tightly.

The key design choice. Every unit is tuned so the off-chip memory bandwidth (targeted at 2 TB/s, comparable to the latest HBM3 chips) is the bottleneck rather than arithmetic, so adding more computation units gives proportional speedup.

The result. A 366 mm² chip achieves a geometric-mean 801x speedup over a CPU baseline across five synthetic HyperPlonk workloads.

Finesse: An Agile Design Framework for Pairing-based Cryptography via Software/Hardware Co-Design

What it does

Pairing-based cryptography is a family of advanced math operations used for privacy-preserving proofs, digital signatures, and zero-knowledge systems. The hardware chips that accelerate these operations become outdated as security standards tighten, requiring expensive redesigns. Finesse is an end-to-end framework — covering compiler, simulator, and chip blueprint — that automates the journey from cryptographic algorithm to working hardware for any pairing curve, cutting the design cycle from months to minutes.

The method, in detail

The idea. A pairing is a special two-input mathematical function over elliptic curve points; computing one requires thousands of multiplications inside large extension fields (nested number systems of increasing size, like complex numbers generalized further). Existing accelerator chips are hand-tuned for one specific curve and must be redesigned when the security level changes. Finesse introduces a layered abstraction that separates 'what to compute' from 'how the hardware does it.'

How it works. Three abstraction layers sit between the algorithm and the chip.

1. Intermediate Representation (IR). High-level pairing operations (multiplications in big extension fields, curve-point additions) are written as typed IR instructions — a notation like a simple programming language that is independent of any specific curve parameters.

2. ISA (instruction set). A simple RISC-style instruction set operating at the level of base-field modular arithmetic — the primitive 'one big integer multiplied by another' step. The compiler lowers IR to ISA instructions, applying optimizations such as dead-code elimination, constant propagation, and a custom 'issue slot affinity' scheduling pass that prevents pipeline bubbles caused by mismatched long-multiply and short-add instruction timings.

3. Hardware pipeline model. A parameterized chip description — number of compute cores, pipeline depths, memory bank widths — that the compiler targets. A cycle-accurate simulator reproduces the hardware behavior without building silicon, enabling rapid design-space exploration.

The key design choice. Issue slot affinity optimization is the central compiler innovation: it partitions the instruction schedule into periodic windows and assigns 'long instruction' (multiply) or 'short instruction' (add) affinity to each slot, preventing the pattern where a long multiply followed by a short add causes a memory write-back conflict and stalls the pipeline.
