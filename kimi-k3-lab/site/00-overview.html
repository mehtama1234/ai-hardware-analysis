Kimi K3, from first principles · Start here · The big picture

It was never really about size.

Between GPT-2 and Kimi K3, the parameter count grew 22,580×. It's tempting to read the whole story as "they made it bigger." But if you strip away the jargon, every step in that seven-year path is an answer to a single, very human question:

When you're reading something long, how do you remember what you've read — cheaply enough to keep going, and well enough to still be useful?

That's it. That's the whole field on one line. Everything below — linear attention, the delta rule, gating, hybrids, mixtures of experts — is a different answer to how to remember. This page connects those dots before we open any code.

Theme 1 · The one problem

A model's real job is carrying the past forward

A language model reads words one at a time and, at every single step, has to predict what comes next. To do that well it needs the context — not just the last word, but everything relevant that came before. So the quiet, central task underneath everything is memory: hold on to what you've read so you can use it later.

And memory has exactly two costs. Space — how much you keep around. And effort — how much work it takes to read from it and write to it, every step. The entire history from GPT-2 to K3 is a running negotiation between those two costs. Push down one and the other tends to push back.

The whole lab in one sentence

Every architecture in this lineage is a different bargain between remembering everything (expensive, perfect) and remembering a fixed amount (cheap, but you must decide what to keep, edit, and throw away).

Theme 2 · What "remember" actually means here

Filing facts under cues, and looking them up by matching

Forget the equations for a second. Concretely, remembering works like a wall of sticky notes. Each note has a little label and a message. When a new question comes in, you don't read every note word-for-word — you find the note whose label best matches your question, and take its message.

In the papers the label is called a key and the message a value, and your question is a query. But the idea is just: file a fact under a cue; recall it by presenting a matching cue. Hold that picture — every rung changes only how the wall of notes is stored and maintained, never this basic act of filing-and-matching.

Theme 3 · The starting answer, and the wall it hits

Remember everything — until you can't

GPT-2's answer is the simplest one imaginable: keep every note, forever. Nothing is ever thrown away, so recall is perfect — any past word is available in full detail. This is the softmax attention you may have heard of, and it's genuinely great.

↑ the good

Perfect recall. Every past token is kept exactly; the model can always reach back and find precisely what it needs.

↓ the wall

The stack of notes grows with every word. And each new word makes you scan the whole growing stack. Long documents become unaffordable — in compute or in memory bandwidth.

That wall is not a small detail — it's the pressure that drives everything after. Session 01 measures it for real: the stored notes climb to hundreds of gigabytes, and the wasted re-reading grows with the square of the length. Perfect memory that doesn't scale is the problem the rest of the lab is trying to escape.

Theme 4 · The pivot that changes everything

Trade the growing pile for a fixed-size board

Here's the move that splits the whole field. Instead of a stack of notes that grows forever, keep one board of fixed size and fold every new note into it. The board never gets bigger — so no matter how long the text, the cost stays flat. This is "linear attention," and it's the escape hatch from GPT-2's wall.

But nothing is free. A fixed board can't hold unlimited detail, so it's lossy — you can't always pull back a single fact perfectly. And if you only ever add to it, the board eventually fills up and everything written on it starts to smear together. You've traded an unbounded-but-perfect memory for a cheap-but-crowded one.

Why this reframes the rest of the story

The moment memory becomes finite, a brand-new problem appears that GPT-2 never had to face: what do you keep, what do you overwrite, and what do you let go? That question — memory management — is where all the remaining cleverness lives.

Theme 5 · The heart of it — managing a memory that can't hold everything

Four increasingly smart ways to tend a finite board

Once you accept a fixed-size memory, progress is a sequence of better policies for maintaining it. Each of the next four rungs fixes one specific shortcoming of the one before — this is the spine of the whole lineage:

—

The same fixed board, four ways of tending it — the animation cycles through each policy.

① Edit in place — the delta rule (DeltaNet)

Don't just pile new facts on top. Before writing, erase the old version of that same fact, then write the new one. Targeted correction — the board stops smearing, because updating a fact replaces it instead of adding noise beside it.

② Do the editing fast (chunked DeltaNet)

Editing note-by-note is slow to train. A clever re-formulation lets the model edit a whole block at once while getting the same result — so the expensive hardware stays busy. Same behavior, now trainable at scale. (The one purely-practical rung.)

③ Forget generally (Gated DeltaNet, from Mamba)

Editing can replace a fact it recognizes — but it can't clear the board for a new topic. So add a dial that gently fades the whole board each step. Now old context can decay away on its own, freeing room.

④ Forget selectively, per feature (KDA / Kimi Linear)

One fade-dial fades everything equally — too blunt. Give each dimension of the board its own fading rate. Some kinds of information linger, others vanish fast. This fine-grained control is Kimi Linear's headline move — and it's enough to beat the remember-everything approach under fair comparison.

Notice the shape of the progress

Nobody is "just scaling." Each step adds capacity in a precise place to fix a named weakness: can't correct → edit in place; can't train fast → chunk it; can't forget broadly → add decay; can't forget selectively → per-feature decay. That's the opposite of blind size.

Theme 6 · Admitting the fixed board isn't always enough

Keep a little perfect memory around

However well you tend it, a fixed-size board still loses detail — and sometimes you genuinely need to reach back and recall an exact earlier moment. So the honest answer isn't "cheap memory or perfect memory." It's both: use the cheap managed board for most layers, and sprinkle in a few layers of the old perfect-recall mechanism for when exactness matters. Mostly flat-cost, occasionally exact. A hybrid.

Theme 7 · The destination, and the real lesson

Spend capacity where it has a job

Kimi K3 puts it all together — and its extra size is not spread evenly. Capacity is placed where it does specific work:

Many specialists, not one generalist

Instead of one huge network processing every word, keep hundreds of small expert networks and route each word to just a few. Enormous total capacity, but only a sliver runs per word. (A "mixture of experts.")

Memory across depth, not just time

Let deeper layers reach back to what earlier layers computed, choosing which to reuse — the same file-and-match idea, but along the model's depth instead of along the text. (This is "AttnRes.")

Plus a handful of small, targeted tweaks — better gates, a sharper activation, tiny low-rank adjustments — each buying a bit more expressiveness cheaply. None of it is "make everything bigger." All of it is capacity with a purpose.

Connect the dots · the whole ladder on one screen

Each rung fixes what the last one couldn't do

Read this top to bottom and the lineage stops being a list of names and becomes a single argument — every rung is the answer to the limitation printed under the rung above it:

00
GPT-2 — Keep every note forever

Remember everything you have read, perfectly. Look anything up by comparing your question to every past note and blending the best matches.

what it still can't do → The pile of notes grows with every word, and you re-read the whole pile each step. Perfect memory that does not scale.

01
Linear attention — Replace the pile with one fixed board

Stop stacking notes. Fold every new note into a single fixed-size board. Now the cost is flat no matter how long the text.

what it still can't do → One board is lossy — you can no longer pull back any single fact exactly, and once it is full, notes smear together.

02
DeltaNet — Edit the board in place

Before writing a new fact, first erase the old version of that same fact, then write the new one on top. Correction, not blind piling-on.

what it still can't do → It can replace one fact it has a match for — but it cannot clear the board for a fresh topic, or let old memories fade on their own.

03
Chunked DeltaNet — Do the editing fast

The editing is naturally one-note-at-a-time (slow to train). A re-formulation lets you edit a whole block of notes at once, so the hardware stays busy.

what it still can't do → Pure speed/training fix — same memory behavior, now trainable at scale. The what-to-forget question is still open.

04
Gated DeltaNet — Let the whole board fade

Add a dial that dims the entire board a little each step. Now you can forget generally — useful when the topic changes.

what it still can't do → One dial fades everything by the same amount. But some kinds of memory should linger while others should vanish fast.

05
KDA / Kimi Linear — Fade each feature at its own rate

Give every dimension of the board its own fading dial instead of one global one. Fine-grained control over what lingers and what disappears.

what it still can't do → A fixed board, however well managed, still loses detail. Sometimes you genuinely need exact recall of a specific earlier moment.

06
Kimi K3 — Keep a little perfect memory, and spend capacity with intent

Mostly use the cheap managed board — but interleave a few layers of the old perfect-recall mechanism for when exactness matters. Then add capacity only where it does a job: many small specialists instead of one generalist, and a way for deep layers to reach back to earlier layers directly.

what it still can't do → This is the destination. The claim: the 22,580x is real, but the wins came from placing capacity where it has a function — not from scale alone.

The one-line big picture

The road from GPT-2 to Kimi K3 is the search for a memory that is at once cheap — fixed in size, flat in cost — and smart — knowing what to keep, edit, and forget — with the humility to keep a little perfect memory on hand for when the summary won't do. Scale rode along. It was never the point.
