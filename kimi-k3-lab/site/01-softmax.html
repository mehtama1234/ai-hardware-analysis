Kimi K3, from first principles · Session 01 of 07

22,580 GPT-2s in one Kimi K3.

In seven years, AI language models grew about 22,580 times bigger — and this lab's whole argument is that the size was never the real story. To see why, we start where it all began: the simplest possible design, the one every later idea improves on. It remembers in the most obvious way imaginable — it keeps a note for every word it has read, and re-reads the entire stack to choose each next word. The memory is flawless. As we'll measure, that is exactly the problem.

Add up all of GPT-2's internal settings and you get 124.4M — the size it's famous for. Kimi K3 has 2.8 trillion. That is 22,516× more, matching the widely-quoted 22,580 to within a fraction of a percent. The number is real; the question is whether simply being bigger is all that changed.

In plain words · the problem, then the idea

The starting point: keep every note

We begin at the beginning — GPT-2's original design, before anyone had worked out how to stretch a model's memory cheaply. To see why everything later exists, you first have to see the baseline: a model that keeps a record of every single word it has read and throws nothing away.

The problem. Keeping one note for every word carries two bills that both keep climbing: the room to store the notes, and the effort to read all of them again for each new word. On a short text it's nothing — a couple of gigabytes for something that fits on a laptop. On a long one — a manual, a transcript, a book — the pile swells past 260 gigabytes. And every time the model adds a word, it re-reads the whole pile to decide what matters. The effort doesn't creep up; it explodes.

The idea. For every earlier word the model keeps one note — a small record of that word and its context. To choose the next word, it makes a single pass over all the notes, scoring each for how relevant it is right now, and lets the strongest ones weigh most. Nothing is discarded, so recall is flawless — as if a writer could lay every earlier sentence on the table, take them all in at once, and naturally focus on the few that matter.

The name, translated. Researchers call this full attention — the model 'attends to' (scores and weighs) every note in its memory before it acts. The name is literal: it pays full attention to everything, every time.

What it still can't do

The trouble is that the re-reading grows with the square of the length: double the text and the work roughly quadruples. So the longer the document, the faster the cost races toward impossible — a hard ceiling baked into the design. The model itself also grew enormously (22,580 times larger from GPT-2 to Kimi K3), but the real bottleneck was never its width. It was always this pile of notes, and how expensive it became to carry and re-read.

How it decides · the mechanism

Score every earlier word, then blend

To choose its next word, the model does one thing over and over: for the word it's on, it scores how relevant every earlier word is, turns those scores into weights that add up to 1, then blends the earlier words together in those proportions. A word may only look backward — never at words that come after it. Here are those weights, computed for real on a short sequence:

That "divide so the shares add to 1" step is the one piece the next rung keeps while making everything else cheaper.

Why this matters

This scoring ties every word to every other word in a way you can't work out ahead of time — which is exactly why the model is forced to keep every earlier word on hand. That is the problem the whole rest of the lab is escaping.

The bottleneck · what grows

The pile of notes grows — forever

To write each next word the model only has to work on the newest one — but to score it against the past, it needs every earlier word's details on hand. So it keeps them all in a stack of notes and adds one note per word. That stack never stops growing — a note for every word, kept separately in every layer of the model:

For a big model reading 128,000 words it is already tens of gigabytes — and every new word means hauling that whole pile out of memory again. That hauling is the wall; the next rung removes it by replacing the growing stack with one fixed-size summary.

The wasted-work ratio grows with the length — double the text and you roughly double the waste. That pressure is what makes a fixed-size memory so attractive.

A footnote · making the re-read cheap

You can re-read without hauling the whole grid

One fix is worth a footnote before we move on. Early on, doing this scoring meant building the entire word-by-word comparison grid in memory at once — and that grid, not the arithmetic, was the real cost as text grew. FlashAttention (2022) computes the exact same result without ever building the whole grid: it works through it in small tiles and keeps only a running result, so it moves far less data around. It doesn't change the answer, and it doesn't shrink the ever-growing pile of notes — it just makes the "re-read everything" step much cheaper to actually run. The pile is still the problem; that's what the next rung attacks.

The one-line aha

The baseline remembers perfectly — right up until the pile of notes becomes too big and too slow to afford, which happens far sooner than you'd think.
