In plain words · the problem, then the idea

The full picture: everything in its place

We have built, piece by piece, a path from the simplest memory trick — a fixed-size board that blends new facts in — to a memory smart enough to keep some things and drop others at its own pace. Now comes the hardest step: putting it all together. Kimi K3 is not bigger in every direction; it is bigger only where being bigger does a job.

The problem. A model that remembers cheaply and also remembers exactly when exactness matters is trying to satisfy two opposite demands at once. The cheap board lets it read millions of words. But sometimes — answering a question three hundred pages in, or quoting one specific line — a blended summary of everything isn't sharp enough; it needs to reach back and touch the exact moment. And even the cheap board has to spend its extra size carefully: pour capacity in evenly and most of it is wasted. The task of assembling is really the task of placing capacity only where it earns its keep.

The idea. Three upgrades sit on top of the cheap memory board. First: instead of one all-purpose network, Kimi K3 keeps hundreds of small specialist sub-networks and, for each word, wakes only a handful of them — most stay asleep, so the work per word stays small even though the total is enormous. Second: the internal squashing step that keeps numbers tame is swapped for a gentler version that knows its own ceiling — it rises where the old one keeps rising, then levels off, like a volume knob that refuses to go past a limit. Third: a deep layer can reach back and pull the work of one specific earlier layer straight forward, instead of getting an equal blend of all of them — so it can fish out a precise detail from far back.

The name, translated. The team of specialists is called a mixture of experts — the plain name says it all: a small router wakes just a few of them per word. The gentler, self-limiting squash is called SiTU (a bounded version of the usual squashing step). The reach-back to grab one exact earlier layer is called attention residuals. And the finished model is Kimi K3, from the lab Moonshot AI — the current frontier of this whole search for cheap, honest memory.

What it still can't do

This isn't the end of trade-offs; it's a smarter arrangement of them. Most of the specialists sleep, so the cost per word stays low — but you've built hundreds of them, so the model is heavy to store. The self-limiting squash never runs away, but it also never lets a value climb as high as the old one, which costs a little in rare cases. And reaching back to one exact layer is sharp, but the model has to learn which layer to grab, and that takes more training. You haven't made memory free; you've made it useful, and paid what it actually costs.

The backbone · everything so far, stacked up

Mostly cheap memory, with full memory mixed in

The model's spine is 23 repeating groups of four layers = 92 layers. In each group, three layers use the cheap fixed-page memory we built over the last rungs, and the fourth is a full keep-everything layer — the "keep a little perfect memory" hedge. Why this shape

The cheap layers carry most of the text at flat cost; the 1-in-4 full layers recover the sharp recall a fixed page can't (Sessions 02–03), which is where the 75%-smaller memory and up-to-6× faster generation of Session 06 come from. The rest of this page is the three things the model adds on top of this spine.

We ran it · why the crowd needs balancing

Left alone, the load goes lopsided

There's a catch to a crowd of specialists, and we can train a small one to show it. Real text is uneven — some kinds of word are far more common — so if the chooser just follows demand, a few popular specialists get swamped while the rest sit nearly idle. We trained a small chooser on deliberately lopsided data, once with no balancing and once with a small "spread the load" incentive. Each bar is one specialist's share of the work, tallest first:

no balancing — busiest 17.6% (4.2× fair)
with balancing — busiest 12.1% (2.9× fair)

On lopsided data with no balancing, the router just follows demand: the busiest single expert handles 17.6% of all traffic (4.2× its fair share) and only 24 of 24 experts carry real load — the rest are nearly idle capacity you still paid to build and run. Add a small 'spread the load' incentive and the busiest drops to 12.1% (2.9× fair share) with 24 of 24 active, trading a little routing precision (81%→74%) for even load. That trade is exactly why a real mixture-of-experts ships with a stability trick.

We ran it · a self-limiting squash

A squash that knows its own ceiling

Deep inside, every model has a little "squash" step that reshapes each number before passing it on. The usual one keeps climbing without limit; this model swaps in a version (called SiTU) that levels off. We computed both — they match near zero, then the old one keeps rising while the new one flattens out:

Far out (at 6): the old squash ≈ 5.99 and still climbing, versus the new one ≈ 1.0, leveled off. A squash that can't run away keeps the numbers steady in a model this size.

The new squash wraps the old one in a soft cap, so instead of climbing forever it levels off (far out, the old one is near 6 and rising while the new one has settled near 1). The report notes the plain version is about 3× slower to compute — offset because the specialists work in a compressed form that roughly halves their work.

We ran it · reaching back across depth

Reaching back to an exact earlier layer

Every trick so far reached across words. This one (called attention residuals) does the same look-up across layers: instead of a deep layer getting an equal blend of everything the earlier layers produced, it can score them and pull out the one earlier result it actually needs. We hid a useful result in one earlier layer among noisy ones and asked each approach to recover it:

reach back to the right layer0.83
equal blend of all layers0.48
recovery = how close the recovered result is to the hidden one, across 8 layers (1 = exact).

Normally a deep layer just gets an equal blend of everything the earlier layers produced, so one useful earlier result is watered down among all the rest. Reaching back scores the earlier layers and pulls out the one that matters — the same look-up trick, but across layers instead of across words — recovering it far better than the equal blend. The model does this every 12 layers, for a small speed gain at tiny extra cost, and to keep useful earlier results from getting lost in the blend.

Connect the dots · the whole ladder

Seven rungs, one idea

Read them top to bottom: each rung is a specific fix to how a fixed-size memory is kept, updated, or retrieved — and K3 is where they meet.

01Keep every noteperfect recall — but the pile of notes grows forever
02One running summaryfold everything into a fixed page — flat cost, but recall blurs
03Erase, then writereplace a fact in place instead of piling on top
04Work in blocksthe same thing, reorganized to train fast in parallel
05A forget dialfade the whole page to clear out stale material
06A dial per slotkeep some kinds of memory while dropping others, at once
