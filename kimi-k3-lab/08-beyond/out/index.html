Kimi K3, from first principles · Bonus · beyond the ladder

How the model learns to remember.

Every rung so far was about the model's shape — its architecture, the tricks for handling long text, the way its memory works. But a beautifully built machine means nothing until someone teaches it to use those pieces. This page is about the other half: how that shape was trained into a model that actually remembers well.

Training move 1 · build reading stamina

Grow the reading length gradually

Instead of throwing whole books at the model on day one, the researchers started it small — short passages, a few hundred words at a time — and let it practice reading and predicting those. Once it got good at short text, they gradually made the passages longer, stretching its reading stamina as training went on. It's like training a runner: you don't start a beginner on a marathon — you start with a mile, then two, then five. Very long passages are expensive to train on and tend to destabilize learning, so this gradual stretching lets the model absorb the skill efficiently — until, by the end, it can hold a context of up to a million words at once.

Training move 2 · nine specialists, one model

Train nine experts, then merge them into one

Here is the move that sets this training apart. Instead of building one single model and teaching it everything through trial-and-error practice (researchers call that reinforcement learning), the team trained nine separate specialist models — one good at writing, another at math, another at careful instruction-following, another at coding, and so on. Each was a narrow expert.

Then, rather than shipping nine models, they merged all nine into a single final model by teaching it to imitate what all nine specialists would do. Picture nine master teachers standing in front of one student, and the student learning to echo all of them — reaching for the right one when the moment calls for it. That merged student is Kimi K3. The technical name is multi-teacher distillation; "distillation" just means pouring the know-how of several teachers into one vessel.

Why bother merging?

Nine narrow experts are easier to train well than one model forced to be good at everything at once. Merging keeps their combined skill but ships a single, coherent model — you get the breadth without running nine models at inference.

Training move 3 · shape and hardware together

Design the model and the machines as one

A clever architecture only pays off if the computers running it can actually use its tricks. So the team didn't design the model in isolation and then hand it to engineers to make it run. The architecture choices and the machine design were shaped hand-in-hand — the memory tricks from the earlier rungs were co-designed with the specific hardware that would run them, so every trick genuinely paid off on real chips. It's like designing a race car and the racetrack at the same time, making sure each aerodynamic feature works with the exact turns and surfaces it will actually drive on.

The payoff

Where the real gains came from

The result is striking: Kimi K3 delivers about 2.5× more capability for the same amount of computing power than the previous model, Kimi K2. That jump did not come from just making the model bigger. It came from all of this together — the smarter architecture from the seven rungs, the training with nine specialists, the gradual length stretching, and the hardware designed to make it all sing. Each piece amplified the others.

A model's memory isn't only wired into its bones at birth — it's learned, shaped by how you teach it to read.
